## Introduction — The city at the factory gate

*Enterprise, institutions and the measure of participation.*

In Ivrea, along Via Jervis, changes to the factory gradually reshaped the city around it. New industrial buildings stood alongside nurseries, libraries, housing and health facilities. Behind the great glass façades lay a different idea of what a workplace could be, though the organisation of production remained exacting and hierarchical.

Adriano Olivetti helped build a company able to compete in international markets. From typewriters to electronic computers, the work drew on designers, engineers, shop-floor workers, artists, sociologists and managers. Those achievements depended on their skills and cooperation, far beyond any one entrepreneur’s will.

Olivetti believed that the consequences of industry obliged one to question institutions. A factory decides where to invest, which kinds of knowledge to train, how to organise production. Its workers, and the city that hosts it, live with the effects of those decisions; they do not necessarily take part in shaping them.

In 1945, with *L’ordine politico delle Comunità* (*The Political Order of Communities*), he proposed a reorganisation of the State built on territorial communities, defined public functions and differentiated ways of selecting those in charge. Three years later, in the Ivrea factory, a Management Council (*Consiglio di Gestione*) came into being, representing workers and management and holding specific powers over social services. The two experiments belonged to different levels. The first sought to redesign the architecture of political power; the second met, day by day, the limits of a participatory body inside a private company.

The Welfare Charter drawn up in 1949–1950, the trade-union disputes and the transformation of ownership that was imagined but never completed make that distance historically observable. The company’s social achievements were substantial. Their durability, however, posed a difficult question: how much would survive the authority of the man who had championed them, and how far could these institutions influence decisions still taken by a small group?

**Olivetti’s question concerns the institutions through which those who live with the consequences of development can understand its reasons, represent their own interests, dissent, and take a real share of the responsibility of governing.**

That distance between political design and the institutions that endured is one of the reasons Olivetti remains so difficult—and so necessary—to read.

## 1. Adriano Olivetti at a glance

**Adriano Olivetti (1901–1960)** was an Italian industrialist, publisher and political thinker. He was born in Ivrea on 11 April 1901 and died on 27 February 1960, on a train journey in Switzerland.

His father, Camillo, had founded in 1908 the Italian typewriter factory that Adriano would help turn into an international company. After a scientific education and hands-on experience of factory work, in 1925–1926 he visited a large number of plants in the United States and studied new techniques of industrial organisation.

He became general manager at the end of 1932 and president in 1938. Under his leadership the company widened its markets, products and facilities, and developed social services and collaborations with designers, architects, scholars and artists. He also launched research in electronics which, within his lifetime, culminated in the presentation of the Elea 9003 computer in 1959.

His activity crossed anti-Fascism, town planning, publishing and politics. In 1945 he published *L’ordine politico delle Comunità*, on the reform of the State’s institutions. In 1947 he founded the Movimento Comunità (the Community Movement); in 1956 he was elected mayor of Ivrea and in 1958 a member of parliament.

The centre of his thought is the **concrete community**: a territorial, social and economic reality that should have political instruments to steer its own development. For him this organisation had a human and spiritual purpose: to make possible a free and full personal life, beyond the immediate demands of production. The proposal joined local autonomy, the organisation of production, culture and participation, without shrinking into a mere programme of company welfare.

Several initiatives were carried out. The general transformation of industrial ownership and of the political order, by contrast, remained a project. His figure therefore belongs as much to the history of innovation as to that of institutions imagined and only partly built.

## 2. Why Adriano Olivetti matters

Olivetti led an industrial transformation that placed a firm born in Ivrea among the international leaders in office machines. He modernised production, widened markets, backed design and invested in electronic research. He did so through complex organisations in which the merit of technicians, designers and workers was an essential part of the results.

The industrial dimension also explains why he gave such weight to territory. A factory affects housing, transport, education and services; town planning and culture became, in his work, concrete fields of intervention. The buildings of Ivrea and Pozzuoli, the libraries and the welfare policies are material evidence of this approach.

Yet the decisive originality, for Cerchi d’inchiostro, lies in the further step Olivetti took: besides organising a company, he conceived a new political order. In *L’ordine politico delle Comunità* he asks about the conditions of representation, the autonomy of territories and the role of expertise. In the factory he supported a Management Council in which those questions took the form of limited rights and powers.

Industrial capacity, institutional design and social experiments can thus be observed side by side. The results are uneven: the constitutional transformation never happened; ownership was not reorganised according to the community project; the Council met limits and conflicts. This coexistence of achievements and unfinished work makes Olivetti a necessary author for a critical history of Italian industry and of the forms of economic responsibility.

## 3. The human problem — The right to take part and to dissent

Someone may work for a firm that pays above-average wages and offers health care, childcare, libraries and training. Those benefits have real material and cultural effects. They can change a family’s life profoundly.

The relationship with economic power raises a different question, though. Who decides which benefits should exist? How much of the budget goes to them? Who decides when a plant opens, expands or closes? A benefit can be guaranteed as a right, administered through an agreement, or granted at management’s discretion: in daily life the effects may look alike, but the institutional position of the people who receive it is not.

A company cannot distribute every responsibility in the same way. Designing a product, financing research or facing a crisis takes competence and the capacity to bear risk. The problem is not to abolish every difference of function. It concerns the procedures through which decisions affecting many people can be understood, challenged and, in some matters, shared.

For Olivetti the question went beyond the workplace. A territory could be transformed by investments, infrastructure and services that did not mainly stem from the decisions of its own representative institutions. One had to ask which political form would make a community responsible for its own development.

The Management Council is a useful test because it lets us observe distinct powers: information, consultation, and deliberation within appropriations set by the company. The political community described in *L’ordine politico delle Comunità*, by contrast, stands for the ambition to organise the legitimacy of government starting from persons, territories and functions.

**The human question is the possibility of dissenting without losing one’s membership in the community, and of contributing to decisions without depending only on the goodwill of those who make them.**

This is not a formula Olivetti ever pronounced. It is the criterion with which Cerchi d’inchiostro tests the distance between his political thought and the institutions that were actually built.

## 4. A life in essentials

### 1901–1924. Ivrea, family and education

Adriano Olivetti was born in Ivrea in 1901 to Camillo Olivetti, an engineer of Jewish origin and a socialist entrepreneur, and Luisa Revel, who came from a Waldensian family. Science, politics and differing religious traditions were part of the household in which he grew up. Camillo founded his typewriter factory in 1908, bringing Adriano into contact with industrial work long before he assumed responsibility for the business.

Adriano studied at the Politecnico di Torino, graduating in industrial chemistry in 1924. In the unsettled political life of post-war Turin he encountered liberal, socialist and anti-Fascist circles, including those associated with Piero Gobetti and Carlo Rosselli.

### 1924–1932. Apprenticeship and America

After graduation, he worked on the shop floor of his father’s company. Between July 1925 and January 1926 he travelled with Domenico Burzio through a large number of American factories, studying production methods, distribution and the relationships between technical staff and workers. The journey furnished practical material for the reorganisation that followed at Ivrea.

Back in Italy, he reshaped manufacturing processes, management and commercial operations. He established the Advertising Department in 1931; the Organisation Office followed in 1932. At the end of that year he became general manager.

### 1933–1943. Industry, planning and political contradictions

As the company expanded its product lines and plants, Adriano brought architects and artists into its orbit, among them Luigi Figini, Gino Pollini and Marcello Nizzoli. From 1936–1937 he coordinated studies for a plan for the Aosta Valley, extending his interest from the organisation of work to the organisation of territory.

His relationship with Fascism resists a straightforward account. **Olivetti joined the National Fascist Party (PNF).** Marco Maffioletti records the membership number from archival documentation; the precise date, **31 July 1933**, is reported by Olivetti himself in a letter of 11 March 1958. In that letter he explained his decision as necessary to protect the company and its workers. His later account of his motives must be distinguished from the independently documented membership and from any assessment of his beliefs. He also maintained contacts with corporatist circles and the regime’s intellectual world during the 1930s, notwithstanding his earlier anti-Fascist activity. In July 1943 he was arrested for opposing Fascism and for contacts with the Allies.

### 1944–1947. Exile and the community project

Olivetti found refuge in Switzerland in February 1944. There he pursued the study of federalism and developed the ideas about a future political order that he had begun to formulate in 1942. *L’ordine politico delle Comunità* appeared in 1945. The magazine *Comunità* followed in 1946, and the Movimento Comunità in 1947. Publishing became one means of bringing questions of government, work and culture into public argument.

### 1948–1955. The factory and experimental institutions

The Management Council took shape at the Ivrea plants in 1948. As the business continued to grow, social services, housing and cultural initiatives expanded alongside it. During the 1950s the company also developed new approaches to organisation, drawing increasingly on sociology and psychology. IRUR was established in 1954 to encourage economic activity in the Canavese, beyond Ivrea itself; the Pozzuoli factory opened in 1955.

### 1956–1960. Politics, electronics and expansion

In 1956 the company reduced the working week to 45 hours without cutting pay, and Olivetti was elected mayor of Ivrea. The Movimento Comunità contested the 1958 general election, winning him a seat in Parliament but failing to establish a substantial national presence.

On 29 September 1958, amid disagreements within the family and senior management, he requested six months’ leave. He retained the presidency while Giuseppe Pero became managing director, returning to full direction in spring 1959. Investment in electronics continued: the Elea 9003 was presented that year, and the first stage of the Underwood operation began. *Città dell’uomo*, carrying the editorial date 1960, was prepared and announced towards the end of 1959.

Olivetti died on 27 February 1960 while travelling by train in Switzerland, near Aigle. The subsequent history of the company, its collaborators and its institutions would unfold without him.

## 5. How the life illuminates the work

Olivetti’s experience in his father’s factory helps us understand one origin of his thought: the lives of those who work are organised through decisions that often do not belong to them.

His apprenticeship on the shop floor taught him the repetition of gestures, the discipline of time, the relation to machines and the hierarchy. The American journey adds a second element. Productivity does not depend only on individual skill: it requires a designed organisation, quality in processes and the capacity to coordinate people and knowledge.

Olivetti sought to bring human dignity into the organisation of efficient production. He tries to think of production as an environment that can be designed and changed, in which the quality of work also depends on how responsibilities are distributed.

His family background adds an idea of social responsibility that predates Adriano. Camillo had already attached importance to the conditions of employees and to the civic dimension of business. The son broadens that orientation and gives it a more articulated cultural and political expression.

Proximity to different religious traditions — the Jewish family of his father and the Waldensian family of his mother — is one element of his formation, without by itself determining the later philosophy. His mature thinking meets personalism, and a conception of human dignity that counts spiritual freedom among the purposes of institutions. The path from family context to political thought has to be reconstructed through readings and works, without presuming an automatic doctrinal continuity.

Exile in Switzerland gave these concerns a political urgency. The crisis of the Fascist State and the prospect of reconstruction demand that he imagine institutions able to limit new concentrations of authority. Olivetti develops a federalist and community project that, while sharing some concerns with other anti-Fascists, arrives at solutions of its own.

Finally, the 1950s show that business success does not remove the difficulties of representation. The existence of a Management Council, the tensions with the trade unions and the obstacles met by the Movimento Comunità bring to the foreground the gap between experiments tried out and institutional transformations completed.

These experiences explain the interweaving of biography, thought and action. They do not entitle us to deduce convictions from decisions, or achievements from declared intentions.

## 6. Industrial Italy and the problem of reconstruction

Olivetti’s story runs through the passage from liberal Italy to Fascism and then to the Republic. Italian industry undergoes an uneven modernisation, with large northern concentrations of production, rural areas and territories where access to industrial work remains limited.

In the 1930s the rationalisation of production coexists with political authoritarianism. Corporatism claims to organise relations between capital and labour in a system controlled by the Fascist State. The instruments of worker participation lack the conditions of union and democratic autonomy that free representation requires.

After the war, material reconstruction coincides with the founding of a new constitutional order. New spaces open up for trade-union organisation, political association and public intervention in the economy. The debate also concerns the relationship between ownership, workers and the powers of the State.

It is in this context that Olivetti formulates an alternative constitutional project. The territorial community should tighten the link between decision and social life. The aim is not a return to a closed local economy: his own company takes part in international markets and technological processes. But political organisation should let places govern responsibilities that the market and the central State, on their own, do not make fully recognisable.

In the years of the Italian economic miracle, production, consumption and the movement of people all grow. Olivetti follows and promotes industrial growth while trying to limit some of its consequences: uncontrolled concentration, the loss of ties to place, the separation of work from culture.

His project belongs to this historical conflict between modernisation and the collective capacity to steer it.

## 7. The disciplinary circles

### Political philosophy — The legitimation of power

The concrete community raises a question about representation: who governs, in whose name and by what procedures? The designed order interweaves suffrage, expertise and social functions, which exposes it to questions about their democratic compatibility.

### Economic history — Growth as organisational construction

The passage from a national factory to an international company shows the relation between product innovation, organisation, investment and markets.

### Sociology of work — Participation and conflict

The Management Council and the trade unions let us tell apart welfare, information, consultation, deliberation, autonomous representation and the effective governance of production.

### Law and the history of institutions — Federalism and community

*L’ordine politico delle Comunità* is a project of constitutional architecture in which territorial powers and political functions take a structural role.

### Town planning and architecture — The social form of space

Ivrea, Pozzuoli and the territorial initiatives make visible the influence of economic decisions on the city, on housing and on collective life.

### Philosophy of technology — Organising without reducing the person

Office machines and electronics raise problems of rationalisation, professional skill and the relation between knowledge and organisational power.

### Cultural studies — Publishing, the arts and social research

The Edizioni di Comunità (Community Editions), the magazine *Comunità*, design, and the presence of intellectuals in the company document a conception of culture as part of industrial and civic transformation.

### Business studies — Ownership, risk and continuity

International growth and the unfinished project of transforming ownership allow us to weigh the distance between social aims, financial constraints and corporate governance.

## 8. Key works and documents

### *L’ordine politico delle Comunità* (*The Political Order of Communities*) — 1945

This is the central text of Olivetti’s political thought, worked out from 1942 and completed in Swiss exile. The first edition appeared in **1945**, printed by the Engadin Press of Samedan for Nuove Edizioni Ivrea, with the subtitle *Le garanzie di libertà in uno stato socialista* (*The Guarantees of Freedom in a Socialist State*). In **1946** a second edition followed, with the subtitle *Dello Stato secondo le leggi dello spirito* (*On the State According to the Laws of the Spirit*). The distinction rests on Davide Cadeddu’s philological reconstruction and on the library cataloguing of the second edition. In 1970 the book was reissued in a different editorial format.

The sources consulted do not agree on the month of the first 1945 publication: this guide therefore gives the year without specifying a month that cannot be verified unequivocally. The original editions have not been collated directly.

The book is not exhausted by a defence of local autonomy. It proposes a constitutional organisation articulated in Communities, Regions and State, with public activities divided according to homogeneous functions. Those entrusted with the functions are selected through different procedures, which the project subordinates to forms of control and legitimation tied to universal suffrage. The system seeks territorial proximity and the quality of ruling classes at once.

Cadeddu identifies a federalist framework, a socialist component and a problem of selecting elites, all brought back to a personalist frame. These are categories of his historiographical interpretation. This guide does not claim to have read the original editions in full; it relies on the specialist reconstruction, the published introductory excerpt and the bibliographic records consulted.

### *Fini e fine della politica* (*Ends and the End of Politics*) — 1949

Published in April 1949 within the Movimento Comunità, it develops the critique of the centrality of parties and the proposal of a democracy built through other forms of social and territorial organisation. The Italian title plays on two words, *fini* (aims, purposes) and *fine* (end): it alludes to the purposes of political action and to the possibility that a given party organisation may lose its historical function.

The work must be read within the political confrontations of the early Republic. The formula of a democracy without parties carries concrete difficulties: how to organise dissent, alternatives of government and competition between programmes? The record of the edition edited by Cadeddu for Rubbettino documents the 1949 publication; the original text has not been read in full for this revision.

### *Società Stato Comunità* (*Society, State, Community*) — 1952

This collection gathers twelve essays tied to the political and cultural reflection of the post-war years. The problem of the State is widened to the relation between personal freedom, community, production and spiritual life. The work illuminates the passage from the constitutional design of 1945 to the public elaboration of the Movement.

The structure of the collection has been verified bibliographically; the twelve essays have not been read in full.

### *Città dell’uomo* (*City of Man*) — edition dated 1960

This collection of writings and speeches, with a preface by Geno Pampaloni, is recorded in bibliographic catalogues as a publication of the Edizioni di Comunità for 1960. Archival documentation places its release or preparation at the end of 1959: it is therefore advisable to keep the two indications apart instead of assuming that the date printed in the book coincides with that of its first distribution.

The whole addresses industry, institutions, culture and the purposes of living together. This revision has verified its bibliographic identification, not its full text.

### The speech to the workers of Pozzuoli — 23 April 1955

On the inauguration of the plant in Campania, Olivetti sets out the industrial reasons and social responsibilities of the investment in southern Italy. The reproduction of the speech, attributed to the collection *Città dell’uomo*, was consulted without collation against the printed edition. It is a primary source for reconstructing the entrepreneur’s public language, not independent proof of the economic effectiveness of his decisions.

### The records of the company’s institutions

Statute, electoral documents, agreements and minutes of the Management Council form a corpus distinct from the author’s works. They allow us to move from declared aims to actual competences, disputes and resolutions. The Archivio Storico Olivetti holds materials on participation; for now this guide uses institutional reconstructions and specialist studies, above all the research of Stefano Musso, without claiming to have read the original minutes.

This separation of sources is part of the method: a constitutional theory, an inaugural speech and an internal regulation tell different aspects of the same protagonist.

## 9. The concrete community

For Olivetti, community begins from a territory in which economic and social relations are recognisable. It does not coincide with a feeling of belonging, nor with the municipal dimension alone as it already exists. His design looks for a political unit in which productive activities, population, the organisation of services and representation can be brought into relation.

The first dimension is **territorial and administrative**. The Communities should have competences of their own and connect with Regions and State in a federal order. Closeness to inhabitants is useful insofar as it allows responsibilities to be recognised and the exercise of public functions to be controlled.

The second is **economic**. Work, ownership and the location of firms affect a population’s real possibilities. Political autonomy therefore requires a relation with the material bases of development. Olivetti also considers a transformation of the owning subjects that could make the responsibility of the firm more plural.

The third is **cultural and spiritual**. The person has dimensions that work does not absorb: education, convictions, relationships, the search for meaning. In the personalist perspective that nourishes Olivetti’s mature reflection, economic and political organisation must create the conditions in which the person can develop inner freedom, culture and relations with others. The reference to the spiritual belongs to the aims of the project, not only to the family’s religious history. Hence his interest in libraries, publishing, education, architecture and currents such as those of Emmanuel Mounier and Jacques Maritain.

The change of subtitle of *L’ordine politico* in 1946 — from the guarantees of freedom in a socialist State to the State according to the laws of the spirit — makes this dimension visible, without exhausting its meaning. It does not amount to an automatic renunciation of the social question: Olivetti tries to interweave material responsibility with the person’s spiritual freedom. This reading rests on the reconstructions of Cadeddu and Gemelli and on contemporary presentations of the project, not on a full reading of the original texts.

A last trait is **institutional**. The community needs procedures to train those in charge and to distribute their tasks. It is precisely this dimension that sets the project apart from a mere celebration of local solidarity.

These ambitions could pull against one another. A territory remained dependent on outside markets; cooperation did not erase conflicting interests; and choosing officials for their expertise raised questions of political legitimacy. Olivetti’s political work faces these difficulties with an organic project that remains open to criticism in its individual solutions.

## 10. Interpretive module I — Place and the right to govern it

*A Cerchi d’inchiostro reading*

The factories at Ivrea changed more than the working day. As production expanded, the city acquired new housing, transport, services and infrastructure. Company resources could bring these changes about at speed; residents gained from them and also lived with their costs. The question here concerns the powers of territorial institutions; workers’ powers within the company require a separate inquiry.

Olivetti recognises that this relation cannot depend solely on the character and convictions of those who run the factory. The order of the Communities should give recognisable responsibilities to territorial subjects able to plan their own future. In the Canavese, IRUR will show on a different operational scale the attempt to create economic initiatives outside the great factory: a real intervention that does not, however, coincide with the establishment of the imagined political order. Proximity mattered politically only insofar as it could be translated into defined powers and responsibilities.

**Cerchi d’inchiostro defines the right to govern a place as the possibility of exercising, through legitimate institutions, real powers over the decisions that durably transform a territory.** It is an interpretive category for the project, not a legal formula Olivetti historically used.

The problem has a dimension of scale. A municipality cannot by itself decide the international policy of an industrial group. It can, however, contribute to the planning of settlements, represent collective interests, ask for an account of the use of land and take part in decisions in the forms the law makes possible. Olivetti’s project tries to realign social geography and the distribution of public competences, without being able to abolish the dependencies between different levels.

The institutional question, then, is not exhausted by the quantity of investment received. One must check who holds the title to decide, with what resources, and before whom they must answer. This is what separates Olivetti’s political problem from a company’s mere rootedness in a place.

## 11. The political order — A constitution of communities

The 1945 project starts from a problem of representation. Olivetti sees the distance between the action of governments and the life of industrial societies that demand ever more articulated expertise. He asks how to give political responsibility back to recognisable territories and, at the same time, how to keep public administration from losing technical capacity.

He proposes the Community as a local political body, built around actual economic and social relations. In Davide Cadeddu’s reading, one of the intentions is to make administrative and representative districts coincide as far as possible, so as to reduce the distance between voters and governors. The Communities were not to be mere executors of central directives: they could carry out public policies of their own.

Functions are a second cornerstone. The author imagines a homogeneous division of the activities of government; each function has a person responsible and a path of training. The **political orders** (*ordini politici*) link persons entrusted with specific responsibilities across the different territorial levels. They are neither simple professional categories nor ministries that already exist: they belong to an alternative constitutional design.

The system admits different criteria of legitimation: election, competitive examination, selection tied to work, and co-optation, depending on the function. In Cadeddu’s interpretation these procedures are not to be isolated from the general principle of universal suffrage, to which they remain subordinate in constitutional forms provided by the author. The framework appealed to universal suffrage without eliminating the selective and elitist features of examination and co-optation. These features distinguish Olivetti’s design from contemporary forms of democratic federalism.

The political intention is to qualify government and to reduce the centrality of parties as exclusive intermediaries. The difficulty concerns guarantees. Could a citizen check who gains a function by co-optation? How would opposition and alternation be organised? What remedies would allow someone to challenge a competence that is claimed but badly exercised? The periodic election of some offices does not by itself resolve the relation between indirect powers, information and accountability.

Olivetti hands down an ambitious and historically situated proposal. Its fertility lies in having treated the distribution of responsibilities as part of the structure of the State. Its limits are measured by its ability to reconcile the selection of governors with equal political rights, pluralism, and the possibility of replacing those who hold power.

## 12. Interpretive module II — Expertise, representation and freedom

*A Cerchi d’inchiostro reading*

The working of an industrial society depends on knowledge that will cannot replace. Designing a plant, understanding a balance sheet or assessing the effects of a technological change all require training and study. Olivetti brings this evidence into the design of political institutions.

Public decisions demand technical knowledge of what is possible. They also require choices among competing ends and interests. Expertise can establish constraints; legitimacy and representation determine who may decide, and to whom an account must be given.

The project of the political orders tries to link the two demands. A tension opens in its architecture, however: who decides which knowledge should give access to responsibility? Who controls the selectors? In which cases may a citizen contest a decision whose necessity the experts claim? The reference to universal suffrage, present in the framework according to Cadeddu, is an important condition, but it must be translated into procedures for controlling powers that are also exercised through examination and co-optation.

**Cerchi d’inchiostro distinguishes the technical quality of a proposal from the legitimacy of the power that makes it binding.** The first must be open to discussion on the basis of evidence; the second requires titles, limits and responsibilities that are publicly recognisable.

Olivetti’s historical merit is to have made this friction an institutional problem and not only a moral one. His proposal offers no solution immune to objection. But it prevents us from considering sufficient, for governing a community, either the competence of the decider alone or consent expressed without adequate knowledge of the decisions.

## 13. Ownership of the firm and the imagined Foundation

In Olivetti’s story, ownership is a point of particular importance. Company services can be developed through a management decision, while the ownership structure determines who ultimately holds control, capital and a decisive share of strategic choices.

Olivetti’s project considers a different organisation of the firm’s ownership. Emilio Renzi reconstructs the idea of a **Camillo Olivetti Foundation** in which shareholding would have been divided among four components: the historic shareholders, the workers and technicians, the local community authority and the institutions of knowledge, represented in the project by the Politecnico di Torino.

This hypothesis has a reach beyond corporate philanthropy. Had it been realised, it would have brought different subjects into the structure of economic responsibility, opening the question of their respective rights of ownership and of governance.

The fundamental distinction is historical: **the overall transformation of ownership was not carried out according to the planned arrangement.** The existence of the idea documents an orientation; it is not proof of a company already governed jointly by the city, the employees and the shareholders.

It also remains to ask what actual distribution of powers would have followed from the imagined shares. Holding a stake does not automatically mean holding the same rights of appointment, direction and control. A full judgement on the model would require a statute, voting rules and verifiable mechanisms of accountability.

The failure to realise the project does not cancel its theoretical importance. It illuminates the distance between the social responsibility exercised by a private owner and the transformation of ownership institutions.

## 14. The factory — Organisation, productivity and labour

Adriano Olivetti understands that a modern company rests on organisation. Industrial skills do not produce results without a structure able to combine them, train staff, control quality and bring products to market.

The journey to the United States brings in methods and observations that shape the transformations of the Ivrea factory. Olivetti develops functional departments, reorganises the work processes, widens the sales network and invests in design and advertising.

His relation to Taylorism is nuanced. On one hand he uses tools for the rationalisation of time and the organisation of processes; on the other he tries to improve the conditions in which workers face those rhythms, recognising the importance of training and of the concrete quality of the workplace.

Organisation does not abolish hierarchies, and does not always harmonise the interests of ownership and workers. It can even increase management’s capacity to observe and discipline work. Gains in productivity must therefore be set against the quality of employment conditions and the possibilities of representation.

Industrial growth brings new professions. Designers, production technicians, managers, salesmen, stylists and researchers collaborate in developing different products. The factory becomes a system in which knowledge crosses functions. According to Emilio Renzi’s historical reconstruction, in 1960 the Olivetti group counted about 35,000 employees, half of them in Italy, and eleven production plants, six of them abroad. These are magnitudes of the overall industrial organisation: alone, they measure neither the quality of participation nor the sustainability of the investments.

This organisational capacity makes important results possible. It also supplies the ground on which to test the central political problem: does technical cooperation among many people translate into a corresponding distribution of the capacity to decide?

## 15. The Management Council — Real powers

The Olivetti Management Council (*Consiglio di Gestione*) was elected on **22 March 1948**. Its origin, however, does not coincide with a sudden initiative of Adriano. After the fall of Fascism in 1943, committees of workers and clerks had arisen; the resistance inside the company and the post-war representative bodies had put forward proposals for participation. From 1945 a negotiation with management developed, marked also by conflicts over the powers to give the body. Adriano’s return to the helm helped reopen the discussion in 1947, until agreement on the Statute at the start of 1948.

The Council thus came out of the meeting, and in part the clash, between workers’ aspirations and company policy. This origin prevents us from describing it merely as a gift of the founder.

### Composition and representation

According to the reconstruction published by the Associazione Archivio Storico Olivetti, the original Statute provided for fourteen councillors, in addition to the company’s president or his delegate, who chaired with a vote. Eight councillors belonged to the elected side and six were appointed by management. Among the elected councillors, six were chosen through a second-degree procedure: employees elected a Consulta (an assembly), which in turn designated three workers and three clerks; the elected side was completed by one representative of the managers and one elected directly by all employees.

The design introduced a stable representative channel, but did not reproduce the structure of the workforce proportionally. Workers, the numerically largest category, were under-represented. The arrangement was contested and changed in 1963, after Adriano’s death, with proportional criteria more favourable to worker representation.

### The content of the powers

Article 28 of the Statute gave the Council **consultative** competences over production programmes, plant and working conditions. Article 29 gave it **deliberative** competences in dividing the sums set aside for welfare services.

This is a decisive legal difference. The Council could express views on industrial matters but did not normally determine company strategy. In welfare it could decide how to use appropriations previously set by the company; it did not autonomously determine the total of the budget devoted to services.

### Results, discussions and conflicts

Between 1949 and 1950 the Council helped draw up a **Welfare Charter** (*Carta assistenziale*) that framed social services as rights of employees, moving beyond the idea of a mere personal concession of the owner. The body also discussed issues of work organisation and company information. Its *Giornale di fabbrica* (the factory journal), launched in 1949, ceased in 1951 after disagreements over the make-up of the editorial board: a concrete episode of the difficulty of representing different interests within a single institution.

The archival reconstruction also links to the debate in the Council the 1955 agreement on shortening working hours, implemented from 1956 from 48 to 45 hours a week at the same pay. A decision that also involved management and the trade unions should not be automatically attributed to the Council alone.

After Adriano’s death, insistent demands emerged to widen the prerogatives over the services budget. Management rejected them. The Council nevertheless continued until **5 April 1971**, when the end of the experience was agreed and it was replaced by bilateral committees dedicated to services.

### The historical measure of the experiment

By duration and continuity, the Council was one of the most significant participatory experiences of post-war Italian industry. It was not a body of full co-determination comparable to models in which workers directly take part in strategic control of the company. Its history shows the simultaneous presence of institutional innovation, limited powers, conflict and democratic learning.

Stefano Musso’s monograph is the principal specialist reference. For this revision the academic record of the volume was consulted, together with a reconstruction by the Associazione Archivio Storico Olivetti that draws on his findings; the original minutes and the full volume remain to be examined in any eventual documentary audit.

## 16. Interpretive module III — Participation and its boundaries

*A Cerchi d’inchiostro reading*

The existence of representatives tells us little about the power they can exercise. The Management Council lets us identify the conditions that separate its presence from an effective capacity to intervene.

The first concerns **access to information**. To consult a body on an investment already decided, or without supplying adequate data, limits the meaning of consultation. The documented complaints about the not always timely information given to the Olivetti Council show that procedure alone does not guarantee influence.

The second concerns **the matter on which one decides**. Dividing a social budget produces real consequences; defining the amount of that budget would confer a further power. The line between the two is not lexical but economic and institutional.

The third concerns **autonomous representation and dissent**. Elected members must be able to defend interests that diverge from management’s. The controversies over the internal journal, over the representation of workers and over union relations are historical tests of this difficulty.

The fourth concerns **the possibility of changing the rules**. A form of participation can adapt to conflicts and to transformations of work, or it can harden into competences its constituents judge insufficient. The demands made after 1960 to widen the Council’s powers are an indicator of the difference between the existing institution and the institution thought adequate.

**Cerchi d’inchiostro calls this the boundary of participation: the point at which representatives may be heard but lack the means to change or scrutinise a decision.** It is an editorial category of Cerchi d’inchiostro, not an expression attributed to Olivetti.

The Ivrea experience shows that the boundary can be partly crossed and can change over time. Checking it requires knowing both the written rules and the actual functioning of the bodies, without mistaking dialogue for co-determination and without denying value to the results obtained within limited powers.

## 17. Trade unions, autonomy and conflict

Industrial relations at Olivetti involved the national trade unions, the internal workers’ committees and bodies inspired by the Movimento Comunità. Political differences did not vanish because of the management’s social services and cultural initiatives.

In 1955 **Comunità di Fabbrica** (Factory Community) was formed, later renamed **Autonomia Aziendale** (Company Autonomy), culturally and organisationally tied to the community project. Its rise changed the balance among the different representative bodies. CGIL and CISL, two of Italy’s major union confederations, challenged aspects of its independence from the company leadership; Autonomia Aziendale itself developed demands of its own on working conditions, hours and profit-sharing.

The controversy cannot be reduced to a contrast between a genuine union and one with no autonomy at all. One has to examine elections, agreements, relations with management and the real possibilities of conflict. Musso’s research documents that the divergences also entered the Management Council, altering its internal balance and contributing to the change in its functions.

The *Giornale di fabbrica* ceased publication in 1951 after an editorial dispute, four years before Comunità di Fabbrica was founded. The difficulties of speaking for differing interests had surfaced well before the new union entered the scene. The agreement that preceded the 45-hour week likewise emerged from negotiations among several actors, not from the founder’s will alone.

Olivetti’s project values the unity of a productive community. Its history shows, however, that **the quality of representation is measured also when interests do not coincide.** An institution that allows disciplined conflict can strengthen coexistence; an institution that hides it risks making inequalities of power invisible.

## 18. Welfare, culture and quality of life

The Olivetti company developed services that included health care, support for motherhood and childhood, canteens, libraries, cultural activities, transport and instruments to ease access to housing. The initiatives had different phases of development, and not every benefit was present in the same form for every worker or at every site.

In 1956 the 45-hour working week at the same pay was introduced, ahead of the national collective agreements. It is a historically recognisable result, which concretely changes the distribution of time between work and personal life.

Company libraries and the circulation of books take on a particular significance. Culture enters the industrial environment through opportunities available to employees, while the Edizioni di Comunità and the magazines promote a wider debate on society, politics and knowledge.

The Olivetti conception treats access to culture as part of the growth of the person. For production-line workers and technicians alike, reading, study and cultural activities offered opportunities beyond the skills demanded by the job.

The welfare policies produce verifiable benefits. They call, however, for a further assessment. A service may depend on the company’s financial stability; it may be exercised as an agreed right or as a benefit administered by management. Legal and organisational forms therefore influence its continuity.

Olivetti sought, through bodies and agreements, to give more structure to part of these interventions. The analysis of welfare must nonetheless stay distinct from that of control over the firm. The quality of working life and the possibility of steering company strategy are not equivalent measures.

## 19. Ivrea, the industrial city

Ivrea is the place where the Olivetti experience becomes built landscape. The original factory grew into a complex of extensions, offices, homes and public amenities shaped by leading figures in twentieth-century Italian architecture.

The glazed surfaces of the plants, the spaces for services and the residential districts express a search for the relation between production and the quality of the environment. Architecture is not treated as an aesthetic wrapping for the factory. It organises light, access, functions and the relations between places of work and places of life.

The programme unfolds over decades and involves different designers. Luigi Figini and Gino Pollini contribute to industrial and residential buildings; other experiences involve architects such as Ignazio Gardella, Marco Zanuso and Luigi Cosenza in different fields and places. This is not the work of a single designer, nor of a single plan carried out in full without change.

In 2018 **Ivrea, industrial city of the 20th century** was inscribed on UNESCO’s World Heritage List. The recognition concerns the exceptional value of the architectural and urban whole, testimony to the ways a modern company tackled the relation between productive activity, social needs and the design of space.

The UNESCO decision is not a certification that the community political project fully succeeded. It allows the recognition and protection of a material heritage in which some of its ideas took historical form.

The city thus also displays a limit. Buildings can outlive the economic and social systems that produced them. Their conservation can become an autonomous cultural responsibility, while employment, ownership and institutions continue to change.

## 20. Pozzuoli, La Martella and the Canavese

### Pozzuoli — The factory and the Southern Question

The Olivetti plant at Pozzuoli, near Naples, inaugurated on 23 April 1955 and designed by Luigi Cosenza, belongs to the strategy of industrial expansion into the Mezzogiorno, Italy’s south. It stands in an environment marked by problems of employment and by a geography different from that of Ivrea.

In the inaugural speech Olivetti presents the investment as a response to industrial needs and as a choice of responsibility towards the territory. His testimony lets us observe the coexistence of commercial interests, organisational decisions and social orientations.

The factory itself is an established achievement. Assessing its long-term effect on jobs and the local economy, however, requires evidence beyond the founder’s inaugural speech.

### La Martella — Designing an inhabited community

The village of La Martella, near Matera, arises within the policies for clearing the Sassi (the ancient cave districts of Matera) and the public initiatives to transform housing and rural conditions. The design involves planners, scholars and institutions; Olivetti makes an important intellectual and organisational contribution through the cultural and research networks in which he takes part.

It would not be correct to attribute the intervention to him as an individual work. The project does, however, help us understand his interest in a planning capable of linking housing, services, work and social structure.

### The Canavese and IRUR — Creating possibilities outside the great plant

In 1954 IRUR was founded, the Istituto per il Rinnovamento Urbano e Rurale del Canavese (Institute for Urban and Rural Renewal of the Canavese), with the aim of promoting productive and agricultural initiatives, countering unemployment and reducing the migratory pressure towards Ivrea.

The institution addresses a contradiction of industrialisation: the success of one large factory can attract labour and concentrate activity, weakening the economic possibilities of surrounding territories.

IRUR tries to build more distributed alternatives. Its existence and its programme are documented; to judge its effectiveness as a whole one would need consistent series on the businesses supported, the investments, the jobs and the activities that actually survived.

The three experiences reveal a common trait of Olivetti’s thinking: industrial production affects the life of places and must be studied also through the social and urban conditions it creates.

## 21. Interpretive module IV — After the founder: the test of institutions

*A Cerchi d’inchiostro reading*

Adriano Olivetti designed institutions while running a company that depended to a considerable degree on his own capacity for direction. This condition makes his project particularly hard to assess. An entrepreneur can build lasting services and set up participatory bodies; so long as the essential resources and the continuity of choices remain traceable to one person, institutional stability has yet to be demonstrated.

To weigh the legacy one must distinguish three levels.

The first is **the work achieved**: a building, a factory, a laboratory or a school can go on existing after those who promoted it. Material endurance does not, by itself, prove the continuity of the original aims.

The second is **the organisation that survives**: the Management Council continued after 1960, changed its rules and ended its activity by agreement in 1971. Its persistence shows a relative autonomy from the founder, though within limited prerogatives. The Edizioni di Comunità and the architectural heritage have had other histories and other forms of continuity.

The third is **power that becomes institutionalised**: the transformation of ownership imagined through the Camillo Olivetti Foundation was not brought to completion; the federal community order did not become the structure of the State. Here the distance between intention and institution remained widest.

For Cerchi d’inchiostro, **the test of institutional endurance** asks what continues to function, under which rules and with how much independence, when the founder who brought together the resources, skills and support is no longer there.

The category is not meant to judge a man retroactively through events he could not govern. It lets us distinguish the heritage he leaves from a shared and sufficiently regulated power that can outlive its personal exercise.

In this perspective the Olivetti experience makes visible a difficulty of industrial reformism: the will to distribute power can arise within a very concentrated leadership. The quality of the intention and the transformation of the rules have to be measured separately.

## 22. Technology, design and knowledge

Olivetti’s international identity was born also from the quality of its objects. Typewriters and calculating machines are industrial products made to meet concrete needs, in which technical design, reliability and formal design must find a balance.

The MP1, the Lexikon 80, the Lettera 22 and the Divisumma 24 belong to different moments of this evolution. The achievements must be attributed also to the designers and technicians who shaped their components and forms. Marcello Nizzoli, Natale Capellaro and Giovanni Pintori are among the decisive figures, respectively in industrial design, mechanical innovation and visual communication.

The success of these products does not depend only on their beauty. It requires efficient industrial processes, distribution networks, after-sales service and the ability to understand customers’ daily work. Design mediates between a complex technology and the user’s gesture: handiness, the layout of controls, legibility and the form of objects become design problems with consequences for the concrete experience of work. This value in use does not cancel the demands of the market, but it shows why Olivetti’s innovation involves mechanics, ergonomics and communication together.

Olivetti promotes an environment in which technical and cultural competences work together. Architects, artists, writers, sociologists and engineers are drawn into different activities. This openness gives the company new possibilities of knowledge, but it depends on the quality of organisational relations and on the real freedom of the professionals.

In 1954 a Service for sociological research and organisational studies was born in Ivrea. The presence of scholars such as Luciano Gallino and Franco Momigliano helps observe production processes and social relations with tools different from those of mechanical engineering alone.

Knowledge thus becomes part of industrial capacity. A political question remains: who defines the problems to be studied, and who decides how the results of research will be used? The collaboration of disciplines raises the quality of the project but does not automatically determine the distribution of the powers that govern it.

## 23. Electronics and industrial risk

In the second half of the twentieth century, office work enters a transformation that leads from mechanical automation to the electronic processing of data. Olivetti does not watch the change from outside: he promotes investments and skills that place the company among the European protagonists of research.

In 1952 a laboratory for research on electronic computers opens at New Canaan, in the United States. In 1955 the Electronic Research Laboratory of Pisa is born; in 1957 Olivetti takes part in founding the Società Generale Semiconduttori with Telettra. Research and production involve Mario Tchou, Roberto Olivetti and many specialists. In 1959 the **Elea 9003** is presented, the fruit of collaboration among electronics, industrial engineering and formal design.

In the same year, 1959, the great American operation opens. In October 1959 the company signs the agreement to acquire initially **about a third of the shares of Underwood**. The holding rose to **about 70% in 1960**, after Adriano’s death. The chronology of the Archivio Storico Olivetti reports 30% and 70% respectively; contemporary documents and reconstructions give slightly different initial values. To write that he personally concluded the acquisition of definitive control of the group as early as 1959 would erase a decisive chronological step.

The American sales network can support the spread of Olivetti products; Underwood’s plants and industrial organisation, by contrast, present problems of modernisation. International growth offers possibilities and at once increases financial needs, the complexity of integration and risk.

Historiography reports the size of the company at Adriano’s death in different ways, partly because of the companies and units considered. Emilio Renzi gives about 35,000 employees in 1960; other reconstructions give higher values. This guide does not turn those numbers into a homogeneous series and does not attribute later financial problems to the electronic investments alone.

The crisis of 1963–1964 and the transfer of control of the Electronics Division to General Electric belong to the later history. The **Programma 101**, presented in 1965 and tied to the group led by Pier Giorgio Perotto, is also a result of his successors and of the skills accumulated, not a product completed under Adriano’s personal leadership.

Electronics makes both the strength of the industrial project and its risk recognisable. The quality of research does not replace checks on balance sheets, ownership decisions and financial conditions; innovation is a collective capacity that requires economic institutions able to sustain it over time.

## 24. The Movimento Comunità and the political test

Founded in 1947, the Movimento Comunità tries to give organised form to the project of social and political reform. The magazine *Comunità* and the Edizioni di Comunità support a cultural programme that involves architecture, philosophy, the social sciences and the problems of democracy.

The political proposal seeks to limit the distance between citizens and government through territorial roots, the competent selection of those in charge and the reorganisation of institutions. It criticises the centrality of parties in the formation and working of public power.

In the Canavese the Movement develops cultural and associative initiatives and achieves local electoral results. In 1956 Olivetti is elected mayor of Ivrea.

The national attempt meets a precise limit. At the 1958 elections Olivetti is the only candidate elected on the Movement’s lists; the experience does not become a parliamentary force able to promote a general reform of the State. The seat later passes to Franco Ferrarotti.

The political confrontation shows the difference between the coherence of an idea and its ability to win wide representation. An organisation built around a cultural project and the figure of its founder can meet difficulties when it must face a plurality of interests, territorial machines and electoral competition.

In September 1958 dissent emerges in the leadership and in the family: Adriano keeps the presidency but temporarily entrusts the role of managing director to Giuseppe Pero; in spring 1959 he returns to full direction. The episode shows that even the founder’s leadership met institutional and family resistance.

The Movement’s experience must not be judged only by the number of its elected members: the publishing and cultural initiatives have effects that exceed the electoral result. But a constitutional order that remained a proposal cannot be considered realised.

## 25. Key ideas

**Community requires an organisation of powers.** In Olivetti’s thought, social and territorial proximity must find its counterpart in political responsibilities and effective functions.

**Material well-being does not exhaust participation.** Wages, services and working conditions can improve profoundly without changing the distribution of decision-making power to the same degree.

**The factory changes the city.** Investment, employment, housing and services shape the organisation of collective life and call for a town-planning and political gaze.

**Expertise is necessary and needs legitimation.** An institution must use specialist knowledge without turning it into an authority withdrawn from the citizens’ control.

**Ownership defines part of economic power.** The social responsibility of an entrepreneur and the distribution of ownership answer different problems.

**Participation has degrees and boundaries.** Information, consultation, deliberation on services and governance of strategy do not confer the same powers.

**The community also pursues the spiritual freedom of the person.** Education, the search for meaning and the recognition of human dignity are ends that economic and political organisation should make possible; personalism is an essential reference of Olivetti’s horizon.

**Culture belongs to industrial life.** Training, reading, social research, art and design help build skills and human possibilities beyond the immediate demands of production.

**Innovation requires organisation.** Technical excellence comes from collaboration among people and disciplines, and entails investments and risks that must be assessed.

**A territory must be able to get through transformations.** The presence of a large employer changes local opportunities, but can increase the economic dependence of the population.

**The political project and the company experiment must be measured separately.** The first defines the order desired; the second shows the competences, limits and conflicts of the institutions actually realised.

**Endurance is an institutional test.** Buildings and products can outlive the founder without his proposals on the distribution of power having been fulfilled; every legacy requires criteria of verification of its own.

## 26. Key concepts

### Concrete community

Territorial and social unit conceived by Olivetti as the basis of a political organisation able to integrate economic, cultural and institutional life. Its definition does not coincide with a mere community of feeling or interest.

### Community federalism

Proposal to articulate public powers starting from local communities, with links and responsibilities at the regional and State levels. In Olivetti’s project it also concerns the formation of representation.

### Political orders (*ordini politici*)

Structures envisaged in Olivetti’s order for exercising and organising particular political functions according to different paths of training and criteria of legitimation. The notion belongs to the author’s original theoretical lexicon.

### Functional representation

An analytical category for describing the presence, alongside general representation, of forms of selection and participation tied to competences and social functions. The term must be used while distinguishing the scholars’ reading from Olivetti’s own formulation.

### Community ownership

An expression usable for the project of transforming the title and responsibility of the firm through a plurality of social and institutional subjects. In Olivetti’s case this project did not become the complete structure of the company.

### Management Council (*Consiglio di Gestione*)

Body constituted at Olivetti in Ivrea in 1948, active until 1971, with representation of workers and management and competences chiefly linked to the company’s social services.

### Company welfare

The set of services, benefits and supplementary conditions offered or organised by a firm. Its presence must be distinguished from the rules of representation and from powers over economic decisions.

### Territorial planning

The organisation of settlements, infrastructure and services. In Olivetti’s experience it is tied to the social responsibility of industry and to the idea of community.

### The right to govern a place

A category of Cerchi d’inchiostro: the possibility of influencing, through representative institutions, the decisions that durably change the living conditions of a territory.

### The boundary of participation

A category of Cerchi d’inchiostro: the actual limit of the decisions over which a person or a representative organisation can exercise influence or power.

### The test of institutional endurance

A category of Cerchi d’inchiostro: the check of which bodies keep functioning, under what rules and with what autonomy, after the promoter has left direction or is gone.

## 27. The constellation

### What prepares Olivetti

**Camillo Olivetti** represents the family origin of an industrial culture and of attention to the conditions of workers. His reformist socialism and scientific training are elements of the context in which Adriano grows up, without automatically determining the son’s choices.

**Gaetano Salvemini**, **Piero Gobetti** and **Carlo Rosselli** belong to the circles and political traditions that help form him. The encounter with reformist socialism and anti-Fascism precedes the formulation of the community project.

**The personalism of Emmanuel Mounier and Jacques Maritain** offers philosophical references for understanding the dignity of the person, the spiritual value of living together and the critique of forms that reduce the human being to an economic function. Olivetti’s elaborations keep their own originality.

**European federalism**, known also through contacts with Altiero Spinelli during exile, contributes to the reflection on the organisation of powers. The community proposal must be kept distinct from the different conceptions of federalism present in the same period.

### Contemporaries, collaborators and interlocutors

**Domenico Burzio** accompanies the American journey and represents the link between the technical experience of the company’s first generation and its modern reorganisation.

**Luigi Figini, Gino Pollini, Luigi Cosenza, Ignazio Gardella and other designers** translate into different works the relation between architecture, industry and housing. Each achievement requires its own attribution.

**Natale Capellaro, Marcello Nizzoli and Giovanni Pintori** illustrate the meeting of technical design, product and communication. Industrial excellence comes from collective skills and specific professional responsibilities.

**Roberto Olivetti and Mario Tchou** take a decisive role in the electronics project. Their responsibilities show how far the technological history of the company exceeds the action of Adriano alone.

**Franco Ferrarotti, Luciano Gallino and Franco Momigliano** belong to the world of the social sciences that interacts with the factory and with the community initiatives. Through these relations industrial organisation also becomes an object of critical research.

**The trade unions**, in particular CGIL and CISL, are indispensable interlocutors for understanding the agreements, the disputes and the limits of representative autonomy at Olivetti.

### Critical interlocutors and intellectual legacy

**Norberto Bobbio** is a reference for the reception and critique of the constitutional project. His assessments must be reconstructed through the actual texts, avoiding isolated quotations and summaries that alter their meaning.

**Davide Cadeddu** has contributed to the philological reconstruction of Olivetti’s political thought and of the different editions of *L’ordine politico delle Comunità*.

**Stefano Musso** lets us observe industrial participation through the history of the Management Council and the conflicts between company and workers.

**Giuliana Gemelli and Emilio Renzi** have explored, respectively, the biographical and cultural dimensions and the industrial and technological dimensions of the Olivetti story.

These scholars belong to the history of later reception and must not be confused with the protagonist’s personal interlocutors.

### Contemporary phenomena

The governance of complex technological systems, the responsibility of firms towards territories, the participation of workers, the training of managers and the fate of industrial cities are fields to which Olivetti’s reflection can offer categories of analysis.

None of these applications means that Olivetti directly anticipated contemporary technologies or institutions. The relation arises from comparing different problems, separated by different historical conditions.

## 28. Legacies

The industrial legacy concerns a network of skills, products and organisations that exceed the manager’s biography. Office mechanics, design and electronic research were developed by different workers, technicians and designers. The Elea 9003 belongs to Adriano’s phase; the Programma 101, presented in 1965, to the company’s later history.

The architectural legacy is particularly visible at Ivrea, recognised by UNESCO in 2018 for its industrial and urban whole. A building can nonetheless be conserved even when the economic model that made it possible has changed. The protection of heritage must be kept distinct from the continuity of employment relations and institutions.

The cultural legacy includes publishing output, social research, training and a network of intellectuals who helped renew Italian debate. These experiences have developments of their own and cannot be considered merely instruments of company strategy.

The political legacy is more limited in its achievements: the Movimento Comunità had local roots, but did not win a national force sufficient to transform the Constitution; the Foundation meant to divide ownership was not established in the form imagined. The Management Council, by contrast, outlived the founder until 1971, through modifications and conflicts.

The different legacies oblige a distinction: the survival of objects, buildings or ideas does not in itself prove the success of the institutions designed to distribute their governance. It is from this plurality of outcomes that the question about the real endurance of the community project arises.

## 29. The contemporary wound — When those who bear the consequences cannot decide

A decision taken in a boardroom may change the lives of workers and residents who never enter it. It may concern a plant, a digital platform, a supply chain or a system used to assign work. The effects fall on individuals and territories; the knowledge and authority needed to change them remain concentrated in the organisation that decides.

Olivetti’s experience supplies a distinction that is still useful: the quality of someone’s working conditions and their power to influence the decisions behind them must be judged separately. A company can innovate, pay well and invest in culture while keeping control of strategy at the top. A community can benefit from industrial activity without having the institutions to face the consequences of a change of ownership or location.

Automated systems make this problem particularly recognisable. In work through digital platforms, automated monitoring and decision support can affect the assignment of tasks, pay or the continuity of the relationship. Directive (EU) 2024/2831 on platform work, within its specific scope, regulates information, consultation of representatives, human oversight and review of certain decisions. Its content offers a contemporary example of rules built around the possibility of knowing and contesting an organisational power; it is not a translation of Olivetti’s institutions, nor a measure conceived from his thought.

To read this situation through Olivetti one must avoid two shortcuts. He cannot be described as an anticipator of today’s artificial intelligence. Nor does his idea of the Community offer an administrative blueprint for organisations that cross traditional territorial boundaries.

The contribution of his story is more precise. First, the project of the political orders asks how to connect specialist competence with public responsibility. Second, the history of the Management Council shows why a body can be judged only by knowing its powers, its access to information and its capacity for dissent. Finally, the imagined Foundation questions the relation between declared responsibility and the structure of ownership.

**The contemporary wound concerns people’s capacity to obtain explanations, exercise control and modify decisions that durably affect their living conditions.** Physical proximity is not a sufficient guarantee; geographical distance does not make irresponsibility inevitable. It is institutions that must organise the relation between those who decide and those who live the consequences.

Olivetti’s story documents both the importance and the difficulty of this. His constitutional proposal remained a project. Company participation realised some powers and left others concentrated. The transformation of ownership was not completed. This distance obliges us to ask, in the present, which mechanisms really work, which can be contested, and which withstand a change of leadership.

## 30. Limits and contradictions

### A partly unimplemented institutional project

The new constitutional structure outlined in 1945 was not adopted by the Italian State. The Camillo Olivetti Foundation remained a project of ownership transformation. The breadth of the design must not be confused with its implementation: this guide recognises the value of the proposal even where proof of its realisation is lacking.

### Qualified government and the risks of selection

Examinations, training paths and co-optation aimed to give government competence. Cadeddu recalls the constitutional subordination of these forms to universal suffrage; it remains to assess how far the system would have allowed pluralism, alternation and control over those who select the leaders. The tension between merit and legitimacy is not removed by the mere design of a new political order.

### Real participation with limited powers

The Management Council was a lasting institutional experiment, with different consultative and deliberative competences. The initial over-representation of clerks and managers relative to workers, and the retention of the main economic powers by management, prevent us from identifying it with a completed industrial democracy. The later changes and contestations show that its boundaries were debated from within.

### Trade-union autonomy and conflict

The closeness of Comunità di Fabbrica and then Autonomia Aziendale to the Movimento Comunità opened controversies over representation. Collaboration between management and workers produced concrete results; conflict remained present. To assess it one must study unions, consultations and agreements, avoiding both hagiography and the automatic dismissal of company-linked union forms.

### The form of social rights

The breadth of welfare was an achievement of great value. The Welfare Charter drawn up between 1949 and 1950 sought to turn services into rights and not concessions. But the company’s control over the amount of appropriations continued to influence the possibilities of those very rights: the problem of paternalism must be measured through these rules, not through a mere label.

### The centrality of the founder

Adriano promoted a greater distribution of responsibility while exercising an exceptionally influential leadership. The Council’s persistence until 1971 shows a real institutional continuity; the incompleteness of the transformation of ownership signals a different limit. The succession does not by itself prove the fallibility of his ideas, but it lets us distinguish initiatives sustained by a leader from institutions able to outlive him.

### The political ambiguities of his formation

His anti-Fascist activity, the 1943 arrest and the exile are documented. With equal clarity one must recall **the enrolment in the PNF on 31 July 1933**, together with the contacts with corporatist circles and some collaborations developed during the regime. Olivetti retrospectively attributed his enrolment to the need to protect the company; that explanation is a partisan testimony and does not eliminate the historical fact. The entrepreneur’s positions changed over time: this sequence allows neither an automatic biographical acquittal nor an undifferentiated identification with Fascist ideology.

### The economics of risk

Electronics and international expansion required high investment. Production results are not enough to describe financial sustainability. The crisis of 1963–1964 must be distinguished from the choices taken personally by Adriano and from the later ownership and managerial interventions. Without balance sheets and original documentation, attributing a single cause is not justified.

### The limit of transferability

Ivrea had a pre-existing industrial culture, skilled workers, a notable company position and the guidance of an entrepreneur with important resources and cultural relations. No comparison licenses us to suppose that the project would have produced identical results in every country or sector. The initiatives at Pozzuoli and in the Canavese show precisely the need to read different local conditions.

## 31. Curiosities that illuminate Olivetti

### The factory already belonged to his father’s story

After graduating, Adriano worked as a labourer in the company Camillo had founded in 1908. His organisational reforms were thus grafted onto an earlier technical and entrepreneurial culture, which he transformed without having created it from nothing.

### The 1945 book changed its subtitle

According to Davide Cadeddu’s reconstruction, the first edition of 1945 bears *Le garanzie di libertà in uno stato socialista* and that of 1946 *Dello Stato secondo le leggi dello spirito*. The catalogue of the Accademia dei Concordi documents the 1946 edition; the differing monthly dating attributed to the first release does not alter this sequence. The change of title makes visible a thought that cannot be reduced to a single political label.

### The Council was born of a difficult negotiation

Its history begins in the workers’ committees and in the resistance inside the company, well before the election of 22 March 1948. The initiative of the employees, the counter-proposals of management and negotiations broken off and resumed are part of its birth.

### A journal that could not represent everyone

The Council’s *Giornale di fabbrica*, launched in 1949, stopped in 1951 after disagreements over the composition of the editorial committee. The episode shows how hard it is to build a shared means of information when the people involved hold different powers.

### The Welfare Charter of 1949–1950

The Council promoted a conception of social services as rights of workers. It is an important case of the passage from assistance to institutional regulation, without attributing to the Council general control of the budget.

### The electronic machines came from an international network

From the American laboratory at New Canaan to the Italian one at Pisa, electronic innovation depended on investment and on specialists such as Mario Tchou and Roberto Olivetti. The Elea 9003 was presented in 1959, while the Programma 101 belongs to the phase after Adriano’s death.

### Underwood: two dates and two holdings

The agreement of October 1959 initially concerned about a third of the American company. The holding reached about 70% in 1960, after Olivetti’s death. The distinction keeps us from attributing to a single personal decision an industrial operation that developed over different times.

### A political reform that meets the ballot box

Olivetti became mayor of Ivrea in 1956 and a member of parliament in 1958. The contrast between roots in the Canavese and a limited national result lets us study the distance between a coherent cultural project and its capacity to win broad political representation.

## 32. Common mistakes

**“Adriano Olivetti founded the factory in 1908.”** It was Camillo Olivetti who founded it. Adriano led its industrial and cultural transformation at a later stage.

**“He was opposed to industrial efficiency.”** He studied and applied advanced methods of organisation; he sought to change their consequences and social purposes.

**“The community was only a set of company services.”** The project included a political and constitutional proposal, with territorial functions and rules of representation.

**“The workers governed the whole company.”** The Management Council had significant competences, above all over social services, but did not exercise general control of strategy.

**“Olivetti had already handed ownership over to the community.”** The transformation of ownership remained a proposal never realised in its overall form.

**“There were no union conflicts in Olivetti’s factories.”** Historiography documents complex relations among management, workers and trade unions.

**“Ivrea’s UNESCO listing shows that Olivetti’s political system worked.”** The recognition protects an architectural and urban heritage; it does not certify a fully implemented community order.

**“The Elea 9003 and the Programma 101 were both achieved under Adriano.”** The Elea 9003 was presented in 1959, during Adriano’s lifetime. The Programma 101 came in 1965, after his death.

**“The later crisis alone proves Adriano’s failure.”** Choices and economic conditions passed through several phases; responsibilities must be studied chronologically.

**“Olivetti simply proposed to eliminate politics.”** He sought a different form of political organisation, one that would reduce the centrality of parties and give weight to communities and functions of government. The practicability and legitimation of that model remain open questions.

## 33. Glossary

**Company welfare.** Services and social benefits organised or funded by a firm for its employees and, in some cases, their families.

**Concrete community.** The territorial and social unit Olivetti took as the basis of political, economic and cultural organisation.

**Co-optation.** A procedure by which a body selects new members through an internal decision. In Olivetti’s project it appears alongside other forms of legitimation.

**Corporatism.** Doctrine and practice of organising productive interests in corporations. Under Italian Fascism it operated within an authoritarian system, distinct from democratic trade-union representation.

**Edizioni di Comunità.** The publishing initiative developed in Olivetti’s milieu, committed to spreading political, philosophical, social and cultural works.

**Elea 9003.** The electronic computer presented by Olivetti in 1959, a result of research and industrial organisation in the field of data processing.

**Electronic research.** The scientific and design activities that led Olivetti to develop data-processing technologies.

**Federalism.** The organisation of public powers among several institutional levels with competences of their own. Olivetti’s formulation starts from the territorial communities.

**Industrial democracy.** A set of institutions and practices through which workers and their representatives can take part in the choices of the productive organisation. It includes models with very different degrees of power.

**IRUR.** The Istituto per il Rinnovamento Urbano e Rurale del Canavese, created in 1954 to promote economic initiatives and counter territorial imbalances.

**Management Council (*Consiglio di Gestione*).** A body of participation set up at Olivetti in Ivrea in 1948, active until 1971.

**Movimento Comunità.** The political organisation founded by Adriano Olivetti in 1947 to turn the community proposal into public and territorial initiatives.

**Paternalism, corporate.** A model in which management offers protections or benefits to workers while keeping a strongly dominant position in defining decisions. Applying the term to a historical case requires checking concrete practices.

**Personalism.** A set of philosophical currents that place at the centre the dignity and relational dimension of the person; one of the references of Olivetti’s thought.

**Political legitimation.** The conditions, procedures and principles by which the exercise of public power is recognised as valid and accountable.

**Political orders (*ordini politici*).** An element of Olivetti’s constitutional project, connected to the organisation of functions and the selection of public officials.

**Representation.** The institutional relation by which a person or body exercises functions in the name of given subjects or interests, according to recognisable procedures and limits.

**Territorial autonomy.** The capacity recognised to a body or community to exercise functions and responsibilities of its own within a wider order.

**Territorial planning.** The coordination of land uses, settlements, infrastructure and services according to objectives of collective interest.

**Universal suffrage.** The right to vote recognised to citizens meeting the general requirements set by law, regardless of class or profession.

## 34. Study and reading paths

### Path 1 — Understanding the political thought

Start with the section on the concrete community, go on to the institutional architecture of *L’ordine politico delle Comunità*, and compare the problems of representation with the module on expertise and freedom. The original 1945 text, the 1946 edition and Davide Cadeddu’s contemporary edition let us observe the evolution and complexity of the formulations. The most important step is to distinguish the principles from the practicability of individual procedures.

### Path 2 — Understanding the factory experiment

Read the history of industrial organisation, the Management Council, the trade unions and the social services. Stefano Musso’s research is the specialist reference for reconstructing the bodies and the conflicts through the documentation of industrial relations. Always ask which decision was entrusted to management, which to the Council and which to the bodies representing labour.

### Path 3 — Reading the industrial city

Start from Ivrea and the UNESCO recognition. Observe the relation among productive buildings, housing and services. Extend the comparison to Pozzuoli, La Martella and IRUR, distinguishing initiatives directly of the company from public interventions carried out with other subjects.

### Path 4 — Technology and the culture of innovation

Follow the productive transformations from the American journey to the mechanical products and the Elea 9003. Compare the role of the designers, the organisation of the laboratories and the financial structure of the investments. The capacity to innovate must be studied together with the distribution of responsibilities and industrial risk.

### Path 5 — The twelfth triad

Compare Olivetti’s project with the public industrial capacity built by Enrico Mattei and with the productive rooting in the inland Marche promoted by Aristide Merloni. The comparison concerns three distinct problems: building resources and capacities, the economic transformation of territories, and participation in governing them. There is no need to attribute a common programme to the protagonists to recognise the relevance of the questions.

- **1947:** the Movimento Comunità is born.

## 37. Frequently asked questions

### 1. Who was Adriano Olivetti?

Adriano Olivetti (1901–1960) was an Italian industrialist, publisher and political thinker. He led the international transformation of the company founded by his father Camillo and formulated a project of institutional reform based on the concrete community.

### 2. What is Adriano Olivetti’s political thought?

It seeks to reorganise the State through territorial communities endowed with real responsibilities, a federalist structure and forms of representation tied to suffrage, social functions and expertise. The proposal is developed above all in *L’ordine politico delle Comunità*.

### 3. What does “concrete community” mean?

It is the territorial and social unit that Olivetti imagines as the basis of the political order. It includes economic relations, culture and institutions, and should have effective capacities of government, beyond the mere closeness of its inhabitants.

### 4. When was *L’ordine politico delle Comunità* published?

The first edition appeared in **1945** with the subtitle *Le garanzie di libertà in uno stato socialista*. The second came out in **1946** with *Dello Stato secondo le leggi dello spirito*, according to Davide Cadeddu’s philological reconstruction, confirmed by the cataloguing of the second edition. The sources do not agree on the month of the first release; the original title pages have not been examined directly.

### 5. Was Adriano Olivetti a socialist?

His formation includes reformist socialism and anti-Fascism, while his mature thought integrates federalism, personalism and other traditions. The community project cannot be faithfully described by a single ideological label.

### 6. What was his relationship with Fascism?

Olivetti’s PNF membership is documented; the date **31 July 1933** is reported in his own account in a letter of 1958. He explained the choice as a measure to protect the company; his interpretation of his motives must be kept apart from the fact of membership. His biography includes anti-Fascist activity, the 1943 arrest and exile. The relationship with the regime must therefore be reconstructed in its phases, without erasing either the enrolment or the earlier and later initiatives of opposition.

### 7. What was the Movimento Comunità?

It was an organisation founded in 1947 to support the community project on the cultural, territorial and political levels. Olivetti became mayor of Ivrea in 1956 and a member of parliament in 1958; the Movement did not reach a significant national parliamentary presence.

### 8. What was the Olivetti Management Council?

It was a body set up in the Ivrea factory in 1948, with representation of workers and management, which operated until 1971. It had an important role above all with regard to social services, and consultative competences in other areas.

### 9. Did the workers decide how to run the company?

They did not exercise a general power over strategy, investment and ownership. The Management Council allowed real forms of participation within limited matters and procedures. The difference is central to understanding its historical significance.

### 10. Why is paternalism discussed in the Olivetti model?

Because many social benefits were organised within a company led by a strong ownership and management. Historiography examines the question together with the rights recognised, the union conflicts and the actual forms of representation.

### 11. What services did Olivetti offer its workers?

Over time the company developed health care, childcare services, canteens, libraries, housing support, transport and cultural activities. The benefits varied by period and plant and must not be assumed to be automatically uniform.

### 12. Why does Ivrea matter?

Ivrea is the main urban laboratory of the Olivetti experience. Factories, housing and services form an architectural and social whole that in 2018 received UNESCO recognition as an industrial city of the twentieth century.

### 13. Why did Olivetti build a plant at Pozzuoli?

The choice joined needs of industrial and commercial development to a sense of responsibility towards the Mezzogiorno and its employment problem. The plant, designed by Luigi Cosenza, was inaugurated in 1955.

### 14. What was IRUR?

IRUR was the Istituto per il Rinnovamento Urbano e Rurale del Canavese, founded in 1954 to encourage new economic activity and reduce employment and territorial imbalances.

### 15. What did Olivetti plan for the ownership of the company?

He imagined a Camillo Olivetti Foundation in which participation would involve shareholders, workers, the local community and institutions of knowledge. The project was not realised as an overall ownership structure of the company.

### 16. What was his relation to industrial design?

He promoted collaboration among engineers, designers, architects and artists, contributing to a recognisable productive and visual identity. The quality of the products depended on the work of figures such as Natale Capellaro, Marcello Nizzoli and Giovanni Pintori.

### 17. Did Olivetti invent the Elea 9003?

The Elea 9003 was the result of a collective project developed by the company and presented in 1959. Adriano backed its industrial direction; Mario Tchou, Roberto Olivetti and other specialists held decisive responsibilities in its realisation.

### 18. Did Adriano Olivetti invent the Programma 101?

No. The Programma 101 was presented in 1965, after his death, and came out of the work of the group led by Pier Giorgio Perotto. It belongs to the history of the electronic skills the company had developed.

### 19. Why was the Olivetti model not applied across Italy?

The constitutional project was not adopted and the Movimento Comunità did not win national political strength sufficient to promote it. The transformation of ownership also remained unfinished. The local experience, moreover, depended on specific economic and organisational conditions.

### 20. What can Adriano Olivetti teach us today?

His experience invites us to distinguish the quality of benefits from the distribution of power. It shows the importance of linking innovation, territory and institutions, while keeping open the check on who can actually take part in economic and political decisions.

### Method and limits of the reconstruction

This guide distinguishes documented events, historians’ assessments and the original interpretive categories of Cerchi d’inchiostro. The expressions *the right to govern a place*, *the boundary of participation* and *the test of institutional endurance* are editorial proposals and are not formulas attributed to Adriano Olivetti.

The publicly accessible documentation allows us to reconstruct the nature of the community project, its main initiatives and the difference between designed institutions and actual achievements. What was consulted directly comprises historical-critical contributions, bibliographic repertories and published archival reconstructions; **not** the full editions of the political treatises, the archive files on the PNF enrolment or the original minutes of the Management Council. This limit weighs above all on the interpretive details of the constitutional mechanisms, without entitling us to present them as verifications from primary sources. No page numbers or verbatim quotations are invented.

The economic and financial picture of the Underwood operation and of the later crisis would require an independent audit of balance sheets and original documents for more precise causal statements. Likewise, the overall employment effectiveness of IRUR is not quantified in the absence of consistent documentary series.

## 40. Editorial close — What a community can decide

The factory at Ivrea left behind objects, buildings and knowledge that belong to the industrial and cultural history of the twentieth century. Olivetti machines crossed national borders; his collaborators helped transform office work and electronic research; and the buildings gave lasting form to an exacting conception of the relationship between production and common life.

There was also a political undertaking, less immediately visible and harder to accept without reservation.

Olivetti understood that the consequences of industry reach beyond the boundaries of a company. He sought institutions capable of connecting economic power, expertise and the life of communities. He tried some forms of participation in practice and worked out others on paper. Conflicts and obstacles remained that he could not overcome.

His legacy does not require us to imagine a society in which interests always coincide. Human dignity, cultural development and spiritual freedom were essential ends of his thought. Participation in decisions is one of the institutional conditions through which Cerchi d’inchiostro chooses to examine those ends. A community’s worth can also be measured by whether its members can represent their differences, demand an account of decisions and change the direction in which their collective future is being taken.

The problem returns whenever a territory’s work, services and living conditions depend on powers that its inhabitants can only watch from a distance.

**A community gains responsibility for its own future when it has not only the resources it needs to live, but also the institutions through which it can take part in deciding how those resources are used.**

This is Cerchi d’inchiostro’s editorial reading of the problems and limits of Olivetti’s experience. It is not a quotation from him, nor a conclusion that history has proved true in every setting.

It is a question Olivetti’s life poses to our own time. His story reveals both the importance of asking it and the difficulty of giving it a durable institutional form.
