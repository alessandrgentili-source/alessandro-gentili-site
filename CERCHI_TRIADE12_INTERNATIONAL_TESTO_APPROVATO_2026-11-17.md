## State, enterprise and community
### Who builds the future — and who decides what it becomes?

In 1953, Enrico Mattei and Aristide Merloni met in Fabriano. Their conversation concerned an industrial opportunity. Mattei was seeking to expand the possibilities of Italy's public energy sector; Merloni had spent decades developing a manufacturing business in the inland Marche. Their interests met in the practical world of factories, markets and technical knowledge. The accounts of the proposed AGIP orders differ, and their respective ambitions should not be confused.

In Ivrea, Adriano Olivetti was pursuing another experiment. His company made internationally competitive machines, while his political thought addressed a question that industrial success could leave unresolved: what authority should belong to the people and communities whose lives are shaped by economic decisions?

These histories occupy three different positions in the organisation of industrial power. Mattei worked to enlarge a country's capacity to negotiate its economic dependencies. Merloni brought productive capabilities into places where employment had often required departure. Olivetti sought institutions through which workers and communities might exercise recognised powers of participation.

Their differences become especially significant when prosperity gives way to uncertainty. An enterprise may provide work without giving its employees authority over its strategic direction. Public ownership may expand national capabilities while leaving questions of political accountability unsettled. Social services may improve working lives without conferring control over investment or ownership.

The years after the founders' deaths make these tensions visible. ENI continued beyond Mattei. Merloni's industrial inheritance followed divergent paths. Olivetti's Management Council survived him until 1971, while his larger constitutional and ownership proposals remained unfulfilled.

What endures when a founder disappears, an employer changes ownership, or a technology loses its market?

The twelfth triad brings this question into the present. Industrial development can leave behind buildings, employment, skills and institutions. Their lasting significance also depends on what people and places remain capable of deciding when the conditions that first sustained them have changed.
