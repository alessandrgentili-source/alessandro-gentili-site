# Edgar Morin — Knowledge and Its Blind Spots

**Cerchi d’inchiostro · Author Guide 37 · Triad 13: Knowledge, Consequences and Decision**

## Introduction — A Question Before the Camera

In the summer of 1960, people on the streets of Paris are approached and asked about their lives. The inquiry begins with “How do you live?” and moves towards the more direct “Are you happy?”, a question Marceline Loridan also puts to passers-by. Edgar Morin and Jean Rouch are making *Chronique d’un été*, released in 1961: a film that will eventually turn its scrutiny upon its own search for truth. The people filmed speak of work, war, memory and private difficulty. At the end, the participants watch some of the footage and argue about what they have seen. A moment one person finds sincere seems contrived to another. The camera has recorded gestures and speech, yet it has also brought a situation into being: the filmed reality now includes its relationship with those observing it.

The problem extends beyond cinema. Inquiry needs a certain distance from its subject, yet every observer brings instruments, categories and expectations that shape what comes into view. Selection makes the world intelligible. Forgetting that we have selected is what allows an account of reality to pass, in our own eyes, for reality in its entirety.

Morin lived through more than a century of European history, gradually turning its wounds and upheavals into questions about knowledge. He encountered war and ideology, studied death, images, mass culture, the transformation of a community, life and systems of ideas. His investigations taught him to distrust explanations that claim to exhaust their subject, as well as visions of the whole that erase the necessary work of making distinctions. His lifelong undertaking raises a demanding question: how can we organise knowledge without losing the world it seeks to understand?

Today that question returns in other forms. A society may possess scientific expertise, archives, statistics and sophisticated instruments while struggling to judge a problem that crosses several domains. Morin’s work presses upon that distance between the knowledge we possess and the understanding we can achieve together.

## Edgar Morin at a glance

**Known before the Resistance as:** Edgar Nahoum. The Fondation Edgar Morin’s biography records that the additional names David and Salomon had been considered at his birth, but did not remain in family use. **Born:** Paris, 8 July 1921. **Died:** 29 May 2026, aged 104. **Fields:** sociology, anthropology, philosophy, epistemology, cinema, education and political thought. **Research institution:** the Centre national de la recherche scientifique (CNRS), which he joined in 1950. **Major undertaking:** the six volumes of *La Méthode*, published between 1977 and 2004. **Central question:** how can we understand phenomena that are at once distinguishable and interdependent without reducing them to a single explanatory principle?

Morin was a leading advocate of *complex thought*: an approach that asks us to place knowledge in context, to distinguish without severing all connections, and to recognise organisation, interaction and uncertainty. His work brings together social research, dialogue with the natural sciences and ethical inquiry. Complex thought is no algorithm for solving every problem. It demands that we examine the conditions and limits of each explanation.

## Why Morin matters

Modernity has enormously expanded our capacity to know. Disciplines have differentiated, methods have grown more exacting, and analysis has isolated factors that once remained confused. Specialisation has been an achievement: without it, much of contemporary medicine, physics, ecology and the social sciences would not exist. Yet knowledge of one part can produce a distorted picture when it forgets the limits of its own field of vision.

An ecological problem involves chemical and biological processes, institutions, economic incentives, political decisions and different timescales. Expertise in each field is indispensable; no single field is necessarily sufficient to understand the problem as a whole. For Morin, the work of reconnecting what inquiry has had to distinguish becomes a question in its own right.

The disciplines retain their methods and boundaries in his work. What demands renewed attention is the quality of the relationships among them. This is why his thought moves across apparently distant questions: human identity, education, democracy, the fate of the planet, and the relationship between what an action intends and what it brings about.

## The human problem: knowing from within the world

Human beings describe phenomena of which they are part. They produce theories while belonging to the cultures that furnish those theories with words, instruments and priorities. They observe society while participating in one; study the mind with a mind of their own; investigate nature through technologies that materially transform their relationship with it. The observer’s position must therefore be examined. This scrutiny is compatible with discriminating between well-grounded knowledge and arbitrary claims.

Morin seeks a rationality capable of correcting itself. Error does not arise only from ignorance. It can develop within coherent systems of ideas, especially when those systems cease to recognise contrary facts, other scales or alternative interpretations supported by evidence. To know is to accept the risk of discovering that our categories are inadequate.

This is a human problem as much as an epistemological one. No one can live by postponing action until knowledge is complete. Uncertainty makes responsibility more difficult, requiring attention to effects that escape the original purpose of a decision.

## Life in brief

**1921–1931.** Edgar Nahoum is born in Paris into a family of Sephardic Jewish origin. His mother, Luna, dies in 1931. The encounter with death will remain a recurrent presence in his work.

**The 1930s and the war.** Philosophy, literature and political writing shape his intellectual formation. After France’s defeat in 1940 he takes refuge in Toulouse; during the occupation he joins the Resistance and adopts the name Morin. According to the account later given of the episode, it arose from someone mishearing “Magnin”, the clandestine name he had chosen with André Malraux’s *L’Espoir* in mind. He comes to know the antifascist struggle from within, alongside the tensions of communist commitment.

**1946–1951.** He writes about postwar Germany. He joins the CNRS in 1950. In 1951 he publishes *L’Homme et la Mort* and is expelled from the French Communist Party. His break with dogmatism receives fuller public expression in *Autocritique* (1959); the expulsion and the later reflection remain distinct moments.

**The 1950s and 1960s.** He studies cinema, stardom, mass culture, everyday life and social change. He contributes to the journal *Arguments*, founded in 1956, and to the Centre d’études des communications de masse, established in January 1960 on Georges Friedmann’s initiative, with Roland Barthes among its early contributors; the journal *Communications* becomes a forum for inquiry and debate. With Jean Rouch he makes *Chronique d’un été* (1961). He conducts research in Plozévet, Brittany, and publishes studies of modernisation and collective phenomena.

**1969 and the 1970s.** A stay at the Salk Institute in La Jolla in 1969 deepens his engagement with biology and genetics. Cybernetics, systems theory and information theory increasingly enter his epistemological construction. *Le Paradigme perdu: la nature humaine* (1973) marks a passage between anthropology and the study of organisation; in 1977 the publication of *La Méthode* begins.

**1977–2004.** He publishes the six volumes of his project while continuing to address politics, education and planetary questions. *Terre-Patrie* (with Anne-Brigitte Kern, 1993) and his books on the reform of thought carry these concerns into civic life.

**The final decades.** His essays, autobiographical writing and public interventions continue until his death in 2026. His longevity is a remarkable biographical fact; the achievement of his work must nevertheless be judged by its texts and the problems they address.

## The life that illuminates the work

The death of his mother alone cannot account for *L’Homme et la Mort*. Yet the loss helps illuminate why Morin’s first major anthropological book takes up an experience that exceeds biological description: the ways human beings create rituals, beliefs, images and relationships around mortality. Death is a natural event and a cultural fact. Its significance changes from one society to another without losing its material reality.

The Resistance offers another kind of apprenticeship. The young Morin must choose under the pressure of historical violence. His commitment to communism and subsequent break with dogmatism resist the neat plot of a moral conversion. In *Autocritique* (1959) he examines his own beliefs and the power of ideological justification. One philosophical question we may draw from that history—as an interpretation, rather than a proven causal explanation—concerns the mechanisms through which an educated intelligence can protect an illusion.

Cinema and the sociology of culture complete this trajectory. Society speaks through more than institutions and explicit ideologies: it also speaks through films, stars, everyday rituals, desires, consumer goods and stories. For Morin, understanding a civilisation means questioning the images through which it imagines itself.

## A century of divided knowledge and new connections

The twentieth century unsettles many inherited certainties about the world’s stability. Mass warfare exposes the abyss into which technical rationality may fall when detached from moral judgment. Physics and biology investigate irreversibility, organisation and transformation; cybernetics and systems theory place feedback and interdependence at the centre of inquiry; the social sciences study individuals and institutions in relationships that change over time.

Morin approaches these developments from a distinctive position. Cybernetics, mathematical information theory and quantitative models of complexity were developed by others; his undertaking was to bring their insights into conversation with a wider reform of knowledge. His interpretations have been influential and, at times, disputed. Recognising both facts keeps the breadth of a synthesis from being mistaken for proof of every step within it.

## The disciplinary circles

**Sociology and mass culture.** Cinema, film stars, consumption and collective forms of everyday life help explain how societies produce meanings. What matters to a society cannot always be found in its official documents.

**Anthropology.** Death, the imagination and identity disclose a human condition in which biological, symbolic and cultural elements intersect without becoming interchangeable.

**Philosophy and epistemology.** Morin examines knowledge and reality, observer and observed, error and correction, parts and wholes. This is the central terrain of *La Méthode*.

**Natural sciences and systems.** Biology, thermodynamics, cybernetics and systems theory provide problems and models with which Morin engages. Cross-disciplinary analogies need scrutiny: a concept that works in one domain does not automatically carry the same meaning into another.

**Education.** Schools and universities are called upon to cultivate the ability to place knowledge in context, as well as to transmit specialist understanding. Reforming thought does not mean abolishing disciplinary competence.

**Politics and ethics.** Democratic life needs citizens able to understand shared problems and decisions accountable for their consequences. Complexity cannot serve as an excuse for evading judgment.

## The works: a library in motion

*L’Homme et la Mort* (1951) explores the human relationship with mortality. *Le Cinéma ou l’Homme imaginaire* (1956) and *Les Stars* (1957) treat cinema and stardom as anthropological and social phenomena. *Autocritique* (1959) revisits the relationship between political commitment and dogmatism. *L’Esprit du temps* (1962) examines mass culture. *Commune en France: La Métamorphose de Plodémet* (1967) grew out of the investigations into the modernisation of Plozévet—the real name of the community studied, transformed in the book’s title. *La Rumeur d’Orléans* (1969) investigates the circulation of a collective rumour and its antisemitic force.

*Le Paradigme perdu: la nature humaine* (1973) prepares a reflection on humanity spanning biology and culture. *Science avec conscience* (1982) argues for scientific inquiry attentive to its own responsibilities. *Introduction à la pensée complexe* (1990) offers a more compact entrance to the principles of complexity. *Terre-Patrie* (1993, with Anne-Brigitte Kern) develops the question of our shared planetary condition. *La Tête bien faite* (1999) and *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur* (1999) examine the organisation of knowledge and education.

These books follow related questions along different paths. The sociology of culture has concerns of its own; epistemological inquiry works at another scale; civic and educational writings examine the consequences of how knowledge is organised. Reading them together reveals continuities without erasing their differences.

## Plozévet: a community that resists the statistical average

During the 1960s, Morin took part in a broad interdisciplinary investigation of the Breton community of Plozévet, in Finistère. Several research teams and methods are involved; his part in the inquiry becomes *Commune en France: La Métamorphose de Plodémet* (1967). “Plodémet” is the pseudonym chosen for the title. Morin explained that he had thought it necessary to protect the place’s identity before abandoning the pseudonym in later editions. The choice of name, in itself, tells us little about Morin’s method.

The community’s transformation resists a simple opposition between tradition and modernity. Small businesses coexist with new forms of consumption, local attachments, political conflicts and differing social aspirations. Understanding these developments calls for close observation, history, economics, anthropology and attention to everyday conduct. Research reveals the interconnections, yet must still reckon with the singularity of the place it describes.

The inquiry provoked controversy. Bernard Paillard’s later account of the “affaire Morin” documents hostile local reactions to the book and the dispute over outsiders’ portrayal of Plozévet. André Burguière’s *Bretons de Plozévet* (1975) examines the community over a longer historical span. Read together, these studies ask whether the changes visible after the war can be understood without the transformations already under way in earlier generations. The tension is one of scale and interpretation: different enquiries can illuminate the same place without producing an identical portrait.

As in *Chronique d’un été*, the people being observed can challenge the account made of them. The community responds to its own representation; that response becomes part of the history of the inquiry. In the reading developed by Cerchi d’inchiostro, statistics, words, consumption patterns, landscapes and social practices are signs that demand several methods of interpretation. No single indicator should be allowed to speak for an entire territory.

## Module I — The camera inside reality

*Chronique d’un été* offers a remarkably concrete experiment in knowing. The filmmakers ask questions, form relationships, record faces and voices. They then screen some of the footage for the participants, who debate whether the images faithfully convey their experiences. The recording itself has become the subject of discussion.

The Cerchi d’inchiostro reading distinguishes three levels: the interviewees’ lives beyond the film; the encounter created by the presence of the camera; and the representation shaped by editing, then reconsidered by those filmed. The document remains, now carrying its own history and point of view. Here reflexivity lies in the ability to question the act of documentation itself.

That passage from the film to Morin’s thought requires care. A cinematic episode cannot establish that every scientific observation alters its object in the same way. A laboratory experiment, an interview and an astronomical observation operate under different conditions. What they share is a question: which features belong to the phenomenon, which to the apparatus of observation, and what controls allow us to distinguish or reconstruct those two dimensions?

In the film, the observer cannot simply claim to be absent. In scientific inquiry, rigour requires procedures and instruments to be known and open to challenge. In both cases knowledge grows when its own standpoint can be examined. This is where the film meets Morin’s principle of the **reintroduction of the knowing subject into all knowledge**: the person who knows is no abstract consciousness standing outside history, culture and the instruments of inquiry. Morin seeks to make knowledge answerable for the conditions under which it is produced.

## *La Méthode*: the making of an intellectual undertaking

The singular title matters: *La Méthode*. Its six volumes, published by Éditions du Seuil between 1977 and 2004, constitute Morin’s most extensive project. The Bibliothèque nationale de France confirms the sequence:

1. *La Nature de la nature* (1977) addresses organisation, order and disorder through the physical world.
2. *La Vie de la vie* (1980) explores the organisation of living things, autonomy and ecological dependence.
3. *La Connaissance de la connaissance* (1986) investigates knowing, its instruments and its limits.
4. *Les Idées: Leur habitat, leur vie, leurs mœurs, leur organisation* (1991) considers the life of ideas in minds and societies.
5. *L’Humanité de l’humanité: L’Identité humaine* (2001) reconstructs the multiple dimensions of human identity.
6. *Éthique* (2004) examines responsibility and action in the relationships between individual, society and humanity.

The larger movement runs through nature, life, knowledge, ideas, humanity and ethics. The project did not begin in its eventual six-part form. He also envisaged a structure in three volumes; a manuscript written in 1983–1984 as the third and concluding volume was set aside while the project continued to grow. The sequence is no ladder leading automatically from physics to morality. Morin repeatedly returns to earlier concepts, carries them into other fields and reformulates them. His wager is that certain problems of organisation can be thought across disciplinary boundaries, without collapsing the differences in method or evidence between fields.

The scale of the undertaking also exposes its central difficulty. A theoretical architecture of such breadth may illuminate analogies, bring relationships into view and open new questions; it may also leave uncertain the passage from philosophical metaphor to empirically testable explanation. Even the most general principle must encounter the variables, measurements and evidence proper to a particular phenomenon.

The enduring value of *La Méthode* lies in the questions it asks about how we know and organise problems. The experimental methods of individual disciplines remain indispensable.

## Module II — The price of an explanation

Every explanation gains its force through a choice. It delimits what will count, identifies relevant variables, retains certain relationships and leaves others aside. This is necessary work. An account that tried to include everything without distinguishing anything would cease to explain.

The cost appears when we forget that a choice was made. Return to Plozévet: demographic data, working lives and patterns of consumption each disclosed part of the community’s transformation, while the disputes over Morin’s account exposed the difficulty of allowing one portrait to stand for everyone. A statistical measure can be accurate within its stated limits and still leave a question unanswered. The same difficulty arises when an economic indicator is asked to represent quality of life, an educational classification to contain a student’s experience, or an environmental assessment to settle claims that the evidence has not yet established. Each instrument has a purpose; explaining its reach is part of using it well.

The Cerchi d’inchiostro reading proposes four questions with which to examine an explanation:

**What does it bring into view?** Which phenomenon does it describe better than alternative interpretations, on what evidence, and at what scale?

**What does it leave out?** Which dimensions does it legitimately exclude, and which omissions might distort the problem?

**Which relationships does it consider?** Does the account allow for reciprocal effects, context and duration, or assume that the phenomenon stands apart from its surroundings?

**How might it be corrected?** What evidence, testimony or comparison would require us to revise the account we have given? This question concerns the warrant for a description; the practical revision of a policy or intervention belongs to a different problem. It also enters into conversation with fallibilist traditions and is not a rule of scientific falsification unique to Morin.

These are four questions proposed here for examining an explanation, inspired by Morin’s demand for contextualisation and knowledge of knowledge. They are an editorial instrument whose value depends on being tested against a particular account and its evidence; they are not a protocol Morin himself formulated.

## Key ideas

**Distinguishing and connecting.** Analysis needs to separate things in order to understand them; comprehension requires us to bring what we have distinguished back into relationship. Complex thought attempts both movements.

**Parts, wholes and organisation.** A whole need not equal the sum of the properties its components display in isolation. The arrangement of relationships may generate new behaviours and constraints.

**Order and disorder.** Organisational processes may weave together regularity, instability, disturbance and change. Disorder does not automatically create, nor does order necessarily mean immobility.

**Autonomy and dependence.** A living being sustains itself through exchanges with its environment. Autonomy is not isolation; it may require organised forms of dependence.

**Knowing knowledge.** Theories, perceptions and ideas must also be examined through the processes that produce, test, revise and sometimes mislead us. The conditions of inquiry deserve scrutiny alongside its results.

**The paradigm’s blind spot.** Knowledge may become blind precisely where it operates most confidently. The categories that make some questions possible may render others unthinkable. For Morin, controlling error also requires examining the cultural and institutional premises of inquiry.

**Uncertainty as a condition.** More information cannot remedy every uncertainty. In some settings, action itself changes the conditions we were trying to predict.

**Understanding and humanity.** Human life involves biological, cultural, emotional, social and individual dimensions. An account confined to a single dimension risks impoverishing our understanding of lived experience.

**An ethics of correction.** To act is to follow consequences, attend to new information and revise a strategy. Good intentions offer no guarantee of a good outcome.

## Key concepts

### Complexity and complex thought

For Morin, complexity concerns the interweaving of heterogeneous elements, interactions, organisation, context and uncertainty that makes analytical decomposition alone insufficient. *Complex thought* seeks to address these problems by bringing distinctions and relationships together. In “Restricted Complexity, General Complexity”, presented in 2005 and published in 2007, Morin distinguishes scientific research into complex systems from a general complexity that also questions the knowing subject and the foundations on which knowledge is organised. The distinction does not deny the value of quantitative modelling. It keeps two different enterprises from being treated as though they were identical.

### The paradigm of simplification and the blindness of knowledge

Morin examines operations of disjunction, reduction and abstraction that have made decisive scientific achievements possible but may become obstacles when elevated into absolutes. A paradigm selects what counts as a problem and what may qualify as an explanation: it enables certain forms of knowledge while concealing other relationships. In *Les Sept Savoirs*, the chapter on the blindness of knowledge considers error, illusion, cultural imprinting and the normalisation of beliefs. A blind spot can arise when a system of ideas protects the very criteria by which it declares itself true.

### The systemic and organisational principle

Knowledge of the parts requires knowledge of the organised whole, and the reverse is equally true. The whole can give rise to emergent properties and constraints that limit the possibilities of some of its components. In Morin’s interpretation, the whole may therefore be both more and less than the sum of its parts. Applying the principle always requires specifying what kind of system is under consideration.

### The hologrammatic principle

Morin suggests that, in certain respects, the part retains a presence of the whole and the whole is implicated in its parts. Individuals carry the language, culture and institutions of the society that also reproduces itself through them. This is a heuristic proposal: the analogy with a hologram does not prove that every material fragment literally contains all the information in a system.

### Feedback and organisational recursivity

*Feedback*, a pivotal concept in cybernetics, describes an effect that modifies the process from which it arose: a control mechanism may stabilise or amplify a change. *Organisational recursivity* goes beyond a regulatory loop: products and effects contribute to producing or regenerating the organisation from which they emerge. For Morin, individuals and society offer an interpretive example. Conflating the two principles would erase a distinction important to his theoretical architecture.

### The dialogic principle and dialectics

The dialogic principle holds together complementary and antagonistic principles that jointly organise a phenomenon: order and disorder, autonomy and dependence, individual and society. It differs from an account of dialectics in which tension must necessarily be overcome through a final synthesis. Morin gives contradiction a strong epistemological role, extending to an inquiry into the limits of logical thought. His dialogic principle invites us to interpret tensions within a phenomenon, rather than providing a formal rule that makes incompatible propositions true in the same sense. Evaluating such propositions remains a separate logical task.

### Auto-eco-organisation

The autonomy of a living system depends upon material, energetic and informational exchanges with its environment. Maintaining an organisation entails dependencies: autonomy and dependence are not simply mutually exclusive. The concept offers a way of thinking about living organisation, rather than a single experimental model for every organism.

### Emergence

Properties and behaviours may depend on the organised relationships among elements and may not be deducible from an inventory of isolated parts. To use the concept scientifically, we must specify the system, scale, property and conditions under which it appears. Merely invoking emergence does not explain a process.

### Reintroducing the knowing subject

This principle brings the question of blind spots into sharper focus. Anyone who produces knowledge does so through education, language, instruments, practices and a cultural horizon. The conditions of observation must therefore themselves be open to examination. Truth does not thereby become a matter of individual opinion; its pursuit requires reflexivity, intersubjective scrutiny and a willingness to correct error. *Chronique d’un été* makes a concrete version of the problem visible without standing in for scientific proof.

### Knowledge of knowledge

This is Morin’s inquiry into the biological, anthropological, cognitive, cultural and social conditions of knowing, developed above all in the third volume of *La Méthode*. Perception, interpretation and argument depend on selections; errors and illusions may arise within the very mechanisms that produce knowledge. Second-order inquiry asks how our representations come to carry authority and how they can be corrected.

### The ecology of action

Once initiated, an action enters a network of interactions that can redirect its consequences away from the original intention. Morin stresses the need to follow those consequences and to distinguish a *programme*, designed for predetermined conditions, from a *strategy* capable of adapting to unforeseen events. In this framework, the *pari*—a conscious wager made under uncertainty—is one element of action. The ecology of action can guide responsibility, but it neither assigns blame automatically nor determines which policy should be chosen.

## The constellation

**Antecedents.** Pascal raises the problem of the relationship between parts and wholes; Montaigne and the tradition of self-examination ask what it means to investigate humanity without the pretensions of a closed system. Hegel and Marx offer distinct ways of conceiving totality, contradiction and history. Gaston Bachelard is an important reference point for thinking about the construction of knowledge and its obstacles. These relationships are not equivalent: philosophical ancestry must be distinguished from documented influence.

**Sciences and interlocutors.** Norbert Wiener and cybernetics, Ludwig von Bertalanffy and general systems theory, and Claude Shannon and mathematical information theory are crucial interlocutors for Morin’s intellectual development. Theories of self-organisation and the work of Heinz von Foerster, Henri Atlan, Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela broaden the questions at stake. Ilya Prigogine offers a point of comparison on thermodynamics and irreversibility. Jean Rouch is a genuine collaborator in cinema; Georges Friedmann plays an important part in the beginnings of Morin’s research career.

**Dialogues and reception.** Jean-Louis Le Moigne and Mauro Ceruti figure prominently in debates about complex thought. Morin’s work has found readers in educational research and interdisciplinary inquiry, though the popularity of its language is no evidence, on its own, that all educational practices inspired by Morin have been effective.

**Disagreements and corrections.** Carlos Reynoso is a direct critic of Morin’s paradigm; Blay and Piqueira challenge specific readings of scientific thinkers; Arpaia offers an interpretive examination of incompleteness. Systems theory and information theory possess objects and formalisms that cannot be wholly subsumed into Morin’s criticism of reductionism. Some scholars dispute the accuracy of his accounts of his scientific predecessors; others ask how his paradigm might become more operational. The resulting disagreement is real and essential to assessing the scope of his work.

**The present.** The governance of technical systems, ecological crises and cooperation between disciplines can all be questioned through Morin’s categories. This does not mean he foresaw today’s artificial-intelligence models or devised technical solutions for regulating them. It is our contemporary interpretation that uses his questions to examine selections, omissions and consequences.

## Educating for pertinent knowledge

Morin’s educational thought addresses a difficulty that runs through civic life: having information does not automatically mean knowing how to place it within a problem. Schools may succeed in transmitting knowledge while falling short if students never learn how to ask questions, compare sources, recognise relationships or distinguish certainty, probability and ignorance.

In *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur*, written at UNESCO’s invitation and published in 1999, Morin identifies seven questions that education should address: the risks of error and illusion; the principles of pertinent knowledge; the human condition; earthly identity; uncertainty; mutual understanding among human beings; and the ethics of humankind. He does not call for the abolition of disciplinary knowledge. His concern is how it is organised and brought into relationship with life.

Education for complexity nevertheless encounters an institutional problem. Who is to teach students to make connections? With what preparation, time and means of assessment? The philosophical proposal gains substance when it comes into contact with curricula, evaluation methods and the material conditions of schooling. Without that encounter, it risks remaining an admirable wish too indefinite to correct actual practices.

Cognitive democracy poses a similar question. Many shared decisions demand knowledge that no citizen can fully possess. Imagining universal expertise offers no solution. We need institutions in which the premises of choices can be challenged, uncertainties communicated, controls exercised and different forms of expertise heard. Making debate accessible is a public good only when intellectual rigour is preserved.

## Module III — When a decision changes the problem

Morin’s own political history gives the question of revising a course of action a human scale. He took part in the Resistance and committed himself to communist politics; in 1951 he was expelled from the French Communist Party. In *Autocritique* (1959) he revisited the beliefs and intellectual defences that had sustained his commitments. These episodes belong to his intellectual biography. They show a thinker confronting the premises of earlier judgments, though they cannot by themselves establish a general law of unforeseen consequences.

The *ecology of action* addresses a further difficulty. Once an intervention enters a field of other people’s responses and competing forces, its effects may depart from the purpose for which it began. Sociologists had already studied unintended consequences, notably Robert K. Merton in 1936. Morin asks what becomes of the actor’s knowledge while the action unfolds. A *programme* relies on sufficiently stable conditions; a *strategy* revises its course as those conditions change. His idea of a *pari*, or conscious wager, acknowledges the risk carried by a decision without treating uncertainty as permission for indifference.

Consider a public measure intended to resolve a practical difficulty. Its **intention** may be stated clearly, and its **intervention** carefully administered, yet the people affected may alter their behaviour in response. Its **consequences** then include effects and incentives the original design did not anticipate. **Correction** depends on examining what actually happened, hearing those affected and deciding who has the authority to change course. This is an illustrative example, not a claim about a particular historical policy; the four terms serve here as distinctions for judgment, not as a universal managerial cycle.

Responsibility must therefore be examined as events unfold. Scientifically foreseeable harm, a neglected possibility and an event beyond reasonable anticipation call for different judgments. Tracking an action is a demand of Morin’s complex thought; the assessment of specific obligations still requires evidence, institutions and ethical criteria that epistemology alone cannot supply.

This argument stands on Morin’s own account of knowing and acting. It also opens a comparison within Triad 13 without making the authors interchangeable: Rachel Carson investigates material effects of human interventions in ecosystems, while John Maynard Keynes examines economic choices shaped by expectations about an uncertain future. Morin’s contribution is the evolving relation between an actor’s knowledge and the situation that the action itself helps transform.

## Legacy and reception

Morin’s reflections have been especially prominent in debates on education and in interdisciplinary culture. UNESCO’s invitation to write *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur* provides institutional evidence of that influence. His writings on the reform of thought have also circulated across philosophy, sociology and organisational theory.

Wide circulation does not by itself demonstrate improved educational or scientific outcomes. A claim that a particular Morin-inspired pedagogy improves learning would require studies of practices, populations, methods and results. A claim that a scientific model based on Morin explains a system better would demand comparison with alternative models. Intellectual influence is a legitimate finding; it should not be confused with empirical effectiveness.

One enduring legacy is a methodological question: which relationships do we lose when we establish our fields of expertise and draw their boundaries? And how might those relationships become visible again without dissolving the quality of knowledge that the disciplines have produced?

## The contemporary wound — An informed society unable to make connections

We now produce archives, models, images and measurements on a scale earlier generations could scarcely imagine. Many public problems are described by specialists capable of analysing real, verifiable aspects of them. Yet translating this knowledge into shared understanding remains difficult. A system may display a great many indicators while leaving obscure the relationship between outcomes, costs and the lives of those affected.

Morin identifies a particular distance: that between divided bodies of knowledge and problems that are multidimensional, global and interdependent. The diagnosis runs through *La Tête bien faite* and *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur*. The gap grows when procedures conceal the criteria by which a problem was defined, disciplines communicate poorly, or the precision of a single measure is mistaken for completeness of judgment.

This is also where an inquiry pursued by Cerchi d’inchiostro comes into focus. *Il costo della complessità* (*The Cost of Complexity*) examines the simplification of thought as an impoverishment of civic mediation. *Il potere di leggere i segni* (*The Power to Read Signs*) asks who has the power to determine which signals, data and interpretations acquire authority. *Giovani e senilità del potere* (*Youth and the Senility of Power*) considers the education of those who will have to decide. The problem of disconnected knowledge belongs to Morin’s own thought. The question of who determines which signs become authoritative, and the emphasis on the civic formation of decision-makers, belong to the interpretation offered by Cerchi. The encounter may deepen our arguments and correct them, reminding us that rigorous science depends upon the indispensable operations of distinguishing and methodical simplification.

We need to defend the complexity of phenomena while distrusting complication used as an excuse. Not every opaque process is profound; not every accessible explanation is superficial. At times clarity is precisely the result of difficult, exacting intellectual labour. Trouble begins when clarity is achieved by erasing relationships, or when the word *complexity* conceals an absence of accountability.

A society can grow ever more skilful at describing fragments of the world while becoming less able to answer for what happens between them. Understanding begins when the boundary of our expertise ceases to be the boundary of our attention.

## Limits and contradictions

**The breadth of the project.** Morin ranges across physics, biology, anthropology, sociology and philosophy. In *Modelos o metáforas* (2009), Carlos Reynoso directly challenges the identification of Morin’s paradigm with scientific theories of complexity, alleging conceptual and technical inaccuracies. His is a sustained and polemical criticism, not a verdict shared by the entire scientific community. It nevertheless calls for careful scrutiny of the passage from a philosophical analogy to a scientifically specified model.

**Formalisation.** Complex thought does not, by itself, furnish quantitative laws, estimates or predictions for every phenomenon it describes. Reynoso’s criticism asks which operational models actually follow from its categories. Morin’s distinction between restricted and general complexity permits the reply that the two enterprises have different aims; it does not make scrutiny of their applications unnecessary.

**The relationship with foundational sciences.** In “The Emergence of Edgar Morin’s Complex Thinking” (2024), Enio A. Blay and José Roberto C. Piqueira discuss Morin’s interpretations of Wiener, von Bertalanffy and Shannon, judging some of his criticisms potentially unfair. In particular, Morin may be asking mathematical information theory to account for meaning, a question outside the formal task Shannon chose. We must distinguish limitations within a theory from limitations resulting from its deliberately defined field of application.

**Comparison with other paradigms.** Robert Delorme’s “Deep Complexity and the problem of criticality in Morin’s and Le Moigne’s accounts of complexity” (published in 2013 in volume 19, issue 1, dated 2012) compares Morin’s and Le Moigne’s principles with his own *Deep Complexity* paradigm, paying particular attention to *criticality* and modelling. This comparison should not be recast as a wholesale rejection of Morin. It differentiates approaches to thinking about, modelling and making complexity operational.

**Contradiction and logic.** The appeal to the dialogic principle must not confuse tensions within reality, contradictions in language and formal incompatibilities. In “Edgar Morin e l’epistemologia dell’incompletezza” (2021), Roberto Arpaia offers a substantially sympathetic reconstruction of Morin’s use of results associated with Gödel and Tarski. His article documents one interpretation of Morin; it does not itself refute that use. The critical question raised here is different: how far can mathematical results, valid under specific assumptions and within defined domains, support more general epistemological conclusions? This objection must be assessed on the merits of the arguments; Arpaia’s sympathetic reconstruction is not evidence that he shares it.

**The promise of practical guidance.** A call to connect fields of knowledge may be persuasive yet insufficient for choosing among alternatives. Public problems involve costs, priorities and responsibilities that epistemology cannot decide automatically. The ecology of action invokes a duty to correct course; on its own, it does not establish who bears responsibility for harm.

**The danger of rhetoric.** Words such as “network”, “interdependence”, “whole”, “relationship” and “emergence” can become a vocabulary that reassures without explaining. They earn their place only when they illuminate relationships that can be identified and tested.

**A further philosophical check.** In “Where Is Morin’s Road to Complexity Going?” (2004), Laurent Dobuzinskis critically examines the implications of Morin’s path for political philosophy and the social sciences. Morin’s own approach also offers an objection to any ambitious synthesis: an account constructed on a great scale may attribute coherence to phenomena that close research does not confirm. Plozévet reminds us that the singular case resists general theory. A sound interpretation must allow the case it studies to correct it.

These objections help define the reach of Morin’s work. His challenge to the organisation of knowledge still matters, and the language of complexity must itself withstand the demands of precision.

## Illuminating details

**A name born of a misunderstanding.** “Morin” was not his original clandestine name. He had adopted “Magnin” in reference to André Malraux’s *L’Espoir*; a fellow Resistance member heard and repeated it as “Morin”. According to the French Ministry of Culture’s account, the misunderstanding became a name that endured.

**The beginnings of a research career.** He joined the CNRS in 1950, before publishing his book on death, at a time when his intellectual profile ranged across sociology, philosophy and political engagement.

**Giving the interviewees a voice.** In *Chronique d’un été*, participants are more than people appearing before a camera. They also discuss the way they have been represented, turning the documentary into an inquiry into its own truth.

**Plozévet and Plodémet.** Morin’s investigation of the Breton community of Plozévet was published under a title using the name “Plodémet”. Distinguishing the real place from the book’s title prevents bibliographic confusion.

**A workshop lasting decades.** The six volumes of *La Méthode* appeared over twenty-seven years, between 1977 and 2004, as an intellectual project repeatedly enlarged and revised.

**The recovered manuscript.** In 2024 Actes Sud published *La Méthode de La Méthode: Le manuscrit perdu*, with the collaboration of Jean Tellez. The BnF catalogue describes the text as originally written in 1983–1984, when it was envisaged as the third volume. Its publication during Morin’s lifetime lets readers trace changes in the project’s architecture. It was a preparatory text intended as a third and final volume, not another volume that alters the sequence of the six canonical Seuil volumes (1977–2004). Some booksellers and catalogue descriptions from 2024 nevertheless classify it as an additional volume in the series; that convention should be kept distinct from the structure of the work Morin published during his lifetime.

## Common misconceptions

**“Morin proves that everything is connected.”** A generic appeal to connection explains little. Relationships, mechanisms, scales and conditions need to be identified.

**“Complex thought and the sciences of complexity are the same thing.”** They partly overlap, but differ in purpose, standards of proof and degree of formalisation.

**“Complexity rejects clarity.”** Morin criticises unwarranted reduction, not the rigorous distinctions that make a phenomenon intelligible.

**“The dialogic principle makes every contradiction true.”** Morin’s dialogic principle holds that antagonistic principles can remain joined in the interpretation of a phenomenon without a final synthesis. Any claim about the truth values of incompatible propositions requires a further, specifically logical argument. That is a matter for debate, not permission to declare any contradiction true.

**“The hologrammatic principle says that every fragment literally contains everything.”** It is a heuristic notion to be interpreted in context, not a demonstrated universal property.

**“The ecology of action absolves anyone who could not foresee the outcome.”** Responsibility depends on available knowledge, reasonable precautions, possibilities of oversight and the capacity to correct course.

**“Complex thought already provides a theory of today’s AI systems.”** Some contemporary readers apply Morin’s categories to questions about models and interpretation. That does not amount to a technical account of present-day AI architectures.

**“Specialisation is a mistake.”** It has enabled many scientific advances. Trouble arises when specialisation prevents a problem from being understood in context.

## Glossary

**Abstraction:** an operation that isolates features of a phenomenon to make it intelligible; it becomes problematic when it loses sight of the context from which those features were abstracted.

**Auto-eco-organisation:** the relationship between the organisational autonomy of a living system and its dependence on the environment.

**Blindness of knowledge:** the possibility that the selection, organisation and legitimation of knowledge conceal errors and illusions; the subject of the first chapter of *Les Sept Savoirs*.

**Complexity:** the interweaving of interactions, levels, organisation and uncertainty that resists wholly reductive descriptions.

**Knowledge of knowledge:** an investigation into the conditions, capacities, limitations and errors of knowing.

**Context:** the ensemble of circumstances and relationships that lend meaning and relevance to information or a phenomenon.

**Dialogic principle:** a principle holding antagonistic and complementary terms together; it must be distinguished from versions of dialectics oriented towards a synthesis of opposites.

**Ecology of action:** attention to the way an action’s consequences change as it enters an environment of interactions.

**Emergence:** the appearance of properties or behaviours arising from the organisation of a system that cannot be explained merely by listing its isolated parts.

**Hologrammatic:** Morin’s principle inviting inquiry into how the whole is implicated in its parts and the parts in the whole, without assuming literal equivalence.

**Organisation:** the configuration of relationships that structures a system and conditions the behaviour of its parts.

**Organisational recursivity:** a process in which effects contribute to producing or regenerating their own conditions; distinct from a merely regulatory feedback loop.

**Reductionism:** a methodological or philosophical approach explaining a phenomenon in terms of more elementary factors; it becomes insufficient when it eliminates relationships necessary to the explanation.

**Pertinent knowledge:** knowledge able to place particular information and competencies within the problems and contexts to which they belong.

**Paradigm of simplification:** a way of organising knowledge through separations and reductions that can become blind to essential relationships.

**Feedback:** a circuit in which an effect acts upon the process that generated it, stabilising or amplifying that process.

**Reintroduction of the knowing subject:** the principle that the historical and cognitive conditions of the person producing knowledge must themselves be examined.

**Programme and strategy:** a programme assumes sufficiently defined conditions; a strategy adapts as action encounters the unexpected.

## Study and reading paths

**To enter his thought.** Begin with *Introduction à la pensée complexe*, then read *La Tête bien faite*. The first helps clarify the principles; the second explains why Morin considers the reorganisation of education necessary.

**For deeper philosophical study.** Read *La Méthode*, especially the first and third volumes, alongside *Science avec conscience*. To assess generalisations, compare concepts with the scientific sources Morin draws upon.

**To encounter the researcher.** Read *L’Homme et la Mort* and *Le Cinéma ou l’Homme imaginaire*, then watch *Chronique d’un été*. Complexity emerges here as a problem born partly from inquiries into life, images and society.

**For civic responsibility.** Read *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur*, *Terre-Patrie* and *Éthique*, distinguishing cultural diagnosis, educational proposal and moral argument.

**For a critical reading.** Set *La Méthode* beside Reynoso (2009), Blay and Piqueira (2024), Delorme (2013) and Arpaia’s interpretive study (2021). They approach Morin for different reasons: direct criticism, scrutiny of scientific sources, comparison of paradigms and philosophical reconstruction. For readers working in Italian, *La testa ben fatta* and *I sette saperi necessari all’educazione del futuro* offer accessible entrances to the educational question.

## Questions to guide the reader

1. Where does necessary simplification end and a reduction that erases the phenomenon begin?
2. How does the observer participate in the construction of knowledge without making truth arbitrary?
3. When is a relationship between disciplines justified by evidence, and when is it merely an analogy?
4. What distinguishes an action with unforeseen effects from an action that has been irresponsibly left unchecked?
5. What institutions make it possible to contest and correct a collective decision?
6. How can we teach people to make connections without expecting everyone to know everything?

## Points to remember

- Knowledge needs both distinctions and reconnections.
- An explanation is more accountable when it understands its own boundaries.
- Morin’s life encompasses the Resistance, self-criticism, social research and epistemological inquiry.
- *La Méthode* is a six-volume project, not a formula to apply mechanically.
- The dialogic principle, recursivity and the hologrammatic principle are conceptual tools that require definition and limits.
- Knowledge of knowledge examines how error is produced within inquiry itself.
- The ecology of action concerns consequences that exceed intentions.
- Morin’s importance does not establish the validity of every scientific analogy.
- Complexity cannot become an excuse for abandoning clarity and responsibility.

## Frequently asked questions

### 1. Who was Edgar Morin?

Edgar Morin (1921–2026) was a French sociologist, philosopher and interdisciplinary thinker. After his involvement in the Resistance and his research into cinema and society, he developed an epistemological project known as complex thought, elaborated especially in the six volumes of *La Méthode*.

### 2. What does complex thought mean for Morin?

It means understanding phenomena through distinctions, relationships, organisation, context and uncertainty, while recognising that an explanation can isolate important factors and still overlook essential interdependencies.

### 3. What is the difference between complexity and complication?

A complex phenomenon may involve interactions and levels of organisation that cannot be reduced to a simple sum. A complicated procedure, by contrast, may merely be unnecessarily difficult. The difficulty of a description does not, by itself, prove the complexity of what it describes.

### 4. How many volumes are there in *La Méthode*?

The canonical series comprises six volumes published by Seuil between 1977 and 2004: *La Nature de la nature*, *La Vie de la vie*, *La Connaissance de la connaissance*, *Les Idées*, *L’Humanité de l’humanité* and *Éthique*.

### 5. What are the dialogic principle, recursivity and the hologrammatic principle?

The dialogic principle examines terms that are both complementary and antagonistic. Recursivity concerns processes in which effects help produce the conditions of their own emergence. The hologrammatic principle proposes thinking about parts and wholes without reducing either to the other. Each requires careful application; none is a universal scientific law.

### 6. What does “knowledge of knowledge” mean?

It means examining how perceptions, ideas, theories and explanations are produced and tested, the instruments they use, and where errors or illusions may arise. Morin also investigates the paradigms that select what becomes visible and the subject who knows: this is the central problem of the third volume of *La Méthode*.

### 7. Why does Morin criticise the fragmentation of knowledge?

Because some problems require connecting knowledge produced in different disciplines. He does not deny the value of specialisation. His criticism concerns the point at which disciplinary boundaries prevent a problem from being seen in context.

### 8. What is the ecology of action?

It is the idea that an action, once it enters an environment of interactions, can produce effects different from its initial purposes. This calls for following consequences, seeking new information and revising strategy where necessary.

### 9. What are the main criticisms of Morin’s thought?

They concern the breadth of his project, the precision of some concepts, empirical applicability and the transfer of ideas between disciplines. Carlos Reynoso attacks the paradigm directly; Blay and Piqueira question some of Morin’s readings of cybernetics, systems theory and information; Delorme compares different approaches to modelling. These are distinct positions, not a unanimous consensus.

### 10. Can Morin help us understand artificial intelligence?

His categories can help us examine how data, models, selections and criteria of interpretation are organised. This is a contemporary application that requires argument. It does not mean Morin predicted today’s AI or explained the technical workings of present-day systems.

### 11. What is the relationship between Morin and education?

Morin advocates an education able to recognise error, place knowledge in context, face uncertainty and understand the human condition. He develops these concerns especially in *La Tête bien faite* and *Les Sept Savoirs nécessaires à l’éducation du futur*.

### 12. Was Morin opposed to modern science?

No. He criticised the claim that analytical decomposition alone could exhaust a phenomenon, while recognising the value of scientific knowledge. His proposal concerns reforming the way different bodies of knowledge are organised and related.

## Closing — The limits of our attention

Knowledge can be precise and incomplete. Such is its ordinary condition, and often its strength: to delimit a problem is to make rigorous inquiry possible. The danger comes when we forget that an inquiry has drawn a boundary, and when the limits required by method become limits upon imagination and responsibility.

Morin devoted his life to examining that passage. He recognised the force of ideas and their capacity for blindness, the autonomy of living things and their dependencies, the need for disciplines and the damage done when they cease to communicate. He left no universal key to complexity. What remains is a more difficult task: learning to distinguish without losing what connects, and correcting our interpretations when the world puts them to the test.

The people filmed in *Chronique d’un été* could challenge the images made of them. A responsible explanation leaves room for a comparable challenge: for the evidence, voices and consequences that its original frame did not contain.
