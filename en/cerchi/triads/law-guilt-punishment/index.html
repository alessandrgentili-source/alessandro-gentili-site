Triad 14 — Law, Guilt, Punishment

Sophocles · Michel Foucault · Cesare Beccaria

A question to carry through the three guides: Who gets to decide when an act amounts to guilt, and how far may the right to punish extend?

SophoclesMichel FoucaultCesare Beccaria

In this reading

Wounds in the PresentWhere the Question Returns in the EssaysThe Limit That Must HoldRead the three guides

A handful of earth has been scattered over Polyneices’ body. The guards sweep away the traces of the burial rite; Antigone returns and performs it again. Brought before Creon, she admits that she knew his prohibition and chose to defy it. No one disputes what she did. What is contested is the authority of the decree itself: the ruler of a city emerging from civil war claims the power to decide which obligations survive death.

In Oedipus the King, the difficulty takes another form. Oedipus conducts an inquiry that will eventually lead back to himself, unaware that he is the man he seeks. In Oedipus at Colonus, he insists on the distance between what he did and what he could have known when he acted. Sophocles locates the wound in judging itself: an action can be reconstructed without settling how responsibility should be assigned. The power to accuse can outrun the knowledge it needs.

At Mettray, a nineteenth-century French penal colony examined by Foucault, the boys’ days are ordered by work, schooling, surveillance and drill. The decision that brought them there tells us little about what will happen inside. Registers and observations accumulate into a continuing account of conduct. A person becomes a case to be corrected, and judgement continues long after the sentence has been passed. Authority enters the ordinary hours of those confined there.

Beccaria wrote in the eighteenth century, well before Foucault, yet he is the final voice in this reading. On what grounds may the state punish? What evidence entitles it to call someone guilty? How much suffering can be justified in the name of protecting society? Law must set intelligible limits to arbitrary power, and punishment must answer for both its purpose and its measure. The irreversibility of death makes the demand more exacting: even a judgement reached according to established rules can be wrong.

The return from the twentieth century to the eighteenth is deliberate. Foucault shows us what happens inside an institution after a sentence has been pronounced; Beccaria makes us ask what could justify the power to pronounce it. His earlier questions have lost none of their force. A legal answer remains indispensable, but it does not settle what happens within the institution. A sentence can be lawful while the practices carried out in its name continue to produce classifications, dependencies and consequences of their own. These three bodies of work do not form a single theory. Read together, they keep open questions that power is often inclined to consider closed.

Wounds in the Present

The first concerns the dead, and those still searching for them. Polyneices is left unburied by the declared will of a ruler. In today’s wars and disappearances, the difficulty may instead lie in identifying remains, establishing what happened, or giving families the information they are owed. The circumstances and legal obligations differ across the centuries. The guide to Sophocles connects them through a civic duty that does not end with death: the fate of a person cannot be administered without regard for those still waiting for news.

The second wound opens when an institution assigns risk before responsibility has been established. The guide to Foucault examines the Dutch SyRI system, designed to flag possible irregularities through the analysis of administrative data. On 5 February 2020, the District Court of The Hague held that the legislation then governing SyRI failed to comply with Article 8(2) of the European Convention on Human Rights. Its interference with private life lacked sufficient justification, given the system’s inadequate transparency and verifiability. A risk flag is no proof of fraud. Yet the distinction may be hard to maintain for someone classified as a risk and affected by the consequences. The practical question remains: how can a person find out what a file says about them, challenge it, and have the record corrected?

Beccaria brings us to a different kind of finality. Capital punishment extinguishes the very time in which a judicial error might come to light. Its contemporary wound concerns the irreparability of the sentence, and the ease with which a public demand for safety can become a demand for greater suffering. Protecting victims is a real duty. That is precisely why facts must be established and the measure of punishment justified before fear determines what response is necessary.

These are distinct problems: respect for the dead, the administrative classification of risk, and the state’s power to punish. Their historical and legal differences matter. What connects them is the need to challenge a decision that claims to know everything that counts.

Where the Question Returns in the Essays

This triad came after essays in which the same unease had already found a voice. In Governi senza firma (Governments Without Signatures, Italian original), power appears through procedures, divided areas of authority and responsibilities that recede from the person affected by their decisions. In Sophocles’ Thebes, everyone knows who gave the order, yet that knowledge cannot prevent catastrophe. The essay examines another difficulty: today even the signature can be hard to find. And when it is found, we must still ask who could have changed the outcome, and why no one did. Foucault helps us trace the steps by which a decision takes shape before appearing as a finished administrative act.

In Il reale in modalità scorrimento (Reality in Scroll Mode, Italian original), sanction takes a less recognisable form. No conviction is needed for someone to become harder to hear, harder to find or less readily believed. The essay follows classifications made by platforms, the workings of reputation and the prospect of becoming irrelevant without knowing the criteria involved. A loss of social visibility is not a punishment imposed under criminal law. That distinction makes turning to Beccaria worthwhile: he asks what safeguards are owed to those whom the state punishes, while the essay describes forms of exclusion in which even recognising a sanction becomes difficult.

Both essays predate these guides. Foucault was already an explicit interlocutor in each of them; the essays name him directly. Sophocles and Beccaria enter this particular conversation more fully through the later guides, rather than being recruited retrospectively to endorse arguments already written. The movement works in both directions. Questions raised in the essays guide our reading of the works; the works now allow those questions to face a more demanding examination. A reading may strengthen an intuition. It may also expose its weakness.

The Limit That Must Hold

A community has reasons to protect itself, and none of these three authors allows us to dismiss them lightly. Sophocles’ city has endured civil war. The institutions studied by Foucault claim to correct conduct. Beccaria’s criminal law must protect common life against offences. The question is what each form of authority considers itself entitled to do in the name of that protection.

When the urgency to punish cuts short the time allowed for listening, what evidence supports the judgement? Which errors can still be corrected? Who has the power to reconsider a decision, and how can the person affected reach them? One way to measure the limits of authority is to ask whether someone affected by its decision can put forward a reason the institution failed to foresee—and have that reason heard.
