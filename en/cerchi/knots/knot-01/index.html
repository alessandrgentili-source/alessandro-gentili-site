The absent interlocutor

Two people quarrel. On returning home, one entrusts the account of what happened to an artificial intelligence. They reconstruct the events, repeat some words and leave others out. The machine gathers the material, gives it order and returns an interpretation.

The other person, meanwhile, remains outside the room.

In March 2026, a study by Myra Cheng and colleagues, published in Science, examined the effects of sycophancy in conversational systems on interpersonal disputes. Across three experiments involving 2,405 participants, the researchers found that even a single interaction with a sycophantic system can strengthen people's conviction that they are in the right and reduce their stated willingness to take responsibility and repair the conflict.

The representation has acquired authority. Only part of the reality from which it arose has found its way into it.

The part and the world

In 1948 Claude Shannon devised a theory whose extraordinary fertility arose from a renunciation: setting meaning aside from the mathematical problem of transmitting information. The boundary makes precision possible.

It is one of science's great lessons: knowing what to leave out.

Edgar Morin asks what becomes of what we have excluded. Specialist knowledge can describe a part of the world accurately while losing sight of the relations that give it meaning. The difficulty begins when the boundaries of a body of knowledge become the boundaries of our attention.

Let us return to the two people. The machine has access to the words of only one of them, yet the apparent completeness of its answer may make us forget the incompleteness of the account.

The absent interlocutor thus becomes the blind spot of an eminently legible representation.

The formation of habit

Daniel Kahneman's studies help us understand the distance between the confidence with which we make a judgement and its actual validity. The ease with which we take in an explanation can become part of its persuasive force. Repetition raises a further problem: what remains of one answer in the judgement that follows.

In 2025 Moshe Glickman and Tali Sharot reported experimental evidence that repeated interaction with biased algorithmic systems can alter people's subsequent judgements, even before the machine offers them another indication.

The earlier representation takes part in shaping the next judgement. Its effect outlasts the individual answer.

Yet another finding is equally important: in a visual perception experiment within the same study, interaction with an accurate algorithm improved the accuracy of participants' independent judgements. Mediation can teach us.

We therefore need to understand what our habits are teaching us, which faculties we exercise and which we gradually leave idle.

The freedom to be contradicted

Michel Foucault investigated the ways in which individuals become objects of knowledge and, at the same time, subjects of a truth about themselves.

Our own time introduces a further possibility: we can seek that truth voluntarily, entrusting a system with the task of interpreting us. We can even direct its work, amend its language, reject its answers and begin again until we obtain a representation in which we wish to recognise ourselves.

Control of the instrument can coexist here with a closing of the mind to correction.

In The Power of Reading Signs we wrote (translated from the Italian):

The machine does not replace us when it surpasses us.

It replaces us when we cease to correct it.

Today that formulation seems insufficient. We can retain the ability to correct the machine while losing the willingness to be corrected by what we have withheld from it, by what we do not know, by the reasons of those who contradict us.

In The Cost of Complexity we identified the desire for confirmation as one of the disquieting tendencies of the present. These studies help us sharpen the problem: easing a difficulty can increase our capacities; systematically avoiding the encounter with other views may deprive us of the experience through which those capacities mature.

The filigree

A representation exceeds its validity when we entrust it with conclusions that its premises do not warrant. Habit and the difficulty of verification can favour this passage.

Our hypothesis is that, in the interactions through which we seek to understand ourselves, this excess may affect something deeper: our willingness to recognise the part of reality that resists our interpretation.

The studies observe some of the mechanisms involved in this transformation. Its bearing on the formation of human identity remains to be understood.

Of the two people who quarrelled, one now possesses a more orderly account of their own reasons.

The other is still waiting to be heard.

    Sources and reading note

    The opening scene is illustrative, not a transcript of an individual case. The studies cited document effects under specific experimental conditions; the hypothesis about the formation of identity and the willingness to be contradicted remains an interpretation proposed by Knots.
