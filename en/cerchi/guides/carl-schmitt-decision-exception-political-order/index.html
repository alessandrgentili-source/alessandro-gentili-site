Introduction — When the Rule Is No Longer Enough

Who decides when the rule is no longer enough—and what happens to law when that decision claims to establish order?

Every political order rests upon a tacit confidence that rules will continue to hold even when people come into conflict.

Constitutions distribute powers; laws establish obligations and guarantees; institutions transform competing interests into decisions that can be recognised as legitimate. The stability of law allows collective life to proceed without constantly having to question the foundations of authority.

History, however, knows circumstances in which this continuity breaks down. Revolution may overturn institutions. War may endanger the security of the state. A constitutional crisis may cast doubt even upon the authority responsible for applying the rules.

Carl Schmitt stands at this threshold.

A jurist of twentieth-century Germany, an interpreter of Weimar's crisis, and a theorist of sovereignty, Schmitt identified in the exceptional situation a problem that reaches into the very structure of political order: when rules cannot fully determine the response to a crisis, who has the authority to decide?

The question runs through his thinking on dictatorship, the constitution, parliamentarism, and the distinction between friend and enemy. It ultimately extends to international law, territory, and the changing character of war.

Schmitt understood that law presupposes political and social conditions it cannot produce by itself. Yet in seeking the foundations of order, he gave decision and political unity a centrality capable of coming into conflict with pluralism and the guarantees of constitutional government.

His own biography makes this tension impossible to set aside.

In 1933 he joined National Socialism. He helped provide legal legitimacy for Hitler's regime, publicly justified state violence, and participated in antisemitic initiatives. These actions belong to the history of his work and require us to examine the relationship between the power of theoretical concepts and the political uses their author made of them.

Schmitt remains worth studying because some of his questions touch enduring difficulties of institutional life. He remains a thinker who must be subjected to criticism because the pursuit of order can become a justification for concentrating power and excluding others.

What he leaves us, then, is a question about a decisive political threshold: the moment when the need to protect a community risks becoming a claim to decide without remaining answerable to the rules that community ought to preserve.

Carl Schmitt at a Glance

Carl Schmitt (1888–1985) was a German jurist and political theorist, a leading figure in twentieth-century disputes over sovereignty, constitutions, public law, and the nature of the political. His thought developed amid the crisis of the European state and, above all, the Weimar Republic.

In Teologia politica he defined sovereignty through the decision on the state of exception. In Il concetto del politico he identified the friend–enemy distinction as the specific criterion of the political. He also studied dictatorship, representation, legality, parliamentarism, and, in later life, the relationship between legal order and space.

He joined the National Socialist Party in May 1933. He helped legitimise the regime in legal terms, justified Hitler's violent action in 1934, and promoted antisemitic initiatives. His subsequent loss of influence within the regime did not amount to opposition to Nazism.

After 1945, excluded from the established university system, he continued writing from Plettenberg. His reception has been wide-ranging and profoundly contentious: categories he developed are studied even by scholars opposed to his political conclusions.

Why Schmitt Matters

Law regulates power and makes coexistence predictable. Yet its operation presupposes institutions capable of applying it and circumstances in which those institutions can function. Schmitt exposes this presupposition, particularly when a crisis reveals its fragility.

His importance lies first in making a purely procedural account of political order difficult to sustain. A constitution may specify who holds a particular competence; public life nevertheless continues to depend on decisions, recognition, conflicts, and social conditions that no body of rules can exhaust.

A second contribution concerns the specificity of the political. Schmitt argues that conflict can become so intense as to divide collectivities into public friends and enemies. The theory compels us to confront extreme forms of antagonism, although it risks giving them a privileged status that obscures mediation, cooperation, and plurality.

A third element concerns the crisis of representation. Schmitt observed the weakening of parliamentary deliberation, the growing power of mass organisations, and the vulnerability of procedures to forces seeking to transform the order itself.

The importance of these problems does not establish the validity of his solutions. Diagnosing institutional fragility does not require accepting the primacy of authority beyond scrutiny. Nor does recognising the possibility of conflict mean treating it as the whole measure of politics.

This is why different constitutional, democratic, and critical traditions continue to engage with his work. His intellectual influence demands a judgment of his historical responsibility instead of softening it.

The Human Problem — Order, Fear, and Authority

Human beings need predictability. Rules allow us to plan our lives without depending on the shifting will of those in office; they constrain arbitrary power and make coexistence possible among people who need not share ideas, interests, or values.

Crisis disrupts this relationship. When institutional continuity seems threatened, the demand for protection may become more urgent than the demand for safeguards. A community asks someone to act, sometimes before asking by what authority and within what limits.

Schmitt understands the political force of this demand. But the human problem lies also in the temptation it creates: to take speed of decision as sufficient proof of legitimacy and interpret every check as an obstacle to collective salvation.

Those governing may claim to protect the order; dissenters may demand that the order remain faithful to its rules. These positions do not automatically carry equal merit in every circumstance. The difficulty lies in finding public standards by which to assess them, rather than allowing those in power to be the sole judges of their own conduct.

The deepest question is not whether crises occur. It is what a community is prepared to grant to those who promise to resolve them—and what it must preserve, even in danger, if protection is not to become domination.

Essential Biography

Schmitt was born in Plettenberg, Westphalia, into a Catholic family. He studied law in Berlin, Munich, and Strasbourg, where he earned his doctorate in 1910, and completed his habilitation in 1916. The First World War and the collapse of the German Empire coincided with the beginning of his intellectual career.

During the Weimar Republic he taught at several universities, including Greifswald, Bonn, Berlin, and Cologne. His early postwar writings addressed authority, dictatorship, and the political foundations of legal order. From the late 1920s to 1932 he increasingly concentrated on the crisis of the constitutional and parliamentary system.

After Hitler came to power, Schmitt joined the NSDAP on 1 May 1933 and took positions in the new legal establishment. In 1934 he publicly defended the killings carried out by the regime during the so-called Night of the Long Knives. In 1936 he promoted openly antisemitic initiatives in legal scholarship.

In December 1936 the SS weekly Das Schwarze Korps publicly attacked him. He lost the editorship of the Deutsche Juristen-Zeitung and his leadership role among university teachers in the National Socialist jurists' organisation; he retained his professorship and the title of Prussian State Councillor. His marginalisation resulted from conflicts within the regime, not from an anti-Nazi break.

After 1945 he was detained and questioned by the Allies; in 1947 he was interrogated in the context of the Nuremberg proceedings without being indicted in the main trial. He did not return to a normal academic career. From Plettenberg he published Il nomos della terra (1950) and Teoria del partigiano (1963), maintaining an extensive network of correspondents and interlocutors. He died in 1985.

The Life That Illuminates the Work — Three Ruptures

1918: The End of Imperial Continuity

Defeat in the First World War, revolution, and the creation of the Weimar Republic showed how quickly an order could lose its institutions and sources of legitimacy. This is the setting in which La dittatura and Teologia politica took shape: the question of decision arose from a radical transformation of the state, not from an abstract exercise.

1933: Collaboration with the New Power

The second turning point concerns Schmitt himself. His earlier critiques of parliamentarism and pluralism should not be treated as mechanical causes of his adherence to Nazism. His collaboration nevertheless shows how previously developed categories can be employed, adapted, and radicalised to legitimise a dictatorship. Intellectual responsibility exists here alongside political responsibility.

1945: Catastrophe and the Rewriting of the Self

After the war, his attention moved towards international order, territory, and the transformation of warfare. This thematic shift was not accompanied by a documented moral reckoning. Autobiographical writings and correspondence must be read by distinguishing testimony, self-defence, and historical reconstruction. The question of order survives, but the circumstances in which it is asked have changed radically.

Weimar — The Crisis of Constitutional Order

The constitution of 1919 established the German Republic and a complex distribution of authority among parliament, government, and president. Article 48 allowed the Reich president, under specified conditions, to adopt extraordinary measures to restore security and public order, while also providing for forms of Reichstag oversight.

Economic crisis, political radicalisation, and increasing difficulty in forming parliamentary governments led, especially after 1930, to more frequent reliance on presidential powers. Procedures remained in place, but their balance was profoundly altered.

Schmitt read the crisis as evidence of the party system's inability to express a sufficiently strong political unity. At different stages and in different contexts, he advocated defending the constitutional order through the Reich president. To present oneself as guardian of the constitution, however, is no guarantee that one's proposed solutions will preserve its democratic pluralism.

One episode shows how concrete these questions had become. In July 1932 the Papen government effectively dismissed the Prussian government in the Preußenschlag, relying on emergency powers. In Preußen contra Reich, the case before the Staatsgerichtshof, Schmitt represented the Reich, while the Prussian government contested the intervention. The dispute over the legality of presidential action thus unfolded in a courtroom as well.

The matter remains historically contentious. We must distinguish what Schmitt claimed he intended to protect, the legal powers he privileged, and the potentially authoritarian consequences of his arguments. Weimar's crisis did not offer only a choice between the impotence of rules and the personal concentration of power. Hans Kelsen and Hermann Heller developed different responses that must be considered before judging Schmitt's outcome necessary.

Disciplinary Circles

Constitutional law. Sovereignty, competences, constituent power, oversight, and the safeguarding of the legal order form the legal core of his thought.

Political philosophy. The friend–enemy distinction and his conception of collective unity probe the relationship between conflict, representation, and pluralism.

Political theology. Analogies between theological concepts and modern categories of the state serve to illuminate the genealogy and structure of authority.

Institutional history. Roman dictatorship, the modern state, the French Revolution, and Weimar are decisive stages in his thinking about extraordinary powers.

Political sociology. Organisation, parties, legitimation, and the masses bring Schmitt into contact with problems also studied by Weber, without making their perspectives identical.

International law and spatial history. In the Nomos, territory, appropriation, frontiers, and mutual recognition among states become conditions of legal order.

War studies. The partisan and the transformations of enmity open a limited dialogue with Clausewitz, distinct from military theories of conflict.

Intellectual history. Bodin, Hobbes, Donoso Cortés, the tradition of public law, and twentieth-century interlocutors place Schmitt within a network of arguments and disputes.

Principal Works — An Intellectual Itinerary, Not a Catalogue

From Form to Decision, 1919–1923

In Romanticismo politico (1919), Schmitt criticises an occasionalism that turns the world into an opportunity for subjective expression while evading binding decisions. La dittatura (1921) distinguishes the temporary defence of an existing order from the foundation of a new one. Teologia politica (1922) centres sovereignty on the question of exception. Cattolicesimo romano e forma politica (1923) emphasises institutional form and representation. The first edition of his work on parliamentarism, also published in 1923, examines the role of public discussion in liberal justifications of parliament.

Constitution and Weimar's Crisis, 1927–1932

Il concetto del politico first appeared as an essay in 1927 and reached its best-known formulation in the book edition of 1932. Dottrina della costituzione (1928) distinguishes the fundamental political decision from individual constitutional rules. Il custode della costituzione (1931) raises the question of guarding the order and fuels the dispute with Kelsen. Legalità e legittimità (1932) explores the possibility that formally valid procedures may threaten the political foundations of the legal order.

National Socialist Complicity, 1933–1936

Stato, movimento, popolo (1933), Il Führer protegge il diritto (1934), and the antisemitic interventions of 1936 are not harmless appendices. They document how Schmitt used his intellectual authority to support and legitimise National Socialism. In Über die drei Arten des rechtswissenschaftlichen Denkens (1934), he distinguished normativism, decisionism, and concrete-order thinking. This argument, which gave renewed emphasis to institutions and social relations, was also used to give juridical form to the regime's order and marks a turning point towards his later reflections on nomos.

Leviathan, Space, and Order, 1938–1950

Il Leviatano nella dottrina dello Stato di Thomas Hobbes (1938) explores representation and the crisis of the state form and contains antisemitic passages that require critical examination. Terra e mare (1942) offers a historical-spatial reading of power relations. Il nomos della terra (1950) links international order with appropriation and the concrete organisation of space, offering a problematic perspective on Europe and limited war.

Enmity in the Age of Irregular Warfare, 1963

Teoria del partigiano (1963) returns to the political through the irregular fighter, territorial attachment, and the transformation of the enemy. It is the most carefully defined bridge towards Clausewitz, not a replacement for his theory of war.

The Threshold of Order — Norm, Normality, Decision

Original Module I · Cerchi d'inchiostro

A legal rule governs a class of circumstances. It assigns competences, establishes conditions, and prescribes consequences. Its application nevertheless requires a world in which those conditions can be recognised and institutions retain the ability to operate.

In this relationship between norm and normality, Schmitt identifies a limit to approaches that seek to understand an entire legal order through normative structure alone. The problem becomes particularly clear in extreme situations: what happens when the circumstances presupposed by the rules cease to exist?

Schmitt's answer gives sovereign decision a fundamental role. Someone must be able to determine that ordinary circumstances have been interrupted and decide what measures are needed to confront them.

Here, however, a crucial distinction arises. To acknowledge that law does not automatically determine every aspect of its application is not to prove that a decision may legitimately free itself from legal constraints. It is precisely this transition that makes Schmitt's theory controversial.

The exception exposes a problem within legal order. It does not, by itself, prove that the solution must be entrusted to an unchecked authority. A legal system can regulate emergency powers, competences, and oversight even when circumstances are extraordinary; Schmitt disputes whether such provisions can exhaust the decision on an extreme exception.

The question Cerchi d'inchiostro draws from this tension is therefore broader than the formula concerning sovereignty: what conditions enable a community to take extraordinary decisions without giving the decision-maker the unilateral power to define the limits of law?

Sovereignty and Political Theology

The opening formula of Teologia politica identifies the sovereign as the one who decides on the state of exception. Under ordinary conditions, competences and powers seem regulated; in an extreme case, Schmitt asks who can recognise the rupture and determine the response.

His critique of normativism does not simply claim that law is useless. It insists that applying a rule presupposes a concrete situation and that law cannot itself create all the conditions for its effectiveness.

The term political theology also concerns a historical and structural analogy: according to Schmitt, some concepts of modern state theory retain the form of secularised theological concepts. In his construction, the relationship between exception and regular order recalls that between miracle and natural law. This is a theoretical analogy, not the simple deification of the sovereign.

The force of the question does not remove the difficulty of the answer. If the last word on the exception belongs to whoever exercises power, we must examine the relationship between that authority and the guarantees that should make it answerable. Sovereignty thus becomes a question about the limits of decision rather than a celebration of its effectiveness.

Commissarial and Sovereign Dictatorship

In La dittatura, Schmitt reconstructs the history of extraordinary powers. Commissarial dictatorship receives an exceptional mandate to defend or restore an existing order. The suspension of certain rules is conceived in the service of their future effectiveness: the declared aim remains constitutional continuity.

Sovereign dictatorship, by contrast, looks towards the foundation of a new order. It is connected to constituent power and the claim to create the conditions under which a new constitution can come into being. Merely describing a power as temporary does not make it a genuine defence of the previous constitution.

The distinction reveals a concrete political threshold. An authority may claim to preserve the order while irreversibly transforming its structure. We must examine the function of the measures, the duration of the powers, and the direction of institutional change.

The distinction made in 1921 does not coincide in every detail with the theory of exception formulated in 1922; the relationship between the texts remains a matter of interpretation. Their conceptual relationship retains a complexity that rules out treating Schmitt's thought as an unchanging system from the outset.

The Concept of the Political — Friend and Enemy

Schmitt asks what makes an antagonism specifically political. His answer rests neither on morality nor aesthetics nor economic advantage, but on the intensity of opposition between collectivities capable of recognising one another as public friends and enemies.

The political enemy, the hostis, differs from a private adversary, the inimicus, against whom an individual feels resentment. War is the extreme possibility implicit in this opposition: in its starkest formulation, the political points to the real possibility that human beings may kill and be killed. Jus belli also concerns the power to demand that members of the community risk their lives and use force against a public enemy. Recognising that possibility does not make war a permanent duty or the ordinary content of politics.

A religious, social, or economic conflict may become political if it divides collectivities over a question concerning their existence. Schmitt therefore holds that politics cannot be abolished merely by declaring the fields in which conflict takes place neutral.

Yet his criterion has an evident limitation. If the possibility of extreme enmity defines the political, mediation and cooperation risk appearing secondary. A pluralistic democracy, by contrast, must be able to recognise significant conflict without systematically turning it into existential antagonism. This is the principal tension between Schmitt's recognition of conflict and democracy's defence of the legitimate adversary.

The Power to Name the Enemy

Original Module II · Cerchi d'inchiostro

A community may face a real threat. But confronting it first requires identifying it, describing it, and determining how it relates to collective existence. To designate someone as a public enemy changes the meaning of the conflict.

An adversary can be opposed within shared rules. The figure of the political enemy evokes the possibility of antagonism that reaches into the continuity of the community itself. The passage from one condition to the other cannot be treated as a mere change of tone.

Schmitt analyses the friend–enemy distinction as the criterion of the political. Cerchi d'inchiostro draws a further question from it: who has the power to classify someone as an enemy, and what guarantees prevent that classification from becoming a means of exclusion?

The problem becomes particularly grave when transferred inside the community. Dissent may be represented as disloyalty, pluralism as dissolution, and people considered outside the dominant identity as threats to security. Schmitt's participation in National Socialism obliges us to examine the historical possibility of such slippages without claiming that every formulation before 1933 was their inevitable cause.

The power to define the enemy touches the boundary within which a community recognises belonging, protection, and rights. For that very reason, it must be open to public challenge and assessment by criteria not dependent solely on the will of those exercising it.

Constitution, Legality, and Legitimacy

In Dottrina della costituzione, Schmitt distinguishes the constitution as a fundamental decision about the form and existence of political unity from individual constitutional laws. In Legalità e legittimità, he examines the vulnerability of an order whose procedures may be used by forces bent on changing or destroying its foundations.

The question arises from Weimar's crisis. What happens when an actor gains power through constitutional mechanisms and uses them to make a future transfer of power impossible? Formal regularity does not necessarily settle the question of democratic continuity.

Schmitt's distinction exposes the difficulty but makes its proposed solution controversial. If substantive legitimacy is entrusted to a body empowered to overstep ordinary checks, what prevents the defence of the constitution from becoming the means of its authoritarian transformation?

Later democratic constitutionalism developed other instruments to confront anti-system forces, including the theory of militant democracy. It would be wrong to attribute those solutions retrospectively to Schmitt. A diagnosis of institutional vulnerability can survive even when its original political prescription is rejected.

Legally Against the Order

Original Module III · Cerchi d'inchiostro

Procedures allow a community to change governments without resorting to force. But they may also be used by those who intend to ensure that others can never exercise that same freedom again.

Political forces may seek to transform powers and checks by exploiting steps provided for in the legal order while undermining the conditions of democratic competition. This is the paradox of the possible procedural destruction of safeguards. The German events of 1933 nevertheless require qualification: the decree following the Reichstag fire and the Enabling Act of 24 March (Ermächtigungsgesetz) operated amid violence, intimidation, and repression of the opposition. The Nazi rise to power cannot be described as an ordinary electoral majority followed by the peaceful application of procedures.

During Weimar's crisis, Schmitt identified a problem concerning the vulnerability of legality, but in 1933 he collaborated in legitimising a regime that destroyed constitutional order. Our question, however, is not his solution. Preventing rules from being turned against the constitution requires protective mechanisms; yet if the authority charged with protecting the order can escape every limit, the remedy risks reproducing the harm.

Two vulnerabilities coexist: legality may be exploited against its own foundations; its defence may become the language of concentrated power. The quality of a constitutional response lies in its ability to confront both without sacrificing one in order to conceal the other.

Schmitt, Kelsen, and Heller — Three Responses to Weimar

The dispute between Schmitt and Hans Kelsen concerns the safeguarding of the constitution, but also the circumstances in which an authority may claim to be its guardian.

Kelsen, a theorist of the normative structure of legal order, gives constitutional adjudication a decisive role in reviewing the acts of public authorities. A court is not immune from interpretive and political problems, but for him it offers an institutional form of legal protection. For Kelsen, the constitutional court, even as a 'negative legislator' when it annuls unconstitutional laws, does not abolish the political dimension of law: it organises oversight through defined competences. He disputes the notion that a president can embody a neutrality above the parties, untouched by the tensions of pluralism.

Schmitt rejects the idea that a judicial institution alone can resolve conflicts reaching into political unity. In the Weimar debate, he privileges the Reich president, elected by the people as a whole, as a possible guardian of the constitution against party fragmentation. This raises a problem of neutrality: why should a political institution be more independent of society's divisions than a constitutional judge?

Hermann Heller takes us beyond a portrayal of the conflict as a simple choice between normativism and decisionism. For Heller, law and the state must also be understood through social conditions and concrete relations; that recognition does not entail abolishing democratic pluralism under a personal will above the rules.

The three perspectives differ in their foundations and cannot be merged. Yet their confrontation reveals an important historical result: recognising that law exists within political reality does not compel us to adopt Schmitt's authoritarian solution. Weimar was a laboratory of genuine alternatives, not proof that there could be only one remedy for crisis.

Democracy, Liberalism, and Parliamentarism

Schmitt separates concepts often associated in modern constitutional democracies. Liberalism values individual rights, freedom, and limits on authority; parliamentarism was historically justified through the ideal of public discussion; democracy, for Schmitt, expresses the identity of rulers and ruled founded on the homogeneity of the people.

This understanding of democracy therefore differs from liberal parliamentary democracy. In his conception, the people may directly manifest unity through public approval, acclamation, and plebiscitary procedures. He even regards dictatorial authority recognised through collective identification as compatible with democracy understood in this way. Participation is conceived less as sustained debate among differing actors than as the public manifestation of political unity.

The distinction has a theoretical and historical cost that must be made explicit. In the preface to the second edition of his book on parliamentarism (1926), written amid controversies surrounding the text, Schmitt argues that democracy requires homogeneity and, when necessary, the elimination of heterogeneity. The language is severe even before 1933. At that stage, homogeneity does not yet coincide at every point with National Socialist racial doctrine, but exclusion has an internal place in his conception of democracy. The precise meaning and its relationship to later radicalisation remain matters of historical debate.

Schmitt also sees a crisis of parliamentarism in mass party society: public discussion risks being replaced by negotiation between organisations and interests. His diagnosis registers real changes in representation, but the political solution he privileges weakens pluralist safeguards.

The decisive question thus concerns the boundary of political equality. Who decides which differences a community may recognise without losing its unity? And which authority obtains the power to expel the heterogeneous? Schmitt's later adherence to National Socialism requires us to read these questions alongside his historical responsibility.

Contemporary constitutional democracy has developed a different response: institutions and rights can sustain common action while recognising that disagreement will endure, rather than treating it as a defect to be removed.

Representation and Political Form

In Cattolicesimo romano e forma politica, Schmitt examines institutions' capacity to make a unity present through forms, symbols, and authority. Representation is more than a technical delegation for him: it concerns the possibility that a community can acquire public expression.

Dottrina della costituzione returns to the problem through the principles of identity and representation. The people are conceived as a political unity; institutions help make that presence effective. These principles may combine in different historical forms.

The theory illuminates the symbolic and collective dimension of the state but carries a risk: whoever represents the people may claim to embody them completely. The plurality of those represented can then disappear behind a supposed identity with whoever commands.

Democratic representation, by contrast, depends on the possibility that representatives can be challenged, replaced, and judged by those in whose name they act. The difference reveals the point at which political unity must encounter its limits.

Schmitt and National Socialism — The Historical Test of His Categories

The year 1933 marks a decisive turning point. Schmitt joined the National Socialist Party on 1 May and assumed significant roles in the regime's legal culture, including responsibility for the university teachers' group within the National Socialist jurists' organisation, the editorship of the Deutsche Juristen-Zeitung from 1934, and the title of Prussian State Councillor. Through publications, appointments, and public interventions, he helped legitimise the new political order.

Law Within the Regime's Structure

In Stato, movimento, popolo (1933), Schmitt interpreted the new order by placing the National Socialist movement and its political leader at the centre. The relationship between state and political unity was reformulated within the Führerprinzip, which assigned the leader an authority incompatible with the pluralist guarantees of constitutional government.

The gravest and most representative intervention followed the killings during the so-called Night of the Long Knives, between late June and early July 1934. In Der Führer schützt das Recht, published on 1 August 1934 in the Deutsche Juristen-Zeitung, Schmitt gave a legal justification for the killings ordered by Hitler. He presented the leader's decision as an act of justice and defence of order, supplying juridical cover for political violence carried out without trial. It was a concrete use of intellectual authority to support state violence.

Antisemitism and Intellectual Responsibility

His collaboration included antisemitic initiatives. In 1935, in Die Verfassung der Freiheit, Schmitt supported the Nuremberg racial laws, portraying them in terms of the supposed freedom of the German people. On 3 and 4 October 1936, he organised the Munich conference Das Judentum in der Rechtswissenschaft, aimed at excluding Jewish authors and persecuting their intellectual legacy in German legal scholarship. This was not a debate between equal academic participants; it was an exercise in antisemitic propaganda and discrimination. Discrimination was not merely an external setting for his career: it found expression in professional and public acts and positions.

In his 1938 book on Hobbes, antisemitism also shapes the interpretation of the crisis of Leviathan and cannot be dismissed as a handful of marginal passages. Postwar documentation, including diaries and notes, further requires caution towards any narrative of complete moral separation from antisemitism.

Marginalisation in 1936

In late 1936, attacks from SS circles questioned Schmitt's reliability, referring also to his earlier positions and associations. He left the journal's editorship and the leadership of the university group of National Socialist jurists, but kept his chair and title of State Councillor. These conflicts belong to the internal history of National Socialism. They do not amount to resistance against the regime.

Continuity, Radicalisation, Transformation

The relationship between his pre-1933 thought and his Nazi collaboration is disputed. One interpretive strand emphasises continuities: his critique of pluralism, centrality of decision, demand for homogeneity, and distrust of parliamentary legality. Another stresses the theoretical transformations produced by his adherence, particularly the place of the movement, the Führer, and völkisch and racial criteria.

The two interpretations illuminate different aspects of the story, but neither resolves all its difficulties. It would be unjustified to infer Nazism as the inevitable outcome of every earlier formulation. It would be equally unjustified to treat 1933 as an interlude unrelated to his theoretical work. Schmitt himself helped transfer and reformulate his categories within the regime.

The Postwar Period and Self-Defence

After the war, Schmitt was questioned by the Allies and did not return to university teaching. In autobiographical writing and postwar statements, he sought to reinterpret his own role. These strategies of self-defence must be measured against documented acts; the absence of criminal indictment must not be mistaken for historical or intellectual exoneration.

The judgment of his collaboration is clear: Schmitt helped legitimise a persecutory and antisemitic dictatorship. The further critical question does not seek to soften that responsibility. It asks how sophisticated theoretical categories could be used by their own author to support the power that destroyed Weimar's constitutional order.

Hobbes and Leviathan

Hobbes is an essential reference for the relationship between fear, protection, and sovereignty. His problem is to understand how people may escape civil war through a common authority capable of guaranteeing security. Obedience and protection meet in the institution of the state. Schmitt emphasises this relationship through the Latin formula protego ergo obligo: political command claims obedience because it promises protection.

Schmitt recognises Hobbes as one of the founders of political modernity and devotes Il Leviatano nella dottrina dello Stato di Thomas Hobbes to him in 1938. His interpretation focuses on the fate of state unity, the symbol of Leviathan, and tensions within representation.

The differences are fundamental. Hobbes builds a theory of the conditions of civil order; Schmitt insists on sovereign decision and the extreme possibility of antagonism. Reading one solely through the other risks obscuring their different projects.

For Cerchi d'inchiostro, the bridge is especially significant. Hobbes translated Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War into English, publishing it in 1629. The sequence Thucydides → Hobbes → Schmitt connects, without conflating them, the crisis of the ancient city, the modern foundation of the state, and the twentieth-century crisis of sovereignty. Hobbes remains an intersection within the constellation, not the triad's fourth author.

Where Does Law Begin? Nomos, Land, and Sea

Original Module IV · Cerchi d'inchiostro

Before the Nomos, the doctrine of Großraum, articulated in 1939 partly through reference to the Monroe Doctrine, had envisaged large spaces ordered by hegemonic powers. Within National Socialist Germany, this approach could help legitimise German imperial claims; it would be misleading to read it as a neutral geographical theory.

In Il nomos della terra (1950), Schmitt examines the spatial dimension of order. Law is not merely a set of rules: it presupposes territories, appropriations, divisions, boundaries, and concrete forms of coexistence. Nomos comes to mean spatial ordering, not simply an abstract rule.

Schmitt's reconstruction of the jus publicum Europaeum emphasises mutual recognition among sovereign states and the historical ability to limit wars between European powers. This diagnosis must be distinguished from idealisation: the limitation of certain interstate conflicts coexisted with colonial wars, appropriations, and violence outside the European space.

Terra e mare (1942) had already organised a reflection on changing relations between land and sea powers. A comparison with Athens and Sparta in Thucydides may suggest questions about space and force, but it does not establish a direct genealogy: the Greek polis and the modern world order are different structures.

The question that emerges from this reading concerns the foundation of order: to what extent does law arise and operate within a concrete spatial arrangement, and what criteria allow us to judge that arrangement rather than taking its mere existence as sufficient justification?

Schmitt forces us to recognise the significance of territory in legal history. Criticism must prevent appropriation and conquest from automatically becoming titles of legitimacy. An order can be concrete and unjust at the same time.

The Partisan and the Transformation of the Enemy

In Teoria del partigiano (1963), Schmitt studies an irregular fighter characterised, in the classical model, by mobility, political commitment, and attachment to territory. War no longer takes place solely between recognisable state military forces; enmity becomes entangled with resistance movements, revolutions, and ideological transformations.

Schmitt distinguishes the real enemy from the absolute enemy and examines the radicalisation of struggle when an adversary is presented as someone to be annihilated in the name of a general principle. Yet his reconstruction must not lead us to idealise European state wars as universally restrained: the earlier order also knew forms of radical violence, especially in colonial territories.

The partisan marks a change in the political context of war. He does not yet provide a complete theory of strategy, friction, or escalation. That is where the reading of Clausewitz begins, without anticipating it.

The Transition to Clausewitz — Conflict, Decision, Force

Thucydides describes the crisis of judgment and speech when conflict penetrates the life of the city. Schmitt examines the foundations of political unity and the criterion of public enmity. Clausewitz will study the use of military force in relation to political purposes.

The three positions do not form a single theory handed down without interruption. They belong to different periods and address different problems: the polis and the Peloponnesian War, the modern state and constitutional crisis, and the wars of the revolutionary and Napoleonic eras.

The editorial tension arises from their different points of focus: what happens to judgment in conflict? Who decides upon order and the enemy? How is a war conducted and limited in relation to a political purpose?

The friend–enemy distinction does not in itself determine the means, intensity, or duration of a war. Precisely what remains unexplained opens the third guide. The triad does not celebrate conflict: it tests politics' capacity to recognise it and assume responsibility for decisions and the limits of force.

Key Ideas

Law presupposes normality. Rules need concrete conditions in order to apply; Schmitt focuses on the moment those conditions collapse.

The exception exposes the problem of authority. Those who recognise a crisis and decide on extraordinary intervention acquire a decisive role. Recognising this does not automatically justify an absence of limits.

The political reaches an extreme threshold in public enmity. The friend–enemy distinction concerns collectivities rather than personal resentments; treating it as the privileged criterion for all political experience remains controversial.

The constitution has a political dimension. Schmitt distinguishes the fundamental decision about the form of political unity from individual constitutional provisions.

Legality can be turned against its own foundations. Procedures may be used by forces hostile to the order; defending that order must nevertheless not abolish constitutional safeguards.

Representation gives unity a form. But no one can presume to embody a genuinely plural community without remainder.

Law has a spatial history. Frontiers and appropriations shape legal orders and require criticism of colonial hierarchies.

Schmitt's biography is a critical test. His collaboration with National Socialism obliges us to examine categories, theoretical transformation, and political responsibility together.

Key Concepts

Sovereignty. The ultimate authority that Schmitt, in his 1922 theory, identifies through the decision on the exception; a contested idea because of its relationship to legal limits.

Decision. An act determining a concrete political situation; it carries different weight in his decisionist period and his later thinking about order.

Decisionism. A view emphasising that authority and the decisive act cannot be reduced to mere deduction from abstract rules.

State of exception. An extreme situation in which the normality presupposed by law's application seems compromised; it is not automatically synonymous with every legally regulated emergency.

Norm and normality. The former prescribes; the latter designates the concrete conditions under which prescriptions can operate.

Political theology. Inquiry into analogies and genealogies connecting theological concepts with modern state categories.

The political. For Schmitt, a dimension identified through the extreme possibility of distinguishing public friends and enemies.

Public enemy (hostis). An opposing collectivity within political antagonism, distinct from personal enmity and private hatred.

Commissarial dictatorship. Extraordinary power directed towards preserving or restoring an existing order.

Sovereign dictatorship. Extraordinary power directed towards constituting a new order.

Constituent power. The political capacity to give form to a new constitution.

Constitution. In Schmitt's positive conception, a fundamental decision about the form of political unity, distinct from individual constitutional laws.

Legality and legitimacy. The former concerns conformity to procedures; for Schmitt the latter invokes the political foundations of the order.

Representation. The form through which political unity acquires public and institutional presence; distinct from mere delegation.

Identity and homogeneity. Categories Schmitt connects with democracy, carrying risks of exclusion and anti-pluralism.

Neutralisation and depoliticisation. Ways of presenting particular spheres as capable of absorbing or removing conflicts that may re-emerge politically.

Nomos. A concrete spatial order associated with the appropriation, distribution, and organisation of land.

Greater space (Großraum). A geopolitical-legal category for ordering extensive territories, historically controversial also because of its use during the Nazi period.

Partisan. An irregular combatant studied through the relationship between territory, political commitment, and changing forms of enmity.

Absolute enemy. A radicalised form of enmity in which the limits of political recognition of an adversary are weakened.

Constellation — Genealogies, Oppositions, Receptions

Before Schmitt

Jean Bodin — an antecedent of sovereignty. The modern definition of supreme power is one of the grounds on which Schmitt reframes the problem of authority.

Niccolò Machiavelli — an affinity of problems. Foundation, necessity, and the preservation of political orders invite comparison without authorising a theoretical identification.

Thomas Hobbes — a documented primary interlocutor. Protection, obedience, fear, and representation connect the modern construction of the state with Schmitt's reading of Leviathan.

Donoso Cortés — an explicit reference. The anti-liberal critique of discussion and the theme of decision enter the genealogy of political theology.

Political Catholicism and German public-law scholarship — formative contexts. Form, representation, and public law generate distinct questions.

Thucydides — an indirect editorial connection. Hobbes's translation creates a historical bridge but proves no direct theoretical lineage from Thucydides to Schmitt.

Contemporaries, Interlocutors, and Opponents

Hans Kelsen — a direct theoretical opponent. The controversy over the guardian of the constitution illuminates the limits of decision and normative guarantees.

Hermann Heller — a Weimar counterpoint. The social reality of the state and the democratic defence of pluralism offer an alternative to decisionism.

Max Weber — an intellectual context. Legitimacy, the state, and rationalisation are shared problems, not equivalent solutions.

Walter Benjamin — a contested constellation. Sovereignty, violence, and exception invite interpretive comparisons that must be distinguished from simplistic genealogies.

Leo Strauss — a critic of Il concetto del politico. His reading questions the relationship between the critique of liberalism and the foundations of the political criterion.

Ernst Jünger — a milieu and interlocutor. War, technology, and the transformations of modernity provide grounds for both proximity and disagreement.

National Socialism — collaboration and responsibility. This is not a contextual label to be placed on the same footing as academic debates: it was the regime Schmitt joined and helped legitimise.

After Schmitt

Hannah Arendt — an interpretive counterfigure. Plurality and public action offer an alternative to the primacy of existential antagonism; she should not be presented simply as a disciple or direct opponent.

Reinhart Koselleck — intellectual relationship and reception. Conceptual history and analysis of modern crises developed partly against the background of a documented relationship with Schmitt.

Jacques Derrida — a critical rereading. Friendship and politics become the occasion for a deconstruction of Schmitt's categories.

Giorgio Agamben — critical reworking. The state of exception takes on a different theoretical configuration: comparison is not identity.

Chantal Mouffe — selective appropriation. Recognition of conflict is transformed into an agonistic theory in which the adversary remains legitimate.

Jan-Werner Müller — a history of reception. Schmitt's postwar thought is studied alongside its persistent political problems.

Contemporary Phenomena Open to Examination

Constitutional emergencies, concentration of executive power, crises of parliamentary mediation, militant democracy, the political construction of enemies, pluralism, borders, and changes in international order. These are not phenomena 'predicted' by Schmitt: they are problems about which certain categories of his may help us ask questions, alongside critical tools developed outside his work.

Legacy and Reception

Schmitt continues to be read because sovereignty, order, conflict, and the limits of law remain open questions. His concepts circulate through jurisprudence, political theory, philosophy, and international relations. Such wide-ranging influence does not imply shared acceptance of his solutions.

In constitutional law, his work provokes debate about norm, decision, and the defence of the constitution. In political theory, the friend–enemy criterion is criticised, adopted, or transformed in attempts to think through antagonism and pluralism. In international relations, the Nomos continues to shape discussions of spatial order, borders, and war.

His reception also includes selective appropriations within radical circles of different political orientations. Analytical use, polemical recovery, and normative endorsement must be distinguished. Schmitt's presence in contemporary debate does not absolve his collaboration with National Socialism; it makes reconstruction of the relationship between categories and political consequences more necessary.

The Contemporary Wound — When Exception Claims to Become Order

An editorial reading by Cerchi d'inchiostro

Schmitt also attributes to the sovereign the ability to judge whether the normal conditions for applying rules are present. Cerchi d'inchiostro takes this thesis seriously in order to reverse its normative perspective: it is precisely this power of classification that a constitutional order must make subject to scrutiny and limits. Our question about the present arises here, separate from any endorsement of Schmitt's solutions.

Every political community must be able to confront circumstances that threaten its continuity. A constitution incapable of governing a crisis risks losing its effectiveness precisely when its guarantees become most necessary.

Yet the exercise of extraordinary powers raises a problem not exhausted by the need for intervention. Who determines that the situation is exceptional? Who verifies that the measures adopted are necessary? Who decides when ordinary conditions can be restored?

Schmitt's thought makes the centrality of decision visible. His political history shows the danger of entrusting the definition of order to a power claiming to encounter no limits.

This tension reveals an especially important question for constitutional democracies. A crisis may require temporary changes in the ordinary exercise of particular powers. Yet the protection of the community must remain distinguishable from a transformation of the power that governs it.

This distinction calls for reviewable procedures, judicial safeguards, temporal limits, and public accountability.

The matter grows more serious when the authority declaring the emergency also claims the power to identify those who threaten political unity. An adversary may be represented as an enemy. Dissent as danger. Challenges to power as obstacles to collective survival.

Exception then becomes a place where what is at stake is not only extraordinary decisions but also the meaning of political belonging and the boundaries of legal protection.

The problem begins when those who declare a crisis also acquire the power to decide what normality must follow it.

This is a question we can put to the present through Schmitt, while keeping our interpretation distinct from his historical positions. It does not imply that every emergency conceals an authoritarian drift. It asks us to recognise that a community can be protected only if, at the moment of crisis, it also preserves the ability to judge and limit the power exercised in its name.

Limits and Contradictions

From the problem of rules to the primacy of decision. Recognising that law requires conditions for its application does not establish that a sovereign decision must escape legal guarantees. The leap remains controversial.

Pluralism and unity. Schmitt tends to regard fragmentation as a threat of state dissolution. Democracy, however, must make differences compatible with the continuity of legal order without demanding a uniform political identity.

Homogeneity and exclusion. Schmitt's theory of democracy raises the question of who is recognised as part of the people. The racial and persecutory use of belonging under National Socialism makes this historically unavoidable.

Description and prescription. The friend–enemy criterion claims to identify what is distinctive about the political, but giving extreme antagonism theoretical prominence may also reinforce its normative role.

Parliamentarism. A diagnosis of declining deliberation does not justify automatically devaluing compromise, rights, or checks on majorities.

Nomos and colonialism. Reconstructing European order risks idealising its restraint of war unless conquest, subordination, and violence outside Europe are also considered.

National Socialism and antisemitism. Schmitt's participation in the regime is a documented historical fact. It cannot be neutralised as a personal contingency or used to avoid critical study of his categories.

Selective receptions. A category reused in different settings often changes its function. Attributing every use to its original formulation creates false genealogies; ignoring its origins may conceal important limits.

Details That Illuminate the Work

A Catholic in imperial Germany. His confessional formation and Catholicism's place in German society help explain his interest in representation and political form without reducing his work to a single religious doctrine.

Weber's intellectual milieu. Schmitt moved in circles in which Weber was highly influential. Historical proximity is not enough to identify their conceptions of the state.

Thucydides in translation. Hobbes published an English translation of the History of the Peloponnesian War in 1629: a verifiable historical bridge for the Constellation.

Schmitt and Shakespeare. In Amleto o Ecuba (1956), Schmitt examined the relationship between tragedy, theatrical form, and history's intrusion.

Postwar Plettenberg. Exclusion from an academic career did not prevent continuing intellectual relationships and visits. Influence of that kind does not amount to moral rehabilitation.

Contrasting receptions. Thinkers of different orientations have criticised or reused Schmitt's categories; this demonstrates the mobility of reception, not consensus about his views.

Common Errors

'Schmitt only writes about exception.' He also addresses constitutions, representation, parliamentarism, nomos, and irregular war.

'Friend and enemy means personal hatred.' The criterion concerns collectivities and public antagonism.

'For Schmitt, politics must produce wars.' War is an extreme possibility, not a permanent purpose.

'Decisionism means doing whatever one likes.' It is a theory of the foundational role of decision, with risks of arbitrariness that require critical assessment.

'Every emergency is a state of exception.' Extraordinary powers may be constitutionally regulated; Schmitt's problem is more radical.

'Legality and legitimacy are synonyms.' Schmitt distinguishes them to investigate the resilience of order under formally valid procedures.

'Kelsen deals with law and Schmitt with reality.' Both address validity, politics, and institutions, proposing different solutions. Heller shows there are further alternatives.

'Schmitt was simply anti-democratic.' He offered a conception of democracy distinct from liberalism, marked by a homogeneity incompatible with fundamental aspects of pluralist democracy.

'Nazism was a parenthesis.' His collaboration was active, public, and ideologically compromised.

'Schmitt opposed Nazism after 1936.' His marginalisation took place inside the regime.

'Nomos is just geography.' It concerns appropriation, distribution, and the institution of legal order.

'Schmitt and Clausewitz offer the same theory.' The former examines the criterion of the political; the latter examines war's relationship to force and political purpose.

Glossary

Article 48. A provision of the Weimar constitution concerning extraordinary presidential powers, subject to conditions and checks specified in the text.

Decisionism. An approach emphasising the independent role of decision in relation to simple normative deduction.

Commissarial dictatorship. An exceptional exercise of powers aimed at preserving an order.

Sovereign dictatorship. An extraordinary exercise of power aimed at founding a new constitution.

Exception. An extreme situation calling into question the normality required for applying rules.

Führerprinzip. A National Socialist authoritarian principle subordinating the order to the leader's authority.

Hostis. Public enemy, distinct from a personal adversary.

Jus publicum Europaeum. A historical order of international law among European states in Schmitt's reconstruction.

Legality. An act's conformity to procedures and applicable rules.

Legitimacy. The foundation for political recognition of an order or authority.

Absolute enemy. A form of radicalised enmity threatening to eliminate the limits of reciprocal recognition.

Nomos. A concrete spatial order founded on appropriation, division, and organisation.

Normativism. An approach studying the structure and validity of a legal order through rules.

Homogeneity. A category of common belonging emphasised in Schmitt's theory of democracy.

Partisan. An irregular fighter at the centre of Schmitt's 1963 reflections.

Constituent power. The political capacity to establish a constitution.

Representation. The institutional form through which political unity is made publicly present.

Sovereignty. The ultimate authority Schmitt identifies through the decision on the state of exception.

Political theology. Study of historical and structural relations between theological concepts and modern state theory.

Reading Paths

Conceptual introduction. Teologia politica and Il concetto del politico, read alongside Weimar's history and knowledge of Schmitt's Nazi collaboration, introduce the two best-known themes without reducing him to a formula.

Law and constitution. La dittatura, Dottrina della costituzione, Il custode della costituzione, and Legalità e legittimità, read together with the dispute with Kelsen and the counterpoint provided by Heller.

Liberalism and representation. La crisi della democrazia parlamentare, Cattolicesimo romano e forma politica, and Il concetto del politico, accompanied by critical studies of pluralism.

State, space, and international law. The 1938 book on Hobbes, Terra e mare, and Il nomos della terra, together with studies of colonial history and international law.

Towards Clausewitz. Il concetto del politico and Teoria del partigiano prepare the problem of enmity while leaving to the later military guide the task of explaining the conduct of war.

Historical responsibility. The writings and interventions of 1933–36 need to be read alongside archival documents and scholarship on antisemitism and the Nazi regime.

Questions to Guide the Reader

  • Can a rule fully determine all the conditions of its own application?
  • Does recognising an exception necessarily imply the unchecked primacy of whoever decides?
  • What distinguishes a political adversary, a public enemy, and personal hatred?
  • Who possesses the power to name the enemy and define belonging?
  • Can a constitution be transformed against its foundations through legal procedures?
  • What distinguishes defending a constitution from concentrating power in an authoritarian way?
  • Why do Kelsen and Heller offer two different counterpoints to Schmitt?
  • What follows from placing homogeneity at the centre of democratic identity?
  • What does Schmitt's collaboration with National Socialism teach us about the relationship between theory and responsibility?
  • What connects international law, territory, appropriation, and limits on war?
  • How does Schmitt's theory of the political differ from Clausewitz's theory of war?

Points to Remember

  • Rules have concrete conditions. Their effectiveness presupposes an environment in which they can operate.
  • Deciding on exception raises a problem of limits. Diagnosing an emergency and legitimising unchecked power are not the same.
  • Political enmity is public. It cannot be reduced to private resentment and need not exhaust every form of politics.
  • Procedures can be used against the order. Defending that order nevertheless requires checks that do not reproduce arbitrary power.
  • Weimar offered theoretical alternatives. Kelsen and Heller prevent us from treating decisionism as the only possible response.
  • Nazi complicity belongs to the history of the work. Analysis of categories and judgment of actions must remain together.
  • Law has a spatial dimension. The Nomos brings it to light but does not automatically justify conquest and inequality.
  • Clausewitz opens a different problem. From the political definition of an enemy to the conduct of war and its purposes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who Was Carl Schmitt?

Carl Schmitt (1888–1985) was a German jurist and political theorist known for theories of sovereignty, the state of exception, the political, and constitutional order. He joined National Socialism in 1933 and helped legitimise the regime.

What Does the State of Exception Mean for Schmitt?

It is an extreme situation in which the normality required for the ordinary application of rules disappears. Schmitt regards it as the moment when sovereign decision comes to the fore.

What Does the Friend–Enemy Distinction Mean?

It is the criterion through which Schmitt identifies the extreme possibility of political antagonism between collectivities. The public enemy is not a personal enemy, and the distinction does not imply that every conflict must become a war.

What Is Schmitt's Decisionism?

It is a conception giving decision a role that cannot be completely deduced from rules. Applied to sovereignty, it raises the question of what limits remain effective in extreme moments.

What Is the Difference Between Commissarial and Sovereign Dictatorship?

Commissarial dictatorship exercises extraordinary powers to restore or defend an existing order; sovereign dictatorship works towards the foundation of a new constitution.

What Is the Difference Between Legality and Legitimacy?

Legality concerns the conformity of acts and procedures with existing rules; legitimacy concerns the political basis on which an order is recognised. For Schmitt, these two dimensions can come into conflict.

Was Schmitt Opposed to Democracy?

He developed a conception of democracy distinct from liberalism and parliamentarism, founded on political identity and homogeneity. It poses serious problems for pluralism and the guarantees of constitutional democracies.

Was Carl Schmitt a Nazi?

Yes. He joined the NSDAP in May 1933, held posts within the regime, publicly justified Hitler's violent action in 1934, and promoted antisemitic initiatives. His marginalisation from 1936 did not amount to opposition to Nazism.

How Do Schmitt and Kelsen Differ?

Schmitt emphasises political decision and the president as a possible guardian of constitutional unity; Kelsen defends institutional legal protection centred on constitutional adjudication. Their dispute also concerns limits on authority and the role of pluralism.

Why Is Hermann Heller Important to the Comparison?

Heller shows that recognising the social and political conditions of law does not require accepting the decisionist solution. He provides a democratic counterpoint to Weimar's crisis.

Why Is Hobbes Important to Schmitt?

Hobbes is a primary reference for sovereignty, protection, obedience, and the state. Schmitt devoted a book to Leviathan in 1938, a reading that must also be examined against the ideological problems of its period.

What Is the Nomos of the Earth?

It is the category through which Schmitt interprets international legal order in terms of appropriation, distribution, and organisation of space. His reconstruction of the European order requires critical examination of colonial history.

What Is the Relationship Between Schmitt and Clausewitz?

Schmitt examines the political, enmity, and the transformation of the enemy; Clausewitz studies war and the relationship between military force and political purpose. Teoria del partigiano provides a passage between the two questions.

Why Study Schmitt Today?

Because his categories help us examine sovereignty, constitutional crises, extraordinary powers, political belonging, and international order. Their use requires critical distance and full awareness of his collaboration with National Socialism.

Further Reading

Thomas Hobbes — Fear, Sovereignty, and Political Order. The relationship between protection and obedience offers the main bridge between Thucydides' world and modern sovereignty.

Max Weber — Rationalisation, Power, and Disenchantment. Legitimacy, the state, and organisation allow a comparison with Schmitt's decisionism without conflating the two.

Niccolò Machiavelli — Power, the State, and Effective Truth. Conflict, necessity, and the preservation of political orders become subjects of dialogue between different traditions.

Thucydides — War, Power, and the Crisis of Speech. The crisis of judgment in the city prepares the editorial problem of order without making Thucydides a direct precursor of Schmitt.

Editorial Closing — Order and Its Price

A political community exists in part to free coexistence from arbitrary power. Rules set limits and allow different people to recognise themselves as subjects of the same order even while they disagree.

Schmitt compelled state theory to examine what happens when this continuity fractures. He showed that law has political and concrete conditions that no normative formula can, by itself, guarantee in every situation.

Yet the force of the question does not automatically rescue the answer. A decision can protect an order; it can also empty it of the guarantees that give it civic purpose. A community may seek unity in the face of danger; it may also surrender to those who govern the power to decide who deserves to belong.

Schmitt's history forces us to recognise this second possibility in its gravest form. His collaboration with National Socialism does not belong at the margins of an intellectual biography: it tests the relationship between theory, authority, and responsibility.

Studying Schmitt therefore means acquiring a problem without necessarily accepting his solution. It means understanding that order needs decisions while continuing to ask which rules prevent decision from becoming its own sole foundation.

After Thucydides, who observed the crisis of speech and judgment in conflict, Schmitt leaves a question about the durability of institutions and the limits of those who claim to save them. Clausewitz will carry the problem into the use of force.

What remains of law, freedom, and community when the decision pronounced in defence of order has ended by changing its meaning?
