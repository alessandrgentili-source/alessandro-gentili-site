Thucydides: war, power and the crisis of language

When war enters the language of political judgment.

Introduction

Two ships sailed from Athens towards Mytilene with opposite orders. The first carried a sentence issued in anger: the men of the rebellious city were to be killed, its women and children enslaved. The second, dispatched after the assembly reconsidered, arrived just in time to prevent the full sentence.

The interval between those voyages is the beginning of an inquiry into power and judgment. What happens when fear, empire and the language of necessity begin to decide what a city regards as just?

Thucydides in five minutes

Thucydides was a fifth-century BCE Athenian historian and general. He survived the plague at Athens and spent many years in exile after the fall of Amphipolis in 424 BCE. His unfinished History of the Peloponnesian War follows the conflict between Athens, Sparta and their alliances from 431 BCE; the surviving text stops in 411, although the war ended in 404.

He investigated conflicting testimony and reconstructed political speeches without pretending to know the exact words spoken (I.22). War appears in his pages as military action, public argument, disease, faction and the difficult work of knowing what has happened.

Fear and the politics of power

In I.23, Thucydides locates the deeper cause of the war in the growth of Athenian power and the fear it aroused in Sparta. An increase in another city's capacity was transformed into an immediate political problem through expectations about the future. Allies, grievances and assemblies helped turn fear into a decision.

The language of compulsion is strong, yet its meaning is disputed. It offers no general law that every rising power must fight an established one. Thucydides shows a changing order interpreted by people who must choose before their fears can be proved right or wrong.

Mytilene: the second decision

In 427 BCE, Cleon defended severe punishment as necessary to imperial security. Diodotus challenged its usefulness: terror could fail to deter rebellion, and Athens should judge what policy would actually serve its interests. The second proposal narrowly prevailed (III.36–50).

The reversal was incomplete. More than a thousand men considered responsible for the revolt were still executed, according to the transmitted text. The episode's force lies in the possibility of correction within a harsh imperial order: an assembly could return its own decision to argument before action made it irreversible.

Corcyra: when words change sides

During the murderous civil war at Corcyra, democratic and oligarchic factions looked to Athens and Sparta for support. Thucydides records how the evaluation of conduct changed: recklessness was praised as courage, restraint suspected as cowardice, loyalty to faction elevated over older bonds (III.82).

The exact philological meaning of his claim about words is disputed, but the surrounding examples reveal a political mechanism. A community can retain words such as prudence and justice while reserving their praise for its own faction. The common language remains; its ability to judge the conflict begins to fail.

Melos: necessity in the voice of power

In 416 BCE, Athens demanded the submission of neutral Melos. The Athenians argued from unequal strength; the Melians invoked justice, hope and possible Spartan help. Melos was conquered, its adult men killed and its women and children enslaved (V.84–116).

The dialogue later became a central text of political realism. Its imperial speakers, however, cannot automatically be treated as the historian's own voice. Conquest creates enemies, obligations and dangers that can later be invoked to justify continued domination. What is called necessity may include the legacy of earlier choices.

Sicily: when power loses its measure

In 415 BCE, Athens approved a vast expedition to Sicily despite limited knowledge of the island. Nicias attempted to deter the assembly by describing the enormous preparations required. The warning increased confidence; Alcibiades pressed the case for expansion (VI.24).

The catastrophe involved resistance in Sicily, Spartan intervention, leadership failures and later political rivalries. Thucydides gives substantial weight to decisions made after the expedition's departure (II.65). The episode asks how a city with real resources can become less discerning about what those resources justify attempting.

What must be kept — and questioned

Thucydides' perspective is exceptionally penetrating and highly selective. Generals, assemblies and elites dominate the narrative; women, enslaved people and subject populations rarely speak for themselves. His speeches are historical-literary reconstructions, and his admiration for Pericles shapes his portrait of democratic leadership.

Modern realism has learned much from his treatment of fear and power. Yet isolating I.23 or the Melian Dialogue can flatten an account rich in institutional conflict and moral uncertainty. The Thucydides Trap is a modern expression associated with Graham Allison, not a theory named by Thucydides.

The contemporary wound

Conflict can alter the language by which it ought to be judged. Security, courage, betrayal and peace may come to mean different things according to who acts and who speaks. Corcyra was a bloody civil war, far more extreme than ordinary disagreement in a modern democracy; the comparison concerns a mechanism, never an equivalence of events.

Can a political community still correct itself when fear has already supplied the terms of debate? This is Thucydides' distinct place in the eleventh triad. Schmitt brings sovereignty and exception to the inquiry; Clausewitz will examine force in relation to political purpose.

Primary texts and orientation

Read Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War: I.22–23; II.65; III.36–50 and III.82; V.84–116; and Books VI–VII. For scholarly orientation: Simon Hornblower, A Commentary on Thucydides (1991–2008); Polly Low (ed.), The Cambridge Companion to Thucydides (2023); Tobias Joho, Style and Necessity in Thucydides (2022).
