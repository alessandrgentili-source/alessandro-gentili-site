A reader's route

Someone begins to use a phrase; a few others repeat it; before long its origin has vanished into ordinary speech. The main essay follows that small event into Gabriel Tarde's work as a magistrate, his account of invention and imitation, his unusual economic psychology of desire and belief, the power of prestige, the paradox of counter-imitation, the newspaper public and his dispute with Émile Durkheim. It closes with a controlled experiment in which the same songs acquire different public fortunes. The experiment belongs to 2006; the problem of how one person's judgment becomes another person's example belongs to Tarde.

The Atlas desk is a separate reading and reference instrument. It provides a biographical chronology, historical and disciplinary context, a map of works, a preserved three-view instrument on the fate of an invention, conceptual distinctions, a four-direction constellation, a working glossary, reading paths, twenty FAQs and the evidence and limits behind the contemporary comparison. The desk is meant to be revisited; the essay is meant to be read continuously.

Main essay

The main essay

1. A phrase without an author

Someone begins to use a new phrase. A few others repeat it, perhaps with a different emphasis. Before long it sounds natural, as though everyone had always spoken this way. A belief has travelled, and the history of its journey has nearly disappeared.

Gabriel Tarde was drawn to such passages. What makes a practice worth copying? How can an unfamiliar idea become credible? What happens when one current of opinion encounters another? His sociology looks closely at the relations through which private convictions and collective habits acquire their form.

The smallness of the example is deceptive. A phrase can travel because a speaker is admired, because a school repeats it, because a newspaper prints it, or because it answers something its hearers have been trying to say. It can also change on every occasion. A borrowed expression, pronounced in a different voice, acquires a use its first speaker never imagined. Society makes itself audible in such apparently minor acts.

For Tarde, the familiar division between an independent person and an overwhelming society begins too late. A person has already encountered teachers, rivals, customs and institutions by the time a conviction feels private. Yet that person may combine those inheritances unexpectedly. The passage between people leaves room for difference, as well as for conformity. How much of what we call our own was already circulating before it reached us?

2. In the magistrate's files

For much of his working life Tarde examined criminal cases in Sarlat, the town in the Dordogne where he was born in 1843. His path towards sociology ran through the practice of law. An examining magistrate confronted particular acts, accounts and circumstances; the growing sciences of crime asked why similar offences appeared in numbers, clusters and recurring forms. No statistical table could supply the entire history of an individual case. Equally, the accumulation of cases demanded some explanation of how patterns arose.

His early work on criminal statistics, law and responsibility grew in a period when criminology was becoming a contested scientific field. Cesare Lombroso's theories concerning criminal types gave biological classification a prominent place. Tarde challenged parts of that project and gave greater explanatory weight to psychological and social relations, including the transmission of practices and examples. This disagreement should not turn him into a modern empiricist in disguise. His own explanations drew on a psychology of suggestion whose assumptions were historically situated and not all empirically secure.

The administrative setting matters. In 1894 Tarde left Sarlat for Paris to direct judicial statistics at the Ministry of Justice. Six years later he took the chair of modern philosophy at the Collège de France. His career carried him from the detail of a case to the scale of the state and finally to questions about what sort of world could be described through relations. Those were different objects of study, linked by a persistent suspicion of explanations that began with a collective entity already formed.

An aggregate may show a stable rate. A court record may show a peculiar act. Between them lies the difficult question Tarde kept asking: what has passed from one person, situation or milieu into another, and how could we know?

3. What passes between two people

In Les Lois de l'imitation (1890), imitation is far more than a person deciding to copy another person's gesture. It includes the passage of customs, ways of judging, techniques and habits of attention. Tarde gives the substance of that movement a striking formulation in a heading to chapter V: “Ce qui est imité, c'est croyance ou désir” — what is imitated is belief or desire (chapter V, section I; translation here). To imitate is also to acquire something of what others take to be true and worth pursuing.

The distinction between belief and desire appears already in his 1880 essay on their possible measurement. In Psychologie économique (1902), it becomes particularly fertile. Consecutive chapters in the first volume examine the economic role of desire and belief: how wishes arise, propagate, come into conflict, strengthen through circulation or lose some of their appeal as they become commonplace; how advertising, conversation and confidence can help a novelty find buyers. The commodity is present, but its value to a particular person has a social history. Somebody has represented its uses, somebody else has found the representation credible, and an expectation has acquired an object.

Consider a profession becoming prestigious among families who had previously imagined quite different futures for their children. The desire for that profession cannot be read from wages alone. Stories about distinction, security and suitable forms of life may have travelled through schools, workplaces and conversation. Material rewards matter; a Tardean inquiry would also ask when those rewards came to signify success, who offered the first convincing example and what altered in the course of adoption. This is an illustrative question, not a reconstructed historical episode.

The account has a necessary limit. Two people may desire the same object without one imitating the other. Similar constraints, opportunities and needs can produce similar conduct. A theory of transmission must find evidence of encounters, routes and recognizable changes. Tarde supplies a vocabulary for searching for such evidence; he does not free the investigator from having to find it.

4. The prestige of a model

Some examples travel farther than others. Tarde paid attention to prestige and to the tendency of models associated with higher status to gain imitators. The vocabulary carries the marks of a stratified nineteenth-century society. To describe an influential model is to observe how people treat it; it grants no moral entitlement to the person copied.

An invention can be admired before it is understood. A technical procedure may be adopted because a prominent institution uses it. A fashionable expression may spread through association with a respected voice. A style of conduct may come to seem natural after being repeated in places where authority is expected. By the time an imitator gives reasons for the practice, some of the work of establishing its credibility may already have occurred.

But the passage is never simply from a sovereign origin to identical descendants. Practices arrive amid existing customs. A borrowed expression is used for purposes its originator did not foresee. A method developed in one workplace meets another workplace's tools, hierarchy and habits. For Tarde, invention and imitation can be understood together: a variation becomes socially consequential through adoption, and adoption can become the occasion for another variation.

The distinction is useful whenever the apparent success of an innovation conceals the relationships needed for it to be tried at all. What was new in the idea, and what made people ready to give it a hearing? Those are separate questions.

5. The person who always says no

Suppose a group adopts a practice and one member responds by making a point of doing precisely the opposite. The refusal may be principled, courageous or merely habitual. Yet the rejected model has still supplied the direction of the response. A person can spend a lifetime opposing something and allow that opposition to organize every choice.

Tarde's notion of counter-imitation makes this uncomfortable dependence visible. His wider account of repetition, opposition and adaptation appears in Les Lois sociales (1898), whose three chapters take those very themes in sequence. They offer kinds of movement in social processes. They are not a compulsory ladder on which every event must climb from imitation to conflict and finally to harmony.

Opposition can stop transmission, divide a public or produce a new arrangement. Sometimes a refusal merely reverses the sign attached to an inherited model. At other times several lines of influence meet and create a practice that belongs fully to none of them. Tarde was interested in the possibility that individuality grows through a multitude of borrowings, not through the fantasy of a person who owes nothing to anyone.

The point matters for intellectual disagreement. If the adversary supplies every question, every category and every object of attention, a seemingly independent argument may remain bound to its opponent. A genuinely different response may begin when the terms themselves change. It is a possibility to investigate, not a guarantee of reconciliation.

6. The newspaper and the people who never meet

A crowd shares a place. A public may share a printed page. In L'Opinion et la foule (1901), which brings together studies written around the turn of the century, Tarde examines people separated by distance who attend to the same events through the press. They need not gather in a square or hear the same speaker. Knowing that many others are reading about an event becomes part of the experience of reading it.

For a magistrate who had worked with cases and aggregates, this offered another way of thinking about social connection. The newspaper could bring dispersed readers into a common field of attention. Conversation gave its reports a further life. A claim might be repeated over dinner, questioned at a café, adjusted among colleagues, remembered when a new event appeared. Publication and conversation worked upon one another.

In the preface to the 1901 volume, Tarde treats the public as a dispersed crowd and singles out conversation as a neglected elementary social relationship. This emphasis keeps his account of opinion close to practices that can be described: reading, knowing others are reading, speaking, being contradicted, passing a report on. Public opinion comes into being through such relations, even though unequal access to print, education and institutions affects who can participate.

Modern networked communication offers different mechanisms, speeds and forms of visibility. Tarde's newspapers were not digital platforms, and his public was shaped by the institutions of his time. The enduring difficulty concerns the distance between encountering an account and forming a judgment in a world where other people's attention is already part of the event.

7. Two ways of explaining a society

At the end of the nineteenth century Émile Durkheim was constructing a sociology capable of treating social facts as realities with their own properties and constraints. Tarde was wary of using the collective as an explanation before examining the relations through which it had been assembled. Their disagreement made sociology's unit of explanation itself a matter of dispute.

Durkheim's Suicide (1897) challenged an explanatory appeal to imitation. The objection was serious: a recurring social pattern might arise from institutional pressures, integration or regulation, with imitation accompanying rather than causing its spread. Tarde's approach could ask how such pressures became effective through local relationships and examples. Neither direction of inquiry makes the other disappear. The disagreement is over what one must establish before claiming to have explained an observable regularity.

A striking difficulty runs through both positions. The individual studied by Tarde has never been untouched by society; the institutions studied by Durkheim are sustained by human practices. The contrast between an isolated person and a collective organism is therefore too simple. The dispute remains fruitful when it obliges investigators to move between the evidence of relationships and the constraints that persist beyond any particular relationship.

Tarde's reach can also exceed what his method can distinguish. If agreement counts as imitation and disagreement as counter-imitation, the investigator needs a way to identify processes that do not fit. A concept that can explain every outcome is at risk of explaining none with sufficient precision. This is not a defect that the attractiveness of a modern analogy can repair.

8. A world made of differences

In Monadologie et sociologie, revised and expanded in 1895 from an 1893 article, Tarde pursues the problem of relations far beyond newspapers, courts and markets. Drawing on Leibniz while changing the role assigned to relations, he imagines a world of differences, associations and compositions. The social appears as a clue to how entities can be understood through what they do together, rather than as a property confined to human groups.

This is the speculative Tarde. Its scale is exhilarating and hazardous. A philosophy of monads cannot be treated as evidence gathered by an examining magistrate; an ambitious analogy across human and nonhuman nature does not establish the same mechanism in each domain. Yet the work also prevents the history of Tarde from ending with the slogan that people imitate one another. He wanted to understand novelty itself: how differences arise, meet and survive as arrangements that might otherwise never have existed.

Later thinkers including Gilles Deleuze and Bruno Latour found reasons to read him anew. Their work belongs to distinct philosophical debates, and the history of reception is not a straight line of inheritance. Tarde's unusual combination of judicial inquiry, economic psychology and metaphysical speculation helps explain both his appeal and the need to keep his different kinds of argument apart.

9. Eight worlds listening to forty-eight songs

A listener may hear a song without knowing whether anyone else has chosen it. Another may meet the same song beside a download count that makes it look successful before the first note. In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Sheridan Dodds and Duncan Watts reported two experiments in an artificial online music market involving 14,341 participants and 48 songs by unfamiliar bands. Participants in the independent condition could listen, rate and download without seeing other users' choices. In the social-influence condition, they could see previous download totals. Those participants were also assigned to eight separate groups whose histories developed independently.

A second design made popularity more conspicuous by ordering the songs according to their accumulated downloads. Greater social influence increased both the inequality of success and its unpredictability across the separate groups. The same songs did not reach the same rank everywhere. This result does not make listening an illusion, or quality irrelevant: in the study the strongest songs rarely performed very badly and the weakest rarely did very well. But the audience's earlier decisions had become part of the conditions confronting later listeners.

The independent condition withheld previous download totals; it could not remove the other influences through which listeners arrived at the experiment. What the researchers altered was the visibility of other participants' choices. The measured result concerned patterns of collective success, with the limitations of this particular artificial market. It did not observe the inner structure of desire or test Tarde's whole sociology. It gave a precise modern form to one of his questions: how can the preference of an earlier stranger become part of a later person's reason to pay attention?

The experiment was conducted a century after Tarde's death. Its parallel worlds show why the history of attention matters to an outcome. A song can be enjoyed by each of its listeners, yet its public success depends partly on the sequence through which they encountered it. The distinction returns us to the travelling phrase. Somewhere a model began to circulate; by the time it reached the next person, its earlier reception had become part of what it meant.

Reference

The Atlas desk

Tarde at a glance

Gabriel Tarde (1843–1904) was a French magistrate, criminologist, sociologist and philosopher. Born in Sarlat, he worked as an examining magistrate, moved to Paris in 1894 to direct judicial statistics and was appointed to the Collège de France in 1900. His central problems concern the transmission of practices, beliefs and desires; the emergence of invention; the dynamics of opposition; and the relations that produce publics and social regularities. His speculative philosophy of difference belongs alongside, though cannot replace, those inquiries.

The governing question: how do beliefs and desires gain social force through encounters without erasing the possibility of invention? The contemporary extension of that question is the source of the guide's editorial interpretation; it is not terminology taken from Tarde.

Why this author matters

We often encounter institutions, fashions or public opinion as accomplished facts. Tarde asks the prior questions: who first offered a model, by which routes did it travel, how was it modified, who refused it and which forms of attention helped sustain it? The questions place a demand on research. A similarity between two people may invite an investigation; it cannot serve as proof of transmission without evidence of a relation.

His most useful warning is methodological. A majority, a trend or an aggregate contains histories that may have been made invisible by their success. His limitation is equally important: the histories cannot always be recovered, and power may persist for reasons that imitation alone cannot explain.

Historical and intellectual setting

Tarde wrote during the expansion of modern statistical administration, mass-circulation newspapers, professional criminology and French sociology. Each field offered a new way to see social regularity. The survey, the court record, the newspaper and the philosophical treatise were different instruments. Tarde moved among them, sometimes illuminating their connections and sometimes carrying a speculative claim beyond what those instruments could establish.

His contrast with Lombroso belongs to the contested history of criminology, not to a simple conflict between science and prejudice. His contrast with Durkheim belongs to the founding arguments over sociology's object and methods. Both controversies are more instructive when their institutional settings remain visible.

Disciplinary circles

  • Criminology and law. Criminal responsibility, comparisons between cases and the limitations of naturalized criminal types.
  • Sociology. Imitation, invention, opposition and the reconstruction of collective regularities from relations.
  • Economic thought. Desire, belief, confidence and the social history of value and demand.
  • Communication and public opinion. Newspapers, dispersed publics, attention and conversation.
  • Philosophy and metaphysics. Difference, association and the speculative extension of relational explanation beyond human life.

A Cerchi instrument: the fate of an invention

The three responses name possibilities in tension. A historical inquiry would still have to establish which models were encountered, what changed as a practice travelled, and what counted as an invention in that setting.

Key ideas

Transmission is part of the making of belief. Convictions can be sincere and socially inherited at the same time.

Inventions need routes. A novelty may disappear or acquire reach through adoption; the route can alter what is transmitted.

A refusal can still depend on its model. Counter-imitation reveals a specific form of relation, though it does not explain every disagreement.

Desires have social histories. Tarde's economic psychology treats wanting, expecting and trusting as elements of economic life.

A public can be dispersed. Shared attention and conversation connect people who do not occupy the same place.

An aggregate hides encounters. A recurring social pattern calls for explanation; similarity alone does not establish imitation as its cause.

Key concepts

Imitation. A process through which practices, beliefs and desires pass between people or settings, not confined to conscious copying.

Invention. A variation or new combination that introduces a possible difference into social life; its impact depends partly on subsequent transmission.

Opposition. Conflict or encounter between different currents of belief, desire or practice.

Counter-imitation. A form of refusal in which a model still defines the direction of the response.

Adaptation. A process through which elements from different lines of influence are reworked into new combinations.

Belief and desire. For Tarde, distinct but interrelated dimensions of what people hold true and pursue; both matter to social and economic processes.

Public. A dispersed association of attention and communication, distinguishable from a physically assembled crowd.

Conversation. A recurring relational practice through which reports, opinions and examples are repeated, questioned and transformed.

The constellation

Before Tarde — problems inherited. Leibniz supplies a philosophical point of departure for the monadological writing, though Tarde alters the status of relations. French moral psychology, studies of suggestion and nineteenth-century statistics supply other questions. The professionalization of criminology brought both opportunities for inquiry and deterministic classifications to contest.

Interlocutors and opponents — explanations disputed. Lombroso and the Italian criminological school form a context for Tarde's early legal and statistical work. Durkheim challenges the reach of imitation as a general explanation of social facts. The relationship between a witnessed act and a durable collective structure remains the central point of tension.

After Tarde — inheritance and disagreement. Later readers including Deleuze and Latour returned to his treatment of difference and association. Their philosophical projects take their own directions. René Girard's later account of mimetic desire, rivalry and violence offers a comparison, without a demonstrated direct derivation or equivalence of concepts.

Today — a problem to investigate. Visible rankings and public counts may turn previous users' actions into information for later choices. The MusicLab experiment gives one specific setting in which that mechanism can be studied. Its results have evidentiary force for the experiment, not for every claim made by Tarde or every contemporary platform.

Legacy and transmission

Tarde occupied a prominent position in French intellectual life around 1900, though the subsequent institutional history of sociology often foregrounded Durkheim and his school. The reappearance of Tarde in later debates about networks, difference and association has sometimes made him sound unexpectedly contemporary. A reading that begins with his actual books is more interesting. The magistrate's concern with cases, the economist's attention to confidence, the sociologist's public and the philosopher's monads do not amount to one seamless method.

His importance may lie partly in that discontinuity. Each field presents a different scale of explanation and a different demand for proof. The modern reader can learn from the movement between them while resisting the temptation to treat a suggestive analogy as a demonstrated causal account.

The contemporary wound

The MusicLab study offers a bounded example of a wider difficulty: earlier choices can become part of the environment in which subsequent choices are made. Participants were shown other people's download totals in the social-influence condition; those histories differed across eight independent groups, and the popularity rankings became less predictable. The observable effect concerns inequality and unpredictability of success in a designed cultural market. <em>Believing</em> a song is good, <em>liking</em> it and <em>seeing</em> that it is already popular are related experiences, but they are not the same variable.

The contemporary challenge is to recover these distinctions when metrics of attention are displayed as though they were immediate evidence of value. The present guide's juxtaposition of Tarde and the 2006 study is an editorial reading informed by social-influence research. Tarde had no knowledge of such experiments. Modern institutions, recommendation systems and economic incentives need their own evidence and analysis.

Limits and contradictions

Transmission needs evidence. Resemblance is compatible with common constraints and independent invention. The theory risks becoming circular if every resemblance is imitation and every difference counter-imitation.

Institutions do more than circulate examples. Law, coercion, resources and unequal standing influence what practices people can adopt. Explaining a pattern through small encounters does not abolish structural power.

Suggestion belongs to a historical psychology. Some of Tarde's assumptions about crowds and influence have been revised by later empirical work; his language can extend farther than a defensible causal test.

His social position shaped the work. Tarde wrote within nineteenth-century hierarchies, and gendered or classed assumptions appear in parts of the tradition. A careful reader should examine rather than excuse them.

Metaphysics and sociology require different warrants. The monadological argument is a philosophical construction. It cannot inherit the evidentiary status of an administrative statistic simply because both appear in Tarde's work.

Curiosities worth following

The magistrate who became a public intellectual. The movement from Sarlat to a Paris statistics directorship and then to the Collège de France connects his provincial judicial career with national intellectual institutions.

A concept older than his famous book. His 1880 essay on belief and desire precedes The Laws of Imitation by a decade. The 1902 economics extends a sustained inquiry rather than inventing desire as a late addition.

The public in print. His discussion of the dispersed public appears in essays written before L'Opinion et la foule was issued as a volume in 1901. It belongs to the history of newspapers and conversation.

An unexpectedly large ambition. The move from imitation to monads shows a thinker willing to ask whether relations are fundamental far outside human sociology.

Common misreadings to avoid

  • “Imitation means copying a visible gesture.” Tarde includes the passage of beliefs, desires, customs and judgments.
  • “The first innovator determines every later outcome.” Reception, opposition and combination change what travels.
  • “Counter-imitation is automatically independence.” Refusal can remain defined by its target.
  • “Tarde is an exact predecessor of social-media theory.” His historical public was organized around print and conversation, with different media and institutions.
  • “MusicLab proves Tarde right.” The experiment tests particular informational conditions, not his whole social philosophy.
  • “Tarde and Durkheim simply choose individuals or society.” Their dispute concerns levels of explanation, evidence and how collective regularities arise.
  • “Monadology is just sociology written at a larger scale.” Its speculative status requires a distinct form of assessment.

Reading paths

From imitation to invention. Begin with Les Lois de l'imitation, particularly the discussion of belief, desire and competing inventions; continue with the three chapters of Les Lois sociales. Ask how an adopted practice can be transformed by the conditions of its reception.

From legal statistics to social explanation. Read selections from La Criminalité comparée alongside the early essays on criminal statistics. Then compare the disagreement over imitation with Durkheim's Suicide. Keep the difference between a recurring pattern and a causal explanation in view.

From the newspaper to the market. Start with L'Opinion et la foule, especially the public and conversation. Move to volume I of Psychologie économique, chapters on desire and belief. Finally read the design and limitations of the 2006 MusicLab study, without treating it as an historical source on Tarde himself.

From sociology to metaphysics. Read the essay Les monades et la science sociale in relation to the later Monadologie et sociologie. Ask which questions about association become more ambitious, and which kinds of evidence are left behind.

Questions to orient a reading

  1. What evidence would distinguish imitation from two people responding independently to the same constraints?
  2. When does an invention acquire social existence: on its first appearance or in its adoption?
  3. How can a refusal remain dependent upon the model it rejects?
  4. What does a newspaper public share when its members have never met?
  5. Why does a statistical regularity need more than a name for its possible cause?
  6. Which aspects of Tarde's economic psychology concern beliefs rather than commodities?
  7. Where does the explanatory disagreement with Durkheim become substantive?
  8. What changes when the speculative Tarde is read alongside the magistrate?
  9. What did the MusicLab experiment actually manipulate, and what was measured?
  10. Whose actions remain invisible when a ranking is presented as a simple measure of success?

Frequently asked questions

1. Who was Gabriel Tarde?

Gabriel Tarde (1843–1904) was a French magistrate, criminologist, sociologist and philosopher. He worked for many years as an examining magistrate in Sarlat and later directed judicial statistics in Paris.

2. What is Tarde best known for?

His theory of imitation, especially in Les Lois de l'imitation (1890), which examines how inventions, practices, beliefs and desires spread through social relationships.

3. What does imitation mean in Tarde?

It is a broad process of social transmission. A person may take up a belief, desire, custom or technique through examples and relations without deliberately copying a visible action.

4. Does imitation eliminate individuality?

No necessary conclusion follows. Tarde was interested in the possibility of new combinations arising from many influences, although the social and material conditions of individuality require separate analysis.

5. What is an invention for Tarde?

A variation or new combination that introduces a possible practice or idea. Its historical reach depends on whether and how others adopt it.

6. What is counter-imitation?

A form of opposition in which someone acts against a model while remaining oriented by it. Refusal can make a genuine difference without establishing complete independence.

7. Are imitation, opposition and adaptation three fixed stages?

No. They describe different movements of social life that can overlap and recur. The guide's three-state reading instrument compares possibilities rather than prescribing a sequence.

8. Why are belief and desire central in Tarde?

He treated them as dimensions of what circulates through imitation. In Psychologie économique (1902) he examined their role in economic life, including confidence, advertising and the formation of wants.

9. Did Tarde write about economics?

Yes. Psychologie économique, published in two volumes in 1902, considers desire, belief and their interaction in economic activity. It extends problems that appeared in earlier writings.

10. What was Tarde's profession before his academic career?

He was a magistrate. His experience in legal investigation and his work on criminal statistics helped shape the questions he posed about individual cases and social regularities.

11. What is the difference between a crowd and a public in Tarde?

A crowd gathers physically; a public may be dispersed but connected through attention and communication. Tarde examined the role of newspapers and conversation in this process.

12. Why was conversation important to Tarde?

Because reports and opinions acquire new meanings when people discuss, repeat, question and modify them. Conversation helps turn circulated information into social relations.

13. How did Tarde disagree with Durkheim?

Tarde emphasized relations and transmission in explaining collective patterns, while Durkheim emphasized social facts and structures with their own constraints. Their dispute also concerned the adequacy of imitation as a causal explanation.

14. What is Tarde's monadology?

A speculative account of difference and association inspired by, but distinct from, Leibniz. It extends the language of relations beyond human sociology.

15. Did Tarde influence Deleuze and Latour?

Both later thinkers engaged with aspects of Tarde's work. Their projects should be studied on their own terms rather than treated as direct continuations of a single doctrine.

16. Did Tarde anticipate social media?

He studied the press, publics and social transmission in his own era. These concepts can illuminate questions about contemporary media, but they are not predictions of digital platforms.

17. What was the 2006 MusicLab experiment?

Salganik, Dodds and Watts studied an artificial online market involving 14,341 participants and 48 unfamiliar songs. They compared choices made with and without information about earlier participants' downloads.

18. What did MusicLab find?

Stronger social influence increased inequality and unpredictability of popularity across independently developing groups. The result belongs to that experimental design, not to all cultural markets.

19. Does the experiment prove Tarde's theory?

No. It gives measurable evidence of effects of visible previous choices in a particular setting. It does not test the entirety of Tarde's historical sociology or the origin of every participant's desire.

20. Why read Tarde with Epicurus and Kahneman?

Epicurus examines desires and their limits; Tarde examines the social circulation of belief and desire; Kahneman examines judgment under uncertainty. Their differences make the conditions of choice more visible.

An editorial closing

The opening phrase reached its last listener by a route the listener may never know. It is now uttered with conviction, in a different voice, perhaps to a different purpose. Somewhere in the passage it changed. Someone will repeat it again tomorrow, or reject it, or join it to another idea that makes the old words mean something new.

That everyday event is small enough to escape notice. Tarde asks us to follow it anyway. His achievement lies in finding questions within the passage; his difficulty lies in proving which passages occurred and which forces compelled them. To look at a settled opinion and recover the possible encounters behind it is to restore history to what had begun to feel inevitable. It also leaves the reader with an exacting question: whose example has become part of my own reasons, and how would I recognize the difference?
