Gabriel Tarde: imitation, belief and desire

What feels personal has travelled through other lives.

Introduction

Someone begins to use a new phrase. A few others repeat it, perhaps with a different emphasis. Before long it sounds natural, as though everyone had always spoken this way. A belief has travelled, and the history of its journey has nearly disappeared.

Gabriel Tarde was drawn to such passages. What makes a practice worth copying? How can an unfamiliar idea become credible? What happens when one current of opinion encounters another? His sociology looks closely at the relations through which private convictions and collective habits acquire their form.

How much of what we call our own was already circulating before it reached us? The question gives Tarde his particular place between Epicurus, who asks what deserves to be desired, and Kahneman, who will examine the judgments through which choices take shape.

Tarde in five minutes

Gabriel Tarde (1843–1904) was a French magistrate, criminologist, sociologist and philosopher. Born in Sarlat, he worked for many years in the judiciary, including as an examining magistrate. His experience of individual cases and criminal statistics prompted a lasting question: how do singular acts become observable social regularities?

In 1894 he moved to Paris to direct criminal statistics at the Ministry of Justice. In 1900 he was appointed to the Collège de France. His major works include The Laws of Imitation (Les Lois de l’imitation, 1890), Social Logic (La Logique sociale, 1895), Social Laws (Les Lois sociales, 1898), Opinion and the Crowd (L’Opinion et la foule, 1901) and Economic Psychology (Psychologie économique, 1902).

Imitation is central, yet it describes only one movement in a larger account of difference, invention, opposition and adaptation. Tarde asks how beliefs and desires pass between people and, through those passages, help compose a society.

Before choice: belief and desire in circulation

A conviction may feel intimate while having passed through many voices. An ambition may be sincerely felt even when its object acquired prestige by being admired elsewhere. Tarde treats belief and desire as fundamental forces in social life; the transmission of a practice also carries ways of judging what is true and what is worth pursuing.

A social genealogy does not make an opinion insincere. It complicates the image of a mind whose preferences were formed in isolation. What reaches us can be accepted, reshaped or brought into tension with other influences. Individuality may arise from the particular combination, rather than from the absence, of what we have received.

Invention: where difference enters

An unfamiliar technique, a new expression, a changed rule or an unexpected combination first appears somewhere. Tarde calls such variations inventions. Their significance is not guaranteed by their novelty. A possibility can remain local, vanish, or be taken up by others and become part of a broader pattern.

Invention and imitation belong together. New possibilities make repetition possible; repetition gives a difference social reach. The process need not produce identical copies. Every adoption meets existing habits and expectations, so the thing transmitted may change as it travels.

Imitation beyond copying

In everyday language, imitation suggests the deliberate copying of a gesture. For Tarde it is a wider process of social transmission, operating through example, habit, education and influence. He traced different directions of imitation: from inward convictions towards visible conduct, through models invested with prestige, and through the competing tendencies of fashion and custom.

The hierarchical vocabulary of his time must be read historically. When Tarde describes people imitating perceived social superiors, prestige names a relation of influence, not a moral rank we should accept. A city, a profession or a fashionable figure may become a model; another influence can interrupt or redirect the process.

Opposition, counter-imitation and adaptation

Where different beliefs or desires meet, they may reinforce one another or come into conflict. Tarde gives opposition a place within social life, alongside repetition and adaptation. In Social Laws these describe broad tendencies: transmission, collision and new combinations. They are analytical movements, not a fixed sequence through which every invention must pass.

Counter-imitation offers a sharper difficulty. A person who always does the opposite of a model may remain governed by it. Resistance can create a genuine difference while still deriving its direction from what it rejects. Another response is recombination: elements gathered from several models become something that no one of them supplied alone.

A public can exist at a distance

In Opinion and the Crowd, Tarde distinguished a physically assembled crowd from a dispersed public. Readers of the same newspaper could attend to one event while sitting miles apart, aware that many others were following it too. Shared attention and awareness of others made distance compatible with collective influence.

Conversation completed the circuit. A report was discussed at a table, repeated at work, doubted in a café; its meaning shifted in countless encounters. Tarde studied the press of his own era. His insight is that opinion is formed through interactions among circulation, attention and discussion, rather than merely deposited in individual minds.

Where does society begin? Tarde and Durkheim

Émile Durkheim insisted that social facts possess properties that cannot be explained simply by the psychology of isolated individuals. Tarde, wary of treating the collective as a ready-made cause, sought the exchanges through which institutions, customs and shared judgments are produced and reproduced. The dispute concerns the direction of explanation.

Durkheim criticized imitation in Suicide (1897), and the two scholars' disagreements became public. Reducing their controversy to 'individual versus society' would diminish both. Tarde's individuals are themselves formed through earlier relations; Durkheim's collective structures are real features of human life. The question is how to explain their formation and persistence.

The philosopher of difference

Tarde's Monadology and Sociology carries his theory of relations into a much more speculative territory. Drawing on and transforming Leibniz, he treats units as compositions of differences and extends the language of association beyond human interaction, towards biological and even physical nature.

This speculative sociology of the universe must be distinguished from the empirical inquiries into imitation, law and public opinion. Later readers, including Gilles Deleuze and Bruno Latour, found fresh questions in his work, but their theories should not be mistaken for a programme Tarde had already completed.

What must be kept — and questioned

Imitation explains a great deal in Tarde, perhaps so much that its boundaries become difficult to test. If agreement is imitation and disagreement is counter-imitation, what evidence could establish that another process is at work? The vocabulary of contagion and suggestion also belongs to a nineteenth-century psychology that later research has considerably refined.

Attention to small-scale transmission does not by itself explain how durable institutions, unequal power and coercive structures maintain themselves. Some of Tarde's writing retains the prejudices and social hierarchies of its time, including troubling assumptions about crowds and gender. His interpretive reach deserves study alongside these limits.

The contemporary wound

A number on a screen can tell us how many people approve of a view before we decide whether we believe it. Recommendations, rankings and circulating images make other people's responses visible; sometimes that visibility changes the value an object seems to possess. Tarde would not have known these technologies or the systems that select what each person sees.

His questions nevertheless illuminate a problem the devices make acute: when does the apparent popularity of something become part of the reason we want it? This is an application by Circles of Ink, not a prediction attributed to Tarde. An individual may act autonomously within social relations, but autonomy now requires some understanding of the relations already shaping the choice.

Primary texts and orientation

Begin with Tarde, Les Lois de l’imitation (1890; revised second edition, 1895); La Logique sociale (1895); Les Lois sociales (1898); L’Opinion et la foule (1901), especially the essays on the public and conversation; and Psychologie économique (1902). For his philosophical project, consult Les monades et la science sociale (1893), subsequently revised as Monadologie et sociologie.

Further orientation: Matei Candea (ed.), The Social after Gabriel Tarde: Debates and Assessments; Robert Leroux (ed.), The Anthem Companion to Gabriel Tarde; and the bibliographical archive of France's École nationale d’administration pénitentiaire (ENAP).
