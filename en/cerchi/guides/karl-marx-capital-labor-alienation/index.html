Karl Marx: capital, labour and alienation

What happens when the world people produce begins to confront them as a power they cannot direct?

Opening — A table goes to market

A wooden table has a straightforward history. Someone selects timber, cuts it, joins the pieces and smooths the surface. It is transported, displayed and offered for sale. The customer sees a finished object and a price. That price makes the table comparable with thousands of other objects, even though the activities that produced them are unlike one another.

Karl Marx begins Capital with the commodity. In the first chapter, he considers an ordinary table which, as soon as it enters the world of commodities, seems to stand on its head and acquire an extraordinary life of its own. The joke has a precise target. In a society of independent producers connected through exchange, relations among people take the form of relations among the things they produce. The price is visible. The social arrangement that gives it force is harder to recognise (Capital, I.1.4).

We still encounter objects in this way. A meal arrives after a tap on a screen; an item appears at the door; a platform describes how efficiently an order has been fulfilled. The transaction is real and the convenience may be considerable. Yet someone allocated the work, owned the infrastructure, set the terms and decided which risks would be carried by whom. The interface rarely tells the whole story.

When the results of human activity appear to govern their makers, how can we recognise the relations that produced them—and decide which of those relations might be changed?

How to read this page

The reading path begins with Marx’s account of alienated work and the strange social life of commodities, moves through capital, value and political history, and ends with the contemporary problem of work organised through platforms and metrics. The reading path

1. A life among revolutions, factories and books

Marx was born in Trier in 1818 and trained in philosophy during the long aftermath of the French Revolution. In Berlin he encountered the legacy of Hegel, while his early work as a journalist brought questions of property, poverty and political censorship into view. His articles for the Rheinische Zeitung forced him to attend to disputes that philosophy alone could not settle. He would spend much of his adult life moving among countries that did not welcome his writing.

In Paris, Marx came into contact with French socialism and met Friedrich Engels, whose knowledge of English industrial society became indispensable to their collaboration. Brussels gave them a place to develop arguments about history and material life. In 1848 they published the Communist Manifesto amid revolutions that would soon be defeated. Marx ultimately settled in London, where poverty, family bereavement, journalism and years of economic study accompanied the writing of Capital.

The reading room of the British Museum is an appropriate image of the mature Marx at work. He consulted reports on factory conditions, economic studies, political histories and the statistics of a rapidly expanding commercial world. His position in London mattered: the empire’s financial and industrial power could be studied through the documents it generated, often from the precarious situation of a political exile. The first volume of Capital appeared in 1867. The second and third were edited and published by Engels after Marx’s death in 1883; they must not be treated as books Marx delivered in finished form.

This life explains his range, without proving his conclusions. Marx was a philosopher of human activity, a critic of political economy, a journalist of revolutions and a political organiser. The tensions between those roles belong to his work.

2. The work that becomes someone else’s world

In the manuscripts written in Paris in 1844, Marx describes a worker who produces an object that belongs to another and appears to grow in power as its maker loses control. He investigates several related separations: from the product, from the activity of producing, from other people and from the human capacity for conscious, shared, creative activity. The last is often discussed through his demanding idea of species-being (Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, “Estranged Labour”).

Alienation here is a social relationship. A person may work long hours and feel exhausted without all the features of Marx’s account being present. Conversely, skilled and well-paid work can still be alienating if the worker has little meaningful influence over the activity, its purposes and its results. The problem concerns the organisation and ownership of productive life; it cannot be diagnosed from a mood alone.

Marx’s account also contains an ambition that deserves scrutiny. He holds that human beings can develop themselves through purposive work and social cooperation. This is a rich claim about human flourishing, but it is not a neutral observation. People differ in what they find fulfilling, and work is never the whole of a human life. Any attempt to use his concept today should ask which forms of autonomy have actually been lost, who experiences the loss and by what standard we judge it.

The early manuscripts were not published as a book during Marx’s lifetime. His later analysis changed its language and explanatory methods. It would be misleading either to discard the early work as irrelevant or to read every page of Capital as an expanded version of the 1844 theory. The continuous question concerns the power of a world made through human activity; the answers develop.

3. A commodity carries a social relation

Capital begins by distinguishing the usefulness of an object from the social form under which it is exchanged. A table is useful because someone can eat, work or gather around it. In a society where products are made for exchange, the table also enters a relation of value with other commodities. Marx examines how different kinds of concrete labour are represented through abstract, socially necessary labour time (Capital, I.1.1–2).

Usefulness and exchangeability should not be confused with the market price displayed on a label. Marx’s account of value involves a specific theory of capitalist production and social labour; actual prices are affected by additional determinations. His theory of labour and value has been debated extensively by economists and philosophers. A useful reading must first understand its internal argument before deciding how far it succeeds as an account of a real economy.

The celebrated section on commodity fetishism explains an especially unsettling consequence. Producers meet one another through the exchange of products. Because their work is coordinated through that exchange, their social relationship appears as a property of the products themselves. This is deeper than a customer failing to know where a purchase was made. Even complete information about a factory would not abolish the social form in which work must establish its value through exchange.

The table’s apparent independence is therefore an effect of how social labour is organised. Marx’s achievement is to make familiar objects strange without treating the people who buy them as simply foolish. They inhabit relations that genuinely confront them through things, money, wages and prices.

4. Where the increase comes from

An amount of money can buy a commodity and later be exchanged for money again. Marx asks what is different when money is advanced as capital, with the expectation of returning as more money: M–C–M′, money–commodity–more money (Capital, I.4). The increase is central to the process, yet exchange alone cannot explain it under the assumptions Marx is investigating.

His answer turns on a distinction between labour and labour-power. Workers sell the capacity to work for a wage. Within Marx’s theoretical account, the value of that capacity is connected to the socially and historically determined requirements of its reproduction. In production, living labour can create value greater than the value of the labour-power purchased. The difference is surplus value. Necessary labour time reproduces the value of labour-power; surplus labour time produces the value appropriated by capital (Capital, I.6–9).

This is why Marx’s analysis of exploitation cannot be reduced to the accusation that every employer simply cheats a worker. His claim is that a formally lawful exchange of labour-power can coexist with a structurally unequal organisation of production. The question is who possesses the means of production and who must sell the capacity to work in order to live. Profits, prices and wages in actual economies require further explanation; a diagram of necessary and surplus labour is a conceptual model, not a ready-made calculation of a modern firm's accounts.

Marx studies how machinery, cooperation, the length of the working day and the pursuit of productivity alter this relationship. He also recognises the extraordinary productive forces released by capitalist industry. His criticism gains force precisely because the same system expands collective powers and concentrates control over their use.

5. History has people inside it

Marx’s critique of political economy is inseparable from a historical question. How do arrangements that seem permanent come into being, and what allows them to endure? In the manuscripts later assembled as The German Ideology, written with Engels in 1845–46, everyday material activity becomes a starting point for explaining social institutions and ideas. The 1859 preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy offers a compressed account of relations of production and forms of political and intellectual life. Neither text removes the need to explain actual history.

Consider The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852), written after the coup of December 1851 in France. The book traces parties, class fractions, rural interests, state institutions and political representation through a crisis whose outcome cannot be deduced from the economy in a single step. Its account of Louis Bonaparte’s rise is concerned with political action and the forms through which groups imagine themselves represented.

The political detail of this book complicates a mechanically deterministic version of historical materialism. Economic conditions matter, and institutions, conflicts, ideologies and political choices have histories of their own. There are serious disagreements about how Marx relates these levels. We should read his historical analyses alongside his compressed theoretical formulations, rather than letting a slogan about the “base” and “superstructure” replace them.

Marx’s concept of ideology changes across texts and is contested in later interpretation. One enduring concern is how historically produced arrangements acquire the appearance of common sense. A wage can appear to express a fully equal contract while leaving the structure of production outside the frame. This does not make every belief false or every institution a simple instrument of one class. It calls for inquiry into the conditions under which a belief becomes plausible and useful.

6. The contemporary wound — When freedom appears as a feature

Imagine a courier who opens an app and sees the day's available work. The interface promises flexibility: there are tasks to accept, routes to follow and a performance score to maintain. The courier may value the freedom of choosing when to log on. The same arrangement may leave little room to negotiate payment, examine a rating or contest an account suspension. Whether those limits exist, and how severe they are, depends on the platform, jurisdiction and contract. They cannot be assumed from the word algorithm alone.

This is a more demanding example than the claim that Marx predicted platforms. The questions belong to the present: who controls access to work, allocates tasks, owns the infrastructure, uses the records and decides what counts as satisfactory performance? The worker may see only a sequence of prompts while experiencing consequences produced by people, institutions and code. Convenience for the consumer and dependency for the worker can be parts of the same transaction.

Digital alienation names one aspect of this problem: the ways work, attention, reputation, data and social life can become objects of measurement and valorisation. It is a contemporary interpretation developed by Cerchi d’inchiostro, absent from Marx’s own vocabulary. Time spent online does not automatically become wage labour, and a digital trace does not in itself generate surplus value in Marx’s technical sense.

The digital economy still rests on material activity: minerals, electricity, warehouses, devices, programmers, cleaners, drivers, moderators and service workers. Its apparent weightlessness can obscure the labour and environmental requirements of the systems that make it possible. Here Marx provides a discipline of attention: follow the relationships through which an attractive service becomes a durable economic power.

A person who can choose among tasks may have genuine freedoms. The harder question is whether that person can influence the conditions under which choices are offered, measurements imposed and work discontinued. An interface can make action easy while making the rules of action hard to contest. That is the wound this guide asks us to examine.

7. The strength and the limits of the diagnosis

Marx is at his most persuasive when he makes the organisation of production visible inside apparently neutral categories. His theory asks who owns, who works, how value is accounted for and why institutions come to seem inevitable. It is less persuasive when readers transform those questions into automatic answers. Class, property and production are crucial; they do not independently explain every action, every cultural form or every experience of freedom.

The labour theory of value remains controversial, especially in the relation between values and market prices and in competing accounts of profit and exploitation. Marx’s anthropology of fulfilling work can also be challenged. His political account of a post-capitalist society is far less institutionally detailed than his criticism of capitalism. Those are significant limits, not reasons to suppress the questions he raised.

The historical legacy demands a second distinction. Marx’s writings, the diverse traditions called Marxism, the revolutionary parties that adopted his name and the states that governed in his name are connected histories. They are not identical objects. Authoritarian rule and repression committed under Marxist banners belong to any responsible account of that legacy; treating them as the inevitable reading of every passage of Marx would be equally reductive.

Marx and Engels themselves sometimes wrote with a confidence about historical development that requires criticism. A serious reading is entitled to ask whether the promised human emancipation is adequately specified, how political power would be constrained, and what safeguards dissent would possess. The absence of such detail cannot be repaired by insisting that economic change will settle every problem.

The table with which we began remains useful. We can admire the craft, use the object, study its price and question the conditions of its production. One kind of understanding need not cancel the others. Marx’s lasting provocation is that a society’s things can tell us something about the relations people have built—and about how those relations might be governed.

8. In the second triad — What can a human life be made to serve?

Capital, values, desire places Marx beside Nietzsche and Leopardi to expose three different pressures on modern existence. Marx investigates the social organisation of labour and value; Nietzsche asks how inherited values arise, claim authority and can be revalued; Leopardi examines the reach of desire and the inadequacy of the satisfactions offered to it.

These thinkers cannot be folded into a single diagnosis of consumer society. Marx’s value in political economy is not Nietzsche’s problem of the valuation of life, and Leopardi’s desire is not a disguised theory of consumption. Their distance is the source of the tension: economic systems measure and organise activity, cultures assign worth, and human wanting often exceeds both available goods and inherited ideals.

Marx at a glance — Life, setting and problem

Who: Karl Marx (1818–1883), born in Trier and deceased in London; philosopher, journalist, critic of political economy and political organiser. His long collaboration with Friedrich Engels shaped major texts and helped preserve and publish unfinished work.

Historical setting: post-Revolutionary Europe, industrial expansion, the revolutions of 1848, the rise of the modern wage-working class, imperial trade and nineteenth-century struggles over democracy, property and social reform. Exile, the study of British industry and political conflict illuminate his movement from German philosophy toward the critique of capitalism.

Why he matters: Marx treats capitalism as a historically specific form of social organisation in which human activity is represented through value, commodities, wages and capital. The human problem: people produce a common world that may acquire power over them. A necessary caution: the early theory of alienation, the mature critique of value and the political writings remain related but distinct lines of inquiry.

Disciplinary circles — How the questions connect

Philosophy and anthropology: Hegel, Feuerbach, human powers, alienation, practical activity and emancipation. Political economy: labour-power, value, surplus value, money, accumulation, machinery and crises. History and politics: class struggles, constitutions, revolution, the state and the difficult autonomy of political conflict. Sociology and culture: ideology, social relations, institutions, religion, representations of normality. Technology and contemporary labour: the organisation of production through machinery, infrastructures and platforms; these last connections require present-day evidence and additional scholarship.

Ideas and concepts to retain

  • Capital is a social relation in motion. Money becomes capital in a process oriented toward valorisation and sustained by specific relations of production.
  • The commodity carries more than its material usefulness. Value expresses a historically particular way of coordinating social labour through exchange.
  • Alienation concerns the form of activity and control. It is not exhausted by dissatisfaction, poverty or fatigue.
  • An apparently free contract can coexist with structural dependency. Marx’s analysis of labour-power asks who controls the conditions of production.
  • Historical conditions matter without abolishing political action. The Eighteenth Brumaire resists simple economic formulas when read closely.

The constellation — Interlocutors, disputes, afterlives

Before and around Marx: Hegel makes historical contradiction central to philosophical inquiry; Feuerbach develops a critique of religious projection that Marx turns toward material life. Adam Smith and David Ricardo provide economic categories that Marx studies and disputes. French socialism and workers’ organisation supply both questions and adversaries. Rousseau’s inquiries into property and inequality belong to a broader intellectual background, without making every parallel a direct line of influence.

Alongside him: Engels is collaborator, intellectual interlocutor, practical supporter and posthumous editor. Proudhon and Bakunin are consequential antagonists in debates over socialism and revolutionary authority. Lassalle represents another contested path in workers’ politics. The workers, journalists and industrialists described in Marx’s writings were not merely illustrations; their conflicts shaped the material he tried to explain.

After Marx: Rosa Luxemburg, Lenin and Gramsci developed substantially different political and theoretical responses. Lukács extended the analysis of reification; Frankfurt School thinkers related social critique to culture and psychology. Later historians and interpreters, including Eric Hobsbawm, David Harvey, Moishe Postone and Michael Heinrich, ask different questions of Marx’s unfinished and contested intellectual inheritance. These are distinct projects, with political consequences that must be evaluated rather than treated as a single school.

In the present: platform labour, supply chains, finance, automation, data extraction and the organisation of attention invite Marxian questions, but none is settled merely by giving it the label capitalism. The relevant ownership, labour process and economic mechanism must be shown in each case.

Limits, revealing details and common mistakes

Limits worth confronting: Marx’s labour theory of value is contested; the relation between early alienation and later economic analysis is debated; his description of post-capitalist institutions is incomplete; universal claims about the revolutionary subject can obscure social differences. Any serious guide must also face repression and political domination in states that later invoked Marx’s name, without confusing those states, their doctrines and Marx’s writings.

Three revealing details: the young Marx began his public career as a journalist dealing with censorship and property disputes; the mature Marx drew on industrial reports and painstaking research while working in London; and his collaboration with Engels was intellectually and materially formative. The biography neither proves nor invalidates the theory, but it helps explain why he wrote in more than one genre.

Avoid: assuming commodity fetishism means only admiration for consumer brands; treating every kind of online activity as productive wage labour; interpreting abstract labour as remote office work; confusing surplus value with all profit as recorded by firms; reducing history to an automatic “base causes superstructure” sequence; identifying Marx without remainder with every later Marxism; claiming he foresaw artificial intelligence.

Reading routes and questions worth keeping

Begin (about 35 minutes): Capital I.1.4 on commodity fetishism; “Estranged Labour” in the 1844 manuscripts; the opening pages of The Eighteenth Brumaire. Ask what changes when a familiar object, a working day or an election is examined through its social relations.

Explore for a week: Capital I.1, I.4, I.6–10; the 1859 preface; The Eighteenth Brumaire I and VII. Record the moments at which Marx moves from an economic category to a historical claim, and whether the evidence supports the move.

Go deeper: study Capital I in full, then compare the 1844 manuscripts, the 1857–58 Grundrisse, Engels’s editorial work on later volumes and the 1875 Critique of the Gotha Programme. Use critical scholarship to distinguish the texts, their editions and their changing purposes.

Keep these questions beside the reading: Who owns the conditions of action? How is value measured? Which social relationship has become harder to recognise because it appears as a thing or a technical necessity? These questions belong to the contemporary reader. They are not quotations from Marx.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Karl Marx?

Karl Marx (1818–1883) was a German-born philosopher, journalist, critic of political economy and revolutionary organiser. He collaborated closely with Friedrich Engels and wrote major works about capitalism, class conflict, labour and historical change.

What does Marx mean by alienation?

In the 1844 manuscripts, Marx describes workers becoming estranged from the product of their work, their activity, other people and their own creative capacities. Later uses and interpretations develop the concept further.

What is commodity fetishism?

In Capital I.1.4, relations between producers appear as relations between commodities and their values. The phenomenon is rooted in the social form of exchange, and is more than ignorance of how goods are made.

What is the difference between labour and labour-power?

Labour is productive activity; labour-power is the capacity to work that wage workers sell. Marx uses the distinction to explain how surplus value can arise under formally lawful exchange.

What is surplus value?

Within Marx’s theory, surplus value is value created by workers beyond the value of their labour-power and appropriated by capital. It is not automatically identical to a firm's recorded profit.

Was Marx against technology?

Marx recognised the productive achievements of modern industry while criticising the social relations through which machinery could control and intensify work. Technology alone does not establish whether a relation is liberating or alienating.

Does historical materialism mean economics determines everything?

No single formula captures Marx’s historical writings. Material conditions and productive relations are important, while political conflict, institutions and representations require concrete explanation. Interpretations of the degree of determination remain contested.

Did Marx predict artificial intelligence or digital platforms?

No. Cerchi d’inchiostro applies some Marxian questions to contemporary labour, ownership, data and metrics. Such applications require evidence about present-day technology and cannot be presented as nineteenth-century predictions.

How does Marx differ from later Marxism?

Marx’s writings, later theories that draw on them, revolutionary movements and states acting in his name have distinct histories. A reader must examine their connections without treating them as interchangeable.

Can Marx be read without becoming a Marxist?

Yes. Reading Marx critically involves understanding his arguments about capital, work and history, testing their explanatory reach, and questioning his assumptions and political conclusions. Agreement is not a prerequisite for study.
