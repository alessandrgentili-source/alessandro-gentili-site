Introduction — A Whole Day at Mettray

Knowledge, institutions, normalisation and freedom: from madness to the care of the self.

At Mettray, near Tours, every hour of a boy's day has already been assigned its purpose. There is a time to get up, a time to work, a time to study. Roll call marks the passage of the day; the cleanliness of clothes and bodies is inspected. The young inmates are divided into groups called “families”—a domestic name for an organisation governed by hierarchies and obligations. The colony officially opens on 22 January 1840. In the pages devoted to it in Discipline and Punish, Foucault finds elements of the cloister, the prison, the college and the regiment. He also describes the smaller groups by reference to five organisational models: family, army, workshop, school and judiciary.

The resemblance between these institutions extends beyond their rules. A boy performs a task, receives a correction, and may find that correction entered into a record. Someone else can consult it after his day is over. Education promises to make him capable; discipline seeks to make his conduct predictable. At Mettray both ambitions operate within the same penal regime. Those who assess the boy's progress also belong to the institution that decides how he will be treated.

Foucault places Mettray near the end of his 1975 book and treats its opening as a symbolic date marking the completion of the “carceral system”. The choice is interpretative: these techniques have different histories and did not all arise on a single day. What matters is their concentration within one life. Punishment reaches into hours, gestures and habits; a description of behaviour begins to shape how a person is treated and imagined.

Foucault's account draws on nineteenth-century documents, selected and organised by the author. Those pages do not give us the personal diary of the boy whose day we are following. He is a figure drawn from the procedures described, not an invented individual witness. The distinction matters. A register preserves what an institution was equipped to observe. It is less able to tell us what the boy might have said about his own time.

Examination continues elsewhere, far from punishment. A medical record holds information needed for care; a school register documents learning; a professional appraisal informs a decision to entrust someone with greater responsibility. Such instruments are indispensable, yet their usefulness also depends on whether what they contain can be corrected. A passing judgement may outlive the encounter that produced it and become the premise of the next.

Mettray allows us to ask who has the authority to name a person, on what evidence, and to what effect. That question changes shape across Foucault's work. It first concerns the division between reason and madness and the emergence of the clinical gaze; then the organisation of knowledge and the government of populations. In his final books it reaches those who seek to give form to their own lives. When we return to the boy at Mettray, the question will have changed: what life remains possible beyond the account an institution gives of him?

Michel Foucault at a glance

Name: Michel Foucault. Born: Poitiers, 15 October 1926. Died: Paris, 25 June 1984. Training: philosophy, psychology, the history of knowledge and institutions. Teaching: among other university appointments, the Collège de France, where he held the chair in the History of Systems of Thought from 1970 until his death. Fields: philosophy, history, epistemology, medicine, psychiatry, law, the social sciences, political theory and ethics.

Major works: History of Madness (1961), The Birth of the Clinic (1963), The Order of Things (1966), The Archaeology of Knowledge (1969), Discipline and Punish (1975), The Will to Knowledge (1976), The Use of Pleasure and The Care of the Self (1984).

Central question: how particular ways of producing truth about individuals become entangled with practices of government and with the forms through which people come to recognise themselves.

Why read him: he helps us see the possible distance between an institution's declared purpose and the actual effects of its procedures on people's lives. He also makes it possible to examine freedom as a situated practice, within relations of power that do not disappear by decree.

Why Foucault matters

Faced with a law, we can ask who enacted it and what limits should govern its application. Faced with the arrangement of a classroom, a medical record or a prison form, authority is less readily visible. Architecture determines who can see whom; a form establishes which information may circulate; an assessment scale makes people who have lived through different circumstances appear comparable. Foucault taught us to read the history of power through these ordinary objects too.

That shift of attention changes what we think needs studying. To understand a diagnosis, we must reconstruct how a particular condition became observable. To understand prison, we must follow the techniques of surveillance, correction and record-keeping that organise a prisoner's day. The intentions of reformers matter, but their effects do not always coincide with those intentions. A less cruel punishment may be accompanied by more extensive scrutiny of conduct.

This inquiry ranges across medicine, the history of psychiatry, political theory, studies of sexuality and ethics. It offers no single explanation that can be applied unchanged to every age. Instead, it offers questions that alter as new material comes into view: what is made visible, who is authorised to speak, how a classification acquires force, and what relations to truth allow us to change our lives.

The human question: being known, being judged

A doctor listens to symptoms, orders tests and interprets the results. A student submits an assignment that will be marked and retained. A judge must distinguish facts, testimony and responsibility. In each situation, someone is made an object of knowledge so that action becomes possible: care can be offered, learning recognised, a ruling given on an offence. Without these operations, many institutions could scarcely fulfil their purpose.

Yet knowledge often outlives the encounter that produced it. A difficulty noted at school can follow a student into later assessments; an incident becomes a precedent; a prediction based on scant evidence acquires the weight of a personal trait. Foucault directs part of his attention to this extension of judgement: how does an authorised description of an individual come into being, what makes it credible, and who can change its effects?

The question concerns those who know and judge as well. Professionals work with inherited categories, available instruments and priorities set by institutions. Using these well demands expertise; recognising their history and limits demands a further exercise of judgement. The tension running through this guide begins here, between the need to describe a life and the possibility of reducing it to what a description can capture. In the comparison with Sophocles and Beccaria, the limits of authority and the reasons that might justify punishment will then come to the fore.

Essential biography

Born in Poitiers in 1926, Foucault began studying at the École normale supérieure in Paris in 1946. His intellectual formation unfolded amid phenomenology, the history of science, psychoanalysis, Marxism and the transformations of post-war French philosophy. Georges Canguilhem, whose work addressed the history of concepts and the distinction between the normal and the pathological, was particularly important to him.

After academic and cultural appointments abroad as well as in France, he taught at Clermont-Ferrand and at the University of Vincennes, among other institutions. In 1970 he delivered his inaugural lecture at the Collège de France, published as The Order of Discourse. His public activity became entwined with campaigns concerning prisons and forms of exclusion. He died in 1984, leaving books published during his lifetime, lectures and other materials that would undergo a long subsequent editorial history.

His biography does not mechanically explain his books. It does, however, provide the setting for a question pursued in archives, classrooms and public debate alike: who may speak about whom, with what authority, and under what conditions?

The life that illuminates the work

Before writing History of Madness, Foucault worked and studied in settings close to psychology and psychiatry. In Georges Canguilhem's teaching he encountered a way of examining the history of knowledge: a scientific category has conditions of emergence and use that are worth reconstructing. Illness, the normal and the pathological thus become historical questions as well as specialist terms.

In 1971 he helped found the Groupe d'information sur les prisons (GIP). The group sought testimony from prisoners and their families, information often absent from official accounts of prison life. Theoretical work on imprisonment and public activism developed in the same period; Discipline and Punish appeared in 1975. This was also a significant methodological moment. An administrative archive speaks from the institution's position; a witness can challenge its version of events.

In his final lectures, the study of antiquity and practices of the self broadened this question of who gets to speak. Foucault examined what it means to govern oneself, to listen to a teacher, and to risk something by speaking truthfully. The inquiry into those who describe us came to meet an inquiry into the work through which we attempt to transform ourselves.

France: institutions and their critics

Foucault worked in a France marked by occupation, the Resistance, reconstruction, colonial wars and struggles over the authority of the state. During the 1960s and 1970s, universities, social movements and public discussions of sexuality, psychiatry, education and imprisonment changed profoundly. May 1968 was decisive to the intellectual climate, although Foucault's inquiry had begun long before and cannot be explained by that single moment.

There was also a much longer historical setting: the development of hospitals, changing European penal practices, transformations in medicine, population statistics and techniques for organising work. Foucault looks in this material for what grand narratives of progress tend to relegate to the margins. Reform may diminish cruelty while devising new forms of observation; a right may limit sovereign power while other instruments reorganise its everyday exercise.

Fields of inquiry

Philosophy and the history of concepts. Archaeology reconstructs the conditions under which madness, disease or “man” can become objects of knowledge. It asks what history lies behind what a discipline takes to be self-evident.

Medicine and psychiatry. The clinical gaze distinguishes symptoms and signs; the history of madness also addresses the institutional separation of people. Medical judgement has a cognitive and therapeutic function, alongside a position of authority that may itself be questioned.

Law and penal history. Regulations and assessments of conduct operate alongside the sentence that establishes legal responsibility. Here the time of a legally determined penalty becomes distinct from the time of continuous observation.

Education and work. Exercise develops an ability; examination records that ability and measures it against a standard. The difficulty arises when the standard also determines who a person may become. The module on apprenticeship returns to this tension.

Politics and administration. Populations enter the analysis through epidemics, movement and statistical frequencies. Government can act by changing the conditions in which events occur, as well as by issuing commands.

Ethics and the history of subjectivity. Practices of the self ask how people work upon their conduct and the truths through which they recognise themselves. This offers another route into the relation between capacity and government.

History of Madness: separating and listening

In 1656 a decree established the Hôpital général in Paris. The name can mislead. Foucault examines it chiefly as an institution of confinement and social order, in which very different conditions and categories—from poverty to unreason—were brought together. His inquiry thus moves from modern diagnosis to an earlier act: separating certain people from the rest of the city and supplying a justification for doing so. Foucault gives this history a sweeping periodisation that later historians would challenge.

The image of a ship of fools also appears near the beginning of History of Madness. It moves through literature and painting, carrying figures sent away from the communities to which they belonged. Whether such voyages amounted to a widespread historical practice is disputed. Separately, Andrew Scull has challenged the strength of the evidence behind Foucault’s larger historical account, particularly the scale of the so-called “great confinement”. The distinction between cultural representation and archival evidence matters precisely because this book presents itself as history.

In the pages on Pinel and the emergence of moral treatment, Foucault examines a scene cherished by narratives of psychiatric progress: the removal of chains. More humane material conditions may coexist with new forms of authority exercised over behaviour, speech and the recognition of illness. Foucault's interpretation is disputed; it neither negates the value of treatment nor warrants treating every institutional change as violence in a new guise.

The 1961 book therefore places two tasks before the reader. We must test how far the documentation supports its broad historical claims and, at the same time, observe how a body of knowledge may acquire the right to speak about another person's suffering. As confinement gives way to psychiatric expertise, the form of authority changes. How much room remains within it for the voice of the person being described?

The Birth of the Clinic: making the body legible

The preface to The Birth of the Clinic (1963) sets two medical descriptions beside one another. In the eighteenth century, the physician Pomme describes subjecting a patient to prolonged baths and interprets what he observes through a theory of the drying of tissues. Less than a century later, Bayle describes lesions and membranes in the brain in minute detail. Both physicians speak about the body, yet their words disclose different worlds of what counts as observation.

Foucault dwells on that difference. The eye never works without language: locating a lesion requires categories, instruments and anatomical practices capable of relating what can be seen to what counts as evidence. Between the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, medicine, hospitals and the circumstances in which disease could be observed were transformed. The clinical gaze acquired a new precision, with real consequences for medical knowledge and care.

In the room where a doctor records symptoms, that history has a human dimension. The patient brings an experience of pain; the professional has the expertise to interpret it and the tools to turn it into a diagnosable case. The difference in their positions is often essential to treatment. It becomes troubling when experience that falls outside the classification ceases to be heard. An archaeology of the clinical gaze does not establish whether a particular diagnosis is correct. It reveals the conditions under which diagnoses come into being and gain authority.

The Order of Things and The Archaeology of Knowledge: the conditions of what can be said

The Order of Things (1966) opens with a fantastic classification, attributed to a Chinese encyclopaedia in a story by Borges, which gathers animals under headings that bewilder the European reader. What seems impossible is more than a list: it is a way of placing things together within a common order. Shortly afterwards Foucault turns to Velázquez's Las Meninas, with its painter, the figures in the painting, the mirror at the back and the position of the viewer. The arrangement of positions itself becomes material for thought. Who sees, who is seen, and who belongs to the representation?

The book travels through general grammar, natural history and the analysis of wealth to show how the conditions that make an object of knowledge thinkable can change. The modern figure of “man”, both the subject who knows and the object of the human sciences, also has a history. The celebrated closing image of his possible disappearance concerns this contingent configuration of knowledge; it does not foretell the biological extinction of humanity.

In The Archaeology of Knowledge (1969), Foucault offers a more systematic account of how such transformations might be described. A statement has its place within rules of formation, positions from which speech becomes possible, and institutions that sustain particular discursive practices. Collecting similar sentences is not enough. We must understand why a particular assertion could exist as an object of knowledge at a particular moment. This attention to the rules of discourse would prepare further questions when Foucault began to follow struggles and the material instruments of power more closely.

Archaeology, genealogy, problematisation

Archaeology arises from the need to explain a difference such as the one between Pomme and Bayle: their medical gazes do not share the same field of objects and statements. Describing those historical conditions means asking what a body of knowledge is able to say, rather than treating its history merely as a procession of great discoveries.

Genealogy gains prominence when Foucault turns to the archives of punitive institutions. Rules, bodies, exercises and resistance shift the inquiry towards contingency and relations of force. Nietzsche is a decisive interlocutor in this critique of necessary origins: an institution can arise from an entanglement of projects, conflicts and failures, without following the unified design subsequently attributed to it.

With problematisation, in his later reflections, Foucault turns to the moment when conduct becomes something to be questioned ethically and worked upon. He looks to ancient texts for the reasons why particular pleasures and practices became matters for thought and transformation, rather than for an immutable code. These three approaches do not constitute a sequence of sealed historical periods. Their differences emerge more clearly when we attend to how the documents, questions and objects of the inquiry change.

Discipline and Punish: from punishment to the carceral system

The opening page of Discipline and Punish takes the reader to Paris in 1757. Robert-François Damiens has been condemned for attempting to kill the king. The sentence prescribes a public torture in meticulous detail. The accounts Foucault reproduces also describe the executioners' difficulty in carrying it out and the length of the condemned man's suffering. His body is where monarchical power intends to make itself visible.

A few pages later, the reader encounters a nineteenth-century timetable for an institution housing young prisoners: waking, prayer, instruction, work, meals and rest. The contrast is startling. Punishment can now resemble the orderly administration of a day; authority settles into a succession of gestures, positions, observations and corrections. It would be historically mistaken to treat these two documents as proof of a uniform, linear transformation in every country. Foucault arranges them at the threshold of a genealogy of punishment.

The book's four parts—Torture, Punishment, Discipline, Prison—prevent us from stopping at the figure of the Panopticon. Reformers sought to curb cruelty and arbitrariness; the prison incorporated techniques of training that had also arisen and been used elsewhere. The underlying problem then becomes the persistence of imprisonment: why is an institution repeatedly criticised for its failures so difficult to replace? Foucault looks for an answer partly in the social effects of classification and the differential management of illegalities.

Public torture, reform and prison

After the spectacle of Damiens, eighteenth-century penal reform brings a question that would transform criminal law: who may punish, under what law, and with what proportion between offence and penalty? Criticism of torture and arbitrariness had real institutional and human consequences. Foucault also observes that a change in legal justification does not, by itself, explain how imprisonment became the dominant solution.

Reformers envisaged penalties that would make the relation between act and consequence intelligible. Prison, meanwhile, organises a duration: the convicted person's time becomes material for exercise, surveillance and transformation. Punishment can continue as treatment of conduct and personality. The difference between the principle of legal restraint and the techniques that fill the prisoner's daily life makes the comparison with Beccaria necessary, without attributing to him answers to an inquiry written two centuries later.

Discipline: space, time, exercise, examination

An orderly line, a repeated exercise, a correction entered in the margin of a notebook. None of these acts is, in itself, a punishment. Foucault studies the particular role such operations acquire when they are coordinated to distribute bodies, occupy time and make differences in performance measurable. Discipline requires assigned places, sequences, hierarchies, observation and repeated tests.

Its most revealing feature is the joining of ability and docility. Exercise makes someone more skilled; repetition under a rule can also make them more responsive to commands. Within disciplinary work, these changes may reinforce one another. The criticism therefore cannot consist in condemning every ordered practice as oppressive. We must look at how authority and initiative are distributed, which mistakes can be corrected, and which become recorded evidence that a person is unsuited to a task.

Among the “means of correct training”, Foucault distinguishes hierarchical observation, normalising sanction and examination. The last combines observation, comparison and writing. A test becomes a document; a succession of documents allows an institution to follow an individual over time and formulate judgements about them. The person already present within the institution's field becomes a “case” that can be consulted even in their absence.

The same classroom can contain exercises that prepare students for independence and procedures that principally reward conformity. The distinction becomes visible in what happens after correction. Has the pupil acquired tools for facing an unforeseen situation, or merely learned to reproduce the expected answer without deviation? The Examination and the Apprentice returns to this difference, bringing Foucault's analysis of discipline into conversation with a problem of our own time.

The Panopticon and the making of a case

Jeremy Bentham's design places cells around a central point of observation. Those inside know they can be watched, but cannot tell whether anyone is watching at a given moment. Foucault reads the Panopticon as a diagram of discipline: a particular arrangement of visibility may produce effects even when the supervisor says nothing. Panopticism does not depend on every prison having adopted that architectural plan.

The image has become famous because it allows us to think about dispersed forms of surveillance, including those now mediated by digital instruments. Yet the book contains another mechanism of equal importance: the examination. A performance is measured against a standard; a judgement enters an archive; successive records make it possible to describe an individual even when they are not present. Discipline thus rests on continuity between observation and documentation.

In prison, a person convicted of an offence may also become known through a biography assembled around habits, relationships and predictions of reoffending. Foucault examines the production of delinquency as an object of knowledge and government. Someone convicted for an act risks being followed indefinitely as a human type. Law has established a fact; prison classification claims to describe a life.

Law, norm, delinquency

Criminal law requires the identification of an act, an offence defined by law, the authority competent to judge it, and specified consequences. A normalising sanction can operate even when no crime has occurred. It compares conduct with a standard of behaviour, performance or health, records a deviation and seeks to reduce it. Both can shape the life of the same person, but they tell different stories about what authority considers relevant.

A prisoner has been convicted of a crime. During the sentence, conduct reports and assessments of rehabilitation may help determine the conditions of that person's life and future possibilities. The act that grounded the conviction remains distinct from the personality constructed through continuing observation and judgement. Foucault asks us to examine precisely this extension: how much of someone's life is made to answer for an act that the law had already defined?

The persistence of prison, despite repeated accounts of its failure, is one of the central problems in Discipline and Punish. Foucault advances the thesis that imprisonment also participates in the differential management of illegalities and in the production of an identifiable delinquent milieu. The historical reach of this explanation must be assessed in specific settings and against particular sources. The book thus brings us to the point where judging an act and administering a person become different operations, often entrusted to the same institution.

Oedipus and the juridical forms of truth

Oedipus wants to know who killed Laius. His inquiry sets in motion voices that do not speak from a single source: an oracle, witnesses' accounts, memories and recognitions that complete and contradict one another. An account emerges from the inquiry the ruler himself has set in motion, until the evidence implicates him. In the second of five lectures delivered in Rio de Janeiro in May 1973, later known as Truth and Juridical Forms, Foucault reads Oedipus the King with close attention to this process of establishing what happened.

His interpretation follows the ways knowledge is distributed among different voices and acquires authority through a procedure. Foucault calls inquiry the procedure through which these different voices, taken together, establish what happened. Sophocles' tragedy also concerns the vulnerability of a man who exercises power without knowing the truth of his own life. From the same story, Foucault's lecture draws a historical question about relations between knowledge, government and forms of inquiry.

The reader need not choose between the tragedy and its twentieth-century reinterpretation. Both must remain in view as distinct works of thought. Theatre presents actions, recognition, mistakes and their consequences; the lecture offers a philosophical interpretation whose premises can themselves be disputed. Their difference sharpens a remarkable question: truth may be sought in order to exercise authority and end by changing what that authority means.

Sovereignty, security, biopolitics and governmentality

A city needs to prevent an epidemic, secure its food supply and regulate movement. It can impose prohibitions; it can discipline schedules and behaviour; it can also examine the distribution of events and alter an environment so that some occurrences become less likely. In the lectures of the 1970s, Foucault broadens the analysis of power to this scale: from individual bodies to populations, from specific corrections to the conditions under which collective phenomena unfold.

Sovereignty is associated with law and territory; discipline organises individuals, spaces, time and exercises; apparatuses of security concern series, frequencies, circulation and probabilities. Foucault does not describe a sequence in which each model abolishes its predecessor. In public health policy, for example, a legal obligation can coexist with behavioural protocols and statistical analyses used to allocate resources. These techniques differ in their object, scale and means of intervention.

Biopolitics names intervention into the vital processes of populations. The study of governmentality examines the rationalities and techniques through which conduct is directed. In Security, Territory, Population (1977–78) and The Birth of Biopolitics (1978–79), the history of the arts of government encounters that of liberalism. To govern may mean arranging an environment in which subjects are left to act within possibilities that have, at least partly, been organised in advance.

Here another difficulty emerges. Policy can influence choices without issuing continuous commands: through incentives, costs, circulation and criteria of risk. Inquiry must then specify who establishes those criteria and what effects they have in the cases under consideration. Governmentality loses its explanatory force when it becomes a generic name for everything that conditions social life.

The Will to Knowledge: confession and the subject

In the opening pages of The Will to Knowledge (1976), Foucault unsettles the image of a nineteenth century that did nothing but impose silence about sex. Modernity builds places in which people are required to speak: the confessional, the medical consultation, the interrogation, the setting of educational observation. Words are gathered, compared and interpreted by people invested with specialist authority.

Among other examples, the book recalls the nineteenth-century case of Charles Jouy, a rural worker involved in sexual acts with young girls and subsequently subjected to medico-judicial examination and classification. Foucault follows the way an episode is converted into questioning, reports and specialist knowledge. The violence or vulnerability involved must not vanish because we are interested in the apparatus: the history of classification cannot exhaust what needs to be said about the people affected. That limit also helps us read Foucault critically.

Confession reveals another side of the problem. Speaking about oneself can bring help and recognition, but the truth one declares is often entrusted to someone else to interpret. The book thus connects knowledge about sexuality, individual disciplines and the regulation of populations. It raises a question that would accompany Foucault's later work: if people are encouraged to recognise themselves in truths produced about them, how is the relation formed through which they can work upon themselves? The move towards ancient ethics arises partly from this question.

The Use of Pleasure and The Care of the Self: freedom as practice

In Xenophon's Oeconomicus, one of the works Foucault reads in The Use of Pleasure, Ischomachus describes how he instructed his young wife in the management of the household. Her education concerns the distribution of tasks, order and responsibility. The man who governs his own conduct is also understood as someone capable of governing property and family. The scene immediately reveals the historical limit of that ideal: the ethical work of the free man unfolds within hierarchies that assign unequal positions to women and enslaved people.

In 1984, with The Use of Pleasure and The Care of the Self, The History of Sexuality took a different shape from the project announced in its first volume. Foucault asks how someone comes to recognise themselves as a moral subject. In Greek and Roman ethics, he studies regimes of bodily conduct, family relations, the advice of teachers, exercises and care of the self. Behaviour acquires meaning through the work people undertake upon their conduct, the reasons that guide them and the kind of life to which they aspire.

Care of the self entails exercise, time, truth and relations with other people. Its history differs from contemporary forms of individual wellness: many of the texts Foucault examines address free and socially privileged men. They cannot therefore serve as a moral blueprint to be applied without mediation.

In his final lectures, devoted in part to parrhesia, speaking truth may mean risking a confrontation with someone powerful. From the study of the classified person, Foucault has reached a question about someone attempting to change their relation to truth, others and themselves. This enlargement does not erase institutional constraints. It changes how the possibilities of freedom can be questioned.

The lectures: a workshop, not a unified posthumous work

Foucault's Collège de France lectures show him testing and revising hypotheses in the course of presenting them. Penal Theories and Institutions (1971–72) and The Punitive Society (1972–73) prepare several questions later explored in the book on prisons. Psychiatric Power (1973–74) and Abnormal (1974–75) examine authority exercised through diagnosis, expertise and categories of deviance. Society Must Be Defended (1975–76) broadens the inquiry towards war, state racism and power over life.

With Security, Territory, Population and The Birth of Biopolitics, attention shifts to rationalities of government, liberalism and the conditions within which populations act. Later courses, including On the Government of the Living, The Hermeneutics of the Subject, The Government of Self and Others and The Courage of Truth, show the investigation of government meeting the study of truth practices and the constitution of the subject.

The years given here refer to the lectures as delivered, not to the publication of the volumes. The Collège de France courses were published as books after Foucault’s death, edited from recordings, manuscripts and surviving notes. Reading them alongside the books issued in Foucault's lifetime reveals shifts and discontinuities. Treating them as a finished, unified work ordered by their author would erase the very quality of experimentation that makes them valuable.

Cerchi d’inchiostro — Module I — How a Case Is Made

A person fails to attend three appointments at a local service office. The register contains three absences; the box for explanations is blank. Imagine, to make the mechanism visible, that the first appointment was missed because public transport changed its timetable, and that notice of the second was sent to a telephone number no longer in use. The third absence truly remains unexplained. If an official consults only the summary, the three incidents become a uniform series. This is a hypothetical case, devised to test Foucault's account of examination.

The analysis in Discipline and Punish allows us to distinguish six stages in this transformation. The sequence is a reading tool developed by Cerchi d’inchiostro, not a classification proposed by Foucault.

Observe. Detect an event: an absence the service needs to record. Select. Decide what goes into the document: the dates, certainly; perhaps not the circumstances. Compare. Give the series a weight in relation to expected attendance. Three operations useful to the continuity of the service may already have changed how the events will be interpreted.

Record. The event is detached from the moment in which it happened. Another official can read the file without having met the person or knowing about the disrupted transport or the incorrect contact details.

Infer. From the sequence of absences, someone concludes that the person is “uncooperative” or “at risk of dropping out”. Such an inference may help provide targeted support. It becomes fragile when presented as a stable trait of personality.

Intervene. A manager decides whether to contact the person, check the information, arrange a new appointment or apply a measure allowed by the rules. The document informs that choice, but does not in itself justify it.

The delicate point lies between recording and inferring. Once recorded, a fact can outlast the experience that produced it; an interpretation may later be taken for the fact itself. A trustworthy archive must be able to accommodate new information, corrections and explanations. Examination provides continuity of knowledge; the institution is answerable for the meaning and consequences it assigns to the traces it preserves.

Cerchi d’inchiostro — Module II — Three Ways of Governing an Infraction

Students from several classes are repeatedly arriving late at a school. The rules provide for warnings; some teachers propose exercises designed to foster responsibility; the school leadership discovers that the timetable of a transport service does not fit the start of lessons. This is a hypothetical situation. It helps distinguish three ways of responding to an observable problem without transferring the historical meaning of penal sovereignty to a school.

Foucault's distinctions between sovereignty, discipline and security, developed in different works, can be tested by examining how the questions asked about the same late arrivals change.

Rules do not become unnecessary because circumstances outside the school contribute to lateness; nor does changing the bus timetable make the learning of punctuality redundant. All three logics may coexist. What differs is what each counts as evidence, whom it addresses and what kind of mistake it may make. The table is a comparison constructed for this guide; its purpose is to separate questions that an indiscriminate measure might conflate.

The comparison with Beccaria will matter when the legitimate exercise of the power to punish is at issue. Here Foucault helps us see that, even when no penalty is imposed, decisions remain about individual conduct and the circumstances in which it takes shape.

Cerchi d’inchiostro — Module III — The File and the Signature

Imagine a family applying for a municipal grant towards school transport. One office receives the application. Another checks the supporting documents; a blank field is converted into the category “eligibility not demonstrated”. A third office prepares the refusal. The final document bears an authorised officer's signature: formally, we know who issued the decision. Yet explaining why that field was left blank, who decided it was decisive and who could have requested further information means retracing a chain of steps. This is a hypothetical case.

The file preserves an administrative history. It may, however, lose the history of interpretations that turned information into a consequence. A signature identifies an authority; by itself it does not tell us how the decision was made. Foucault's genealogy invites us to look further upstream, where procedures and forms of knowledge make a person describable. The question of publicly justifying the outcome is then posed in legal and civic terms belonging to our contemporary comparison.

Three essays by Alessandro Gentili help distinguish what is at stake along the way. In Chi decide il Sole? (Who Decides the Sun?, essay in Italian), the question is how selection determines what a decision-maker can see: on the application, the filled-in field becomes more visible than the circumstance never recorded. Fenomenologia del potere (Phenomenology of Power, in Italian) examines the language through which institutions turn conflicts and choices into processes, procedures and formulas of compatibility: “eligibility not demonstrated” can sound conclusive even when it arises from a simple gap in the evidence gathered. Governi senza firma (Governments Without a Signature, in Italian) follows responsibility as it disperses across administrative levels and competencies: the existence of a final signatory does not settle who is answerable for the intermediate steps.

Reading a file thus requires four questions, distinct from the sequence through which a case is made in the preceding module: the provenance of information, its translation into categories, the consequence authorised by the decision, and the possibility of challenge by the person concerned. Here the last concerns legal and administrative safeguards—access to reasons, correction, defence, review—and does not coincide with Foucault's notion of counter-conduct.

A file may accurately record that an application field was blank, yet the decision drawn from it can still be challenged. If the family proves that the supposedly missing document had already been submitted, the administration must be able to correct its decision. That possibility measures something which procedural regularity alone cannot ensure: responsibility for reconstructing, and when necessary correcting, the path from record to signature.

Cerchi d’inchiostro — Module IV — The Examination and the Apprentice

A young sales analyst prepares a report on sales performance. An artificial intelligence system organises the data, draws the charts and proposes a persuasive conclusion: the market has grown. The work passes formal review, her manager approves it and the client receives it. On a second assignment, however, she encounters a less straightforward comparison: the definition of an “active customer” has changed between the two periods. The graph still looks coherent; the conclusion may no longer be sound. This is a hypothetical example. The decisive issue is the ability to interrupt a process that produces an apparently good result.

In Essay 15, Il mondo senza apprendisti (A World Without Apprentices, in Italian), Alessandro Gentili distinguishes assisted performance from embodied competence. The first is what can be delivered within a particular architecture of support; the second is what a person can explain, adapt, transfer and correct when the context changes. The essay acknowledges documented benefits of generative assistance, but warns that organisations may count a beginner’s assisted output as productivity and, in the process, use up the opportunities in which that beginner would learn to work without such assistance.

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault studies exercises that improve skill while training people to respond to a standard. Examination records what is done and helps define who is regarded as capable. The contemporary problem lies in this second operation: who has the power to declare the analyst trained, and what evidence authorises that judgement? An approved chart shows that a task produced an accepted result. It says much less about what will happen when data are incomplete or a definition changes.

Cerchi d’inchiostro distinguishes three tests of formation, developed in dialogue with Essay 15 rather than taken as a taxonomy from Foucault.

Execution. Can someone deliver an adequate result using the expected tools? This test documents performance and makes it possible to assess the work produced.

Understanding. Can they explain how the sources were chosen, what comparison supports the conclusion and why a classification might be unreliable? Review with an experienced colleague reveals an ability that the finished chart does not display.

Deliberate departure. When the definition of a variable changes, can the person stop, reconstruct the conditions of comparison, correct the work and ask for help at the right point? This ability to depart from a familiar procedure is the most demanding test because it requires practice across different cases, tolerable mistakes and time to learn.

Training cannot demand instant independence from someone still learning. It requires observation, attempts, corrections and support. Some tasks can be performed with assistance; others must allow the beginner to demonstrate what they can do when that shortcut fails. An assessment capable of recognising both circumstances increases the organisation's responsibility towards those it trains.

Triad 8, which brings together Alessandro Manzoni, Carlo Collodi and Pier Paolo Pasolini, offers a distinct counterpoint: Pinocchio learns through mistakes in Collodi’s story; Pasolini examines how an environment can educate its inhabitants; Manzoni exposes judgement to the failures of institutions. These are three different situations. None coincides with Foucault's account of discipline; together they remind us that the effects of education are measured in the life that follows the examination. For the analyst, that moment arrives when the model proposes a convincing conclusion and she must find the professional courage to stop it.

Key ideas — What changes after Foucault

  1. Truth has historical conditions of production. Knowing a person requires procedures, vocabularies and institutions; their criteria can themselves be examined.
  2. Power also works through abilities. Organising behaviour can increase efficiency while narrowing the space for autonomy in the same movement.
  3. The effects of reform exceed its intentions. Making punishment less cruel does not prevent new forms of control from emerging.
  4. An individual becomes a case when their life can be documented. Examination links observation, normalisation and the archive.
  5. Law and the disciplinary norm raise different questions. A practice that is formally lawful may still demand scrutiny of its proportionality, effects and contestability.
  6. Governing also means arranging conditions. Security, risk and statistics become tools for influencing collective events.
  7. Confession produces a historical relation to the self. Telling the truth about oneself can be both an experience of speaking and a governed practice.
  8. The subject is not a fixed figure. Foucault's later work opens the question of practices of freedom and of changing one's relation to oneself.

Key concepts — How they work

Archaeology. The analysis of the historical rules that make certain statements and objects of knowledge possible. It distinguishes what an era is able to think from the opinions of any individual author.

Genealogy. An inquiry into the contingencies, practices and relations of force through which institutions and regimes of truth are formed. Its debt to Nietzsche lies above all in the critique of necessary origins and reassuring narratives.

Knowledge/power. A relation in which practices of knowing make interventions possible, while relations of power foster the production and authority of certain forms of knowledge. It does not follow that a scientific proposition is false because it has a social setting.

Discipline. A technology for governing individual bodies, organised around time, space, exercises, observation and correction. It can produce both skill and docility.

Examination. The combination of hierarchical observation and normalising sanction that documents the individual and makes them comparable, describable and assessable over time.

Normalisation. A process in which a norm of behaviour, health or performance guides comparisons and interventions. Foucault's different analyses require us to distinguish disciplinary normalisation from mechanisms of security.

Dispositif (apparatus). A heterogeneous assemblage of practices, discourses, institutions, rules and instruments with a strategic function in a historical field. The concept is developed particularly in the genealogical analyses and clarified in interventions and interviews.

Panopticism. A mode of exercising power illustrated by Bentham's model: the asymmetrical possibility of being observed produces effects on conduct.

Delinquency. A category and milieu socially produced and administered through the relations between prison, policing and criminological knowledge; not a neutral synonym for every crime committed.

Biopower and biopolitics. The analysis of techniques that take the life of bodies and populations as objects of government, through individual disciplines and the regulation of collective phenomena.

Governmentality. A field of rationalities and techniques through which the direction of conduct can be studied, especially in connection with the emergence of modern arts of government and liberalism.

Subjectivation. The process through which ways of being a subject are formed, including through relations with practices of truth and work upon oneself.

Care of the self. A historically variable family of ethical practices through which a person works upon their conduct; not a contemporary formula for self-help.

Parrhesia. An ethical and political form of truth-telling in which frankness may expose the speaker to risk; in the later lectures it also becomes a question concerning the relation between subject and government.

Constellation — Interlocutors, disputes, inheritances

Before: inherited questions

Nietzsche is a decisive interlocutor for genealogy. In On the Genealogy of Morality, guilt and punishment have histories that unsettle explanations constructed after the fact. Foucault brings that suspicion of necessary origins into the archives of institutional practice. Guide 05 to Nietzsche helps distinguish a philosophical debt from a difference in method.

Bentham provides the design of the Panopticon; Beccaria, within the history of eighteenth-century reform, raises the question of the legitimacy and proportionality of punishment. Kant returns in reflections on the conditions of knowledge and, in the later Foucault, on a philosophy of the present. Marx offers a point of comparison between discipline and the productive usefulness of bodies; Weber, between discipline and administrative rationalisation. Hobbes offers a model of sovereignty from which Foucault's microphysics of power differs.

During: teachers, interlocutors, controversies

Georges Canguilhem is fundamental to the history of concepts and to the question of the normal and the pathological. The comparison with Sartre concerns the subject and the figure of the intellectual; that with Deleuze, relations between power, desire and transformation. Derrida challenges Foucault's reading of Descartes in History of Madness, opening a dispute over the premises of the separation between reason and madness.

Through the Groupe d'information sur les prisons, prisoners become direct participants in an inquiry into the conditions of confinement. Their testimony introduces a kind of knowledge that an administrative account cannot supply on its own.

After: questions others put to Foucault

Judith Butler rethinks the normative constitution of the subject and the possibilities of transforming norms. Nancy Fraser asks what reasons justify criticism of the forms of power being described. Jürgen Habermas challenges the conditions of validity of genealogy. Ann Laura Stoler questions the place of colonialism in Foucault's history of sexuality. In each of these relationships, inheritance also involves disagreement.

The present: productive differences within Cerchi d’inchiostro

Triad 4—Freud, Gramsci, Hobbes—distinguishes the unconscious, hegemony and sovereignty from discipline. Triad 6—Cicero, Tocqueville, Condorcet—raises the question of public safeguards, participation and education. Triad 8—Manzoni, Collodi, Pasolini—examines formation and judgement through history, experience and environment.

Among Alessandro Gentili's essays, Fenomenologia del potere examines institutional language; Governi senza firma, the attribution of responsibility for distributed decisions; Chi decide il Sole?, the conditions that make something visible; and Il mondo senza apprendisti, the development of competence. The File and the Signature and The Examination and the Apprentice pursue the closest connections. This constellation brings distinct arguments into relation without inventing direct intellectual influences.

Legacy and reception

One measure of a philosophical concept's fertility is whether it allows other researchers to see a new object. In 1995, David Armstrong's “The rise of surveillance medicine” described a shift in twentieth-century medicine: attention extends beyond a disease located in a patient's body towards the monitoring of populations regarded as healthy, risk factors and the redefinition of normality. It is a specific example of how Foucault's question about the medical gaze opened inquiries extending beyond the nineteenth-century clinic.

In 1999, Nikolas Rose's Powers of Freedom examined forms of government that also operate through capacities distributed among states, professionals, families and organisations. Governmentality thus became a way of studying the rationalities of public policy without attributing every effect to a single command. Later uses of the term still require the identification of particular instruments and historical circumstances: a theoretical word cannot take the place of research.

The reception of Foucault’s ideas has also prompted criticism of his own work. In Race and the Education of Desire (1995), Ann Laura Stoler returned to his history of sexuality and asked what changes when colonialism and racialisation enter the account rather than remaining at its margins. Elsewhere, Foucault's tools have been used to study prisons, health institutions, gender and digital environments. The result is a history of applications, revisions and disputes. The Constellation identifies interlocutors; the legacy shows what their studies actually did with the problems Foucault had opened.

The contemporary wound — When risk takes the place of guilt

A public authority wants to identify possible fraud in the provision of benefits and subsidies. It uses SyRI (Systeem Risico Indicatie), a system established under Dutch law to combine data and flag situations for closer attention. Someone classified within a risk profile may not know which criteria produced that classification. The difficulty then concerns the possibility of understanding why one has become the object of an investigation, whatever its eventual outcome. This is not the biography of an individual citizen. It is the problem posed by the system's opacity and examined in court.

On 5 February 2020, the District Court of The Hague ruled that the legal framework governing SyRI was incompatible with Article 8(2) of the European Convention on Human Rights. The judgment declared particular provisions non-binding in the form then in force, because of insufficient transparency and controllability and an inadequate balance between anti-fraud objectives and the protection of private life. It did not prohibit statistical risk analysis in general: it concerned the safeguards afforded by that framework. A risk signal is not proof of wrongdoing.

What matters for this guide is the passage from a recorded event to a prediction and then to an intervention. An authority holds information collected for specified purposes; a procedure combines it; the result makes certain people appear more deserving of scrutiny. If that classification comes to be read as a stable quality of a person, the distinction between probability and responsibility narrows. Foucault's analysis of knowledge/power helps us understand how this visibility is constructed; the legal standards needed to justify a restriction of rights, however, must come from other sources, including the court's judgment.

The distance between a file and the life to which it refers can be narrowed only if those concerned can learn and challenge the reasons for an intervention, and if decision-makers can actually reconstruct the path taken by the data. This is where Triad 14 holds three problems apart. In Sophocles, authority risks discovering too late what its decision has done. Foucault shows how procedures construct objects of knowledge. Beccaria asks what limits must constrain the exercise of punitive power. In none of these cases does a label exhaust the question of guilt.

Limits and controversies

Historical accounts under examination

History of Madness has profoundly influenced how the history of psychiatry is studied. It has also faced serious documentary criticism, including that of the historian Andrew Scull, concerning the use of sources, the extent of the so-called great confinement and certain generalisations about Europe. Subsequent historiography has revealed a greater variety of local practices. When a historical account encounters contrary documents, its philosophical value cannot by itself shield it from revision.

The periodisation in Discipline and Punish is also debated. Changes in punishment and the spread of imprisonment were not uniform across states; law, religion, economics, administration and politics followed different paths. The book is powerful in its choice and comparison of practices, but should not be made into a complete, self-sufficient chronicle of modern punishment.

On what grounds can power be criticised?

One philosophical difficulty concerns the normative criteria of criticism. If we can show that truths and institutions take historically particular forms, why judge a given relation of power illegitimate? Nancy Fraser and Jürgen Habermas, with different arguments, insisted on making the reasons for criticism explicit. That does not make Foucault a relativist for whom every judgement is interchangeable: his work takes positions against particular historical practices. But the relation between those interventions and a general theory of rights or justice remains open to dispute.

Who is missing from the archive?

Narratives based on institutional archives can reproduce the standpoint of those who had the power to write. Foucault's attention to excluded voices does not resolve every question of representation. Feminist and postcolonial scholarship has shown the need to confront differences of gender and race, empire and colonial forms of government more directly, including places where his account of European modernity risks becoming a general measure of history.

Care of the self is not equally available to everyone

The ancient ethics explored in Foucault's final books were historically bound to social hierarchies and unequal conditions of access. Their interest in self-formation does not make them contemporary models to be recovered without criticism. Freedom does not grow simply because it is presented as personal exercise; rights, resources and relationships matter to the possibility of practising it.

The value of a Foucauldian reading survives when it accepts such objections as questions for research. It weakens when every criticism is explained away in advance as yet another effect of power, placing the philosopher beyond the possibility of being proved wrong.

Revealing details

A chair devoted to systems of thought. At the Collège de France, Foucault taught the history of systems of thought. The name suggests why his lectures crossed medicine, law, economics and antiquity without settling permanently within a single discipline.

A group that sought prisoners' voices. In 1971, the Groupe d'information sur les prisons collected testimony and questionnaires about prison conditions. It also shifted attention towards the origin of public speech: who gets to describe prison?

A preface built around two medical descriptions. The Birth of the Clinic opens with Pomme and Bayle, separated by less than a century: a contrast revealing how theory changes what the eye believes it sees.

A course title that can mislead. The Birth of Biopolitics (1978–79) is largely devoted to the history of liberal and neoliberal rationalities. Course titles need to be read alongside the lectures actually delivered.

A fourth volume published decades later. Confessions of the Flesh appeared posthumously in 2018, edited by Frédéric Gros. Its editorial history requires us to distinguish the reconstruction of work an author left behind from the publication of a book authorised in the author's lifetime.

Common misreadings

  • “Foucault said everything is power.” The term is analytically valuable only when it describes specific relations and techniques, rather than becoming a synonym for every social phenomenon.
  • “Disciplinary society is the Panopticon.” Surveillance, examination, hierarchies, registers and normalisation form a much wider field.
  • “Power acts only from above.” Foucault studies dispersed relations and practices without denying the existence of formal institutions and hierarchies.
  • “Conforming behaviour proves successful learning.” A verified result does not, by itself, show whether someone can face a new problem.
  • “All care is a form of domination.” Historical investigations of the conditions of care do not negate the clinical and human value of effective practices.
  • “Foucault opposed all education.” His analysis of discipline calls for scrutiny of exercise and examination; it does not offer an indiscriminate condemnation of teaching.
  • “The modern prison was born on a single day.” The date of Mettray is a symbolic choice within a historical interpretation.
  • “Security replaces discipline, which replaces sovereignty.” These rationalities can coexist and combine.
  • “The ethics of the self proves Foucault abandoned politics.” In the later work, freedom remains tied to practices of government and truth.
  • “Artificial intelligence confirms his prophecies.” Application to digital systems is a contemporary interpretative undertaking that requires independent sources.

Reading pathways

If you have twenty minutes. Read the opening at Mettray, Michel Foucault at a glance, the discussion of discipline and examination, How a Case Is Made, the contemporary wound and the FAQs. This gives a first sense of the method and its consequences without reducing it to the Panopticon.

If you have an hour. Add the map of the works, History of Madness, the relation between law and norm, Oedipus and the Juridical Forms of Truth, The Examination and the Apprentice, and the limits. The questions and controversies will come into a wider view.

If you want to read Foucault himself. Begin with the Discipline and Prison parts of Discipline and Punish, keeping the first two parts in view; then continue with The Will to Knowledge. Return to the archaeological books with the help of a reliable introduction to the method, and approach The Use of Pleasure and The Care of the Self to understand the later questions.

If you study law, public administration or policy. Read Discipline and Punish alongside Truth and Juridical Forms and the lecture courses Security, Territory, Population and The Birth of Biopolitics. For the contemporary discussion, consult the SyRI judgment and the relevant body of rights: analysing power is not a substitute for knowing the law that applies.

If you are interested in education. Start with the chapter on the means of correct training in Discipline and Punish. Then read Il mondo senza apprendisti and ask which exercises develop the capacity for judgement, which confine someone to conformity, and which may do both.

Questions to think with

  1. When does a record make behaviour intelligible, and when does it fix that behaviour into an identity?
  2. What changes when a judgement concerns an act rather than a personality?
  3. Which instruments of observation are indispensable to care, and what safeguards prevent their abuse?
  4. Can an institution cultivate skills and docility at once? Through which practices?
  5. Who chooses the indicators that turn a biography into a risk profile?
  6. How can a provisional classification become a permanent consequence?
  7. Which aspects of Foucault's later ethics help us resist seeing the subject as merely a product of the surrounding environment?
  8. Where does the need to assign responsibility for public decisions expose a limit in Foucault's account?

These are working questions: they call for evidence, comparisons and arguments. Quoting a celebrated sentence will not resolve them.

Points to remember

One place — Mettray. The penal institution makes visible the coexistence of education, work, surveillance and correction. From here the reader can return to the opening scene and the history of prison.

One technique — examination. Observing, comparing and writing turn a performance into a reusable trace. How a Case Is Made follows its consequences.

One distinction — law, norm, risk. Judging an offence, correcting conduct and acting on a probability demand different reasons and safeguards. The second module develops the comparison.

One transformation — from discourse to subjectivity. Archaeology, genealogy, governmentality and ethics do not form a succession of closed systems; they change how we ask about the ways we are constituted and can act.

One test — apprenticeship. A well-executed result does not by itself show how much judgement has become available to the person who produced it. Examination must be able to face the test of autonomy.

One limit — responsibility. Reconstructing how a judgement is produced is not enough to justify its effects. The possibility of challenging it belongs to the civic quality of institutions.

Frequently asked questions

1. Who was Michel Foucault?

Michel Foucault (1926–1984) was a French philosopher and historian who studied the formation of knowledge, institutions and the practices through which human beings are observed, governed and constituted as subjects. He taught at the Collège de France and took part in public debates, particularly concerning prisons and exclusion.

2. What are Foucault's major works?

His books published during his lifetime include History of Madness (1961), The Birth of the Clinic (1963), The Order of Things (1966), The Archaeology of Knowledge (1969), Discipline and Punish (1975), The Will to Knowledge (1976), The Use of Pleasure and The Care of the Self (1984). The Collège de France lecture courses, published later, broaden this picture.

3. What does knowledge/power mean?

It means that the production of knowledge can be bound up with the practical conditions for acting upon individuals, and that relations of power can favour certain forms of knowledge. The concept does not imply that science is always a lie or that all knowledge is merely propaganda.

4. Why did Foucault study timetables, registers and exercises?

Because these instruments allow institutions to organise conduct and retain information about individuals. In Discipline and Punish, their use can develop skills and docility in the same movement. The question is what opportunities for autonomy they actually create.

5. Is the Panopticon the model for every modern prison?

No. It is a design by Bentham that Foucault uses as a diagram of possible surveillance: those being observed cannot know when the gaze is actually upon them. His analysis concerns the logic of asymmetrical visibility, without assuming that all prisons adopted the design.

6. What is the difference between law and norm?

Law establishes requirements and legal consequences through defined authorities and procedures. In Foucault's analysis of discipline, norms also operate through standards of behaviour, health or performance. The two can act together, but they raise different questions about responsibility and correction.

7. Why did Foucault criticise prison?

In Discipline and Punish, he examines the spread of modern imprisonment, the disciplinary techniques at work within it, and the production of knowledge about delinquency. He also questions why prison persists despite repeated criticism of its effectiveness. His genealogy is not simply a programme for legislative reform.

8. Why were health statistics important to Foucault?

Because they make it possible to act on distributions of events and collective phenomena—mortality, epidemics and patterns of movement—without intervening in the same way upon each person. The study of biopolitics distinguishes this scale from techniques directed at individual bodies.

9. Can governments govern without imposing prohibitions?

Yes. An authority can shape choices by arranging incentives, spaces, information and conditions of access. In his work on governmentality, Foucault studies these ways of directing conduct, especially in the history of liberalism.

10. What is the relationship between Foucault and Oedipus the King?

In the second of his 1973 lectures in Rio de Janeiro, collected as Truth and Juridical Forms, Foucault reads Oedipus the King through practices of inquiry and the production of truth. This is a documented engagement with Sophocles, distinct from interpretations that privilege the psychoanalytic dimension.

11. Did Foucault believe every educational institution was oppressive?

No. His analysis shows how many educational practices employ disciplinary techniques that can increase both ability and conformity. Evaluating a school nevertheless requires attention to learning, exercise, room for mistakes, autonomy and responsibility. This guide also develops the comparison through Essay 15, Il mondo senza apprendisti.

12. What changes in Foucault's later work?

The books of 1984 and the later lectures explore practices of the self, veridiction and the historical forms of the moral subject. The study of freedom as practice modifies and extends his earlier investigations of power; it does not cancel them.

13. Did Foucault predict digital surveillance and artificial intelligence?

No. He died in 1984 and studied institutions, texts and practices of his own time and earlier periods. His concepts can help us question contemporary digital systems, but their effects, operation and legal regulation require independent evidence.

14. What are the main criticisms of Foucault?

Historians have challenged aspects of his accounts of madness and punishment; philosophers such as Fraser and Habermas have questioned the normative grounds for criticising power; feminist and postcolonial scholars have identified limitations concerning gender, race and colonialism. A rigorous reading distinguishes the value of his questions from the evidential strength of particular answers.

A note on sources and method

This guide distinguishes primary works and documents checked against identifiable passages from interpretations reconstructed through scholarship and reference works. Particular passages were checked directly on the Mettray colony, the means of correct training, the opening of The Birth of the Clinic, confession in The Will to Knowledge, Xenophon's Oeconomicus as discussed in The Use of Pleasure, and the identifying details of the SyRI judgment. Contemporary administrative and educational examples introduced in the modules are expressly hypothetical. The critical studies cited here were not all read in full; where they inform the guide through established scholarship and reference works, their arguments are represented with corresponding caution. This distinction matters especially where historical evidence or philosophical interpretation remains disputed.

Closing reflection — What a File Cannot Decide

At the Mettray colony, the boy goes through a day arranged by others. Exercises show what he can repeat; observations record what is judged to conform; a correction becomes part of the history of his conduct. The register can grow more precise at every stage. Yet that precision belongs to what the institution has chosen to write down.

Foucault followed the transformations of this way of knowing from prison to the clinic, from confession to population statistics. In his final books he also encountered texts in which people deliberately work on how they live, expose themselves to another's words or risk something by speaking the truth. That turn changes how we see the boy: we cannot exhaust who he is in the place assigned to him by a system of observation, however elaborate.

We return, then, to Mettray without claiming to know the voice of a boy whom the documents cited do not allow us to hear. We know that the institution could judge his exercises, correct his mistakes and record his progress. What room did he have to show something the register had never anticipated?
