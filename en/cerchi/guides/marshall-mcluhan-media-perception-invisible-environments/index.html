Marshall McLuhan: media, perception and invisible environments

The most familiar medium can be the hardest to see.

Introduction

For half an hour, the electricity fails in a city neighbourhood. Shop signs go dark; lifts stop; familiar streets acquire unfamiliar edges. Activities that seemed independent reveal a shared condition. The outage makes an environment visible through its absence.

Marshall McLuhan chose electric light for one of the most arresting arguments in Understanding Media. Light can make surgery, night work or an evening game possible without itself conveying a story. The activities matter, yet the enabling medium has effects that cannot be read from any one activity. It changes when things can happen, where they can take place and how people organize their lives around them.

A newspaper invites us to examine its headlines. A telephone call draws attention to its words. McLuhan turns our gaze towards the forms that carry such content, and towards the habits that form around them. What becomes difficult to see when a medium has become the ordinary environment of experience?

McLuhan in five minutes

Herbert Marshall McLuhan (1911–1980) was a Canadian literary scholar and media theorist. Born in Edmonton and raised largely in Winnipeg, he studied at the University of Manitoba and Cambridge, where he completed a doctorate in 1942 on Thomas Nashe and the tradition of grammar, dialectic and rhetoric. His work on language, literary form and modernism preceded his studies of advertising, print, radio and television.

He became a Roman Catholic in 1937, an important element of his intellectual life but no all-purpose explanation of his media theory. In 1946 he joined St. Michael's College at the University of Toronto, where he developed much of his work and directed the Centre for Culture and Technology from its establishment in 1963.

His major publications include The Mechanical Bride (1951), The Gutenberg Galaxy (1962), Understanding Media (1964), The Medium Is the Massage (1967, with Quentin Fiore), and War and Peace in the Global Village (1968, also with Fiore). Laws of Media (1988), developed with his son Eric McLuhan, appeared after Marshall's death. Celebrity brought his terms into everyday speech while often detaching them from the arguments that gave them force.

From close reading to the media environment

McLuhan learned to read forms before he claimed to read technological environments. At Cambridge, the influence of practical criticism, rhetoric and literary modernism encouraged attention to arrangement, rhythm, juxtaposition and the work that an expression performs. A poem is changed by its form; a newspaper page and an advertisement might deserve a similarly exacting reading.

In Toronto, that method entered a wider conversation. Harold Innis had studied how the material character of communication systems relates to institutions, power, empires and the organization of time and space. McLuhan acknowledged the debt, while moving the inquiry towards perception and the reconfiguration of human activity. Work with Edmund Carpenter and others in the interdisciplinary Explorations milieu gave these ideas a collaborative laboratory.

The shift from literary scholarship to media theory was therefore an enlargement of the object of criticism. An environment could be read for the effects of its form, even when no single author had composed it.

Three books, three changes of attention

The Mechanical Bride treats advertisements, comics and industrial popular culture as objects worth studying closely. Its pages isolate ordinary images from the flow that makes them seem harmless or inevitable. The book's satire sometimes bears the moral assumptions of its period, yet its central gesture is still valuable: familiarity need not be the end of interpretation.

The Gutenberg Galaxy asks how print, reproducibility and typographic sequence might contribute to wider habits of thought. McLuhan associates them with visual organization, regularity, segmentation and aspects of modern individualism. The connections are provocative. History of the book and reading has since shown that oral, manuscript and print cultures overlap and that institutions, markets and readers mediate these developments.

Understanding Media extends the very word medium. Clothing, clocks, roads, money and automobiles enter the discussion alongside print, telephone and television. The category is illuminating when it identifies a change in human association; it becomes less precise if every useful object is simply called a medium.

The medium is the message

The best-known formulation in Understanding Media, chapter 1, turns on changes in scale, pace and pattern. A railway carries passengers and freight, yet the effects of a railway are not exhausted by what happens to be aboard it. Speed, distance, work and the possible shape of towns can be altered by the system itself.

McLuhan uses electric light as an especially clear example. When illumination enables an activity, its social effects do not require a verbal message. Likewise, a new communication medium can rearrange how often people meet, how quickly they expect a reply and how widely an event is encountered.

Content remains consequential. A truthful report and a fabricated one need different judgments. McLuhan's point is that analyzing their content alone leaves another dimension unexamined: the shared form that helps determine their reach and the circumstances of reception.

Extension, narcosis and adaptation

In McLuhan's vocabulary, technologies extend human faculties. A wheel extends movement; a telephone extends the reach of a voice; an external recording system changes our relation to memory. Such extensions make new actions possible, and they also require adjustment.

His treatment of Narcissus in chapter 4 of Understanding Media gives that adjustment a troubling aspect. The person becomes accustomed to an extension and loses sight of how it has altered the surrounding balance. McLuhan speaks of narcosis and, more radically, auto-amputation. These are exploratory metaphors, not established neurological laws.

An everyday technology can be simultaneously useful and difficult to inspect. The ease of using it often depends on no longer attending to every condition that makes its use possible. The question becomes what else has changed during that habituation.

Print, electricity and different rhythms of experience

McLuhan associated typographic culture with visual sequence and an orientation towards ordered, repeatable units. He associated electric communication with a stronger experience of simultaneity: the telegraph, telephone, radio and television allow distant events to enter attention rapidly and, at times, together.

His language of a changing sensorium names relations among the senses, rather than a simple replacement of sight by hearing. The contrasts are often too sweeping to serve as historical periods. Different forms of communication coexist, and their effects depend on education, access and social use.

What survives this criticism is a question about rhythm. A medium can change how long news takes to arrive, how widely it can circulate and how close a distant demand feels. Geographical distance remains real while the geography of attention changes.

Figure, ground and the art of noticing

We tend to notice the words spoken on a call more readily than the expectations of availability that the telephone helps establish. The words become figure. The system of timing, reach and social obligation recedes into ground.

McLuhan increasingly used this relation to think about media as environments. The new medium attracts attention at first; the established one is often the means through which we attend to something else. A critic can reverse the focus, bringing an ordinarily unnoticed condition into view.

An anti-environment interrupts habit enough to expose its structure. Art, a new technological form or even a breakdown can reveal the old setting as a setting. This is the purpose of the following reading device: it changes the question while holding the scene still.

The global village is not a promise of harmony

Electric simultaneity led McLuhan to the image of a global village. The phrase is sometimes remembered as a prediction that connection would bring humanity into easy agreement. His own accounts were more unsettling. Heightened proximity can intensify involvement, rivalry and collective pressure alongside shared awareness.

A village is a place where other people's actions are difficult to ignore. Enlarging that condition across distance produces exposure, not necessarily understanding. McLuhan's discussions of retribalization must also be read critically: the anthropological vocabulary of his period can flatten differences between societies.

His insight concerns the intensity of contact. It is better read as a problem about the consequences of proximity than as a prophecy about the Internet.

Hot and cool media, probes and tetrads

McLuhan distinguished hot and cool media by his ideas of definition and the participation they invite. Radio and cinema were among his examples of hot media; the telephone and the television of his time appeared among the cool. The categories are historically unstable, especially when devices combine forms and audiences use them in different ways.

His aphorisms frequently operate as probes: statements intended to disturb a habitual angle of vision and expose unsuspected relations. That method can be inventive, yet its suggestiveness must never be mistaken for empirical confirmation.

With Eric McLuhan he developed the tetrad, published in Laws of Media (1988). It poses four questions about an artefact: what does it enhance, make obsolete, retrieve and reverse into when taken to an extreme? The questions are simultaneous analytical prompts, not four compulsory historical stages or scientific laws comparable to those of physics.

The counterargument: technology has a social history

Raymond Williams challenged approaches that attribute too much independent power to technologies. In Television: Technology and Cultural Form (1974), he insisted on social intentions, institutions and practices in the invention and adoption of technical systems. A medium's consequences cannot be fully detached from the purposes and power relations through which it is built.

Innis makes a related correction from another direction: control over communication has always been tied to institutions and political organization. McLuhan brings the sensory and environmental dimension into view, but may push those questions of ownership and social struggle too far into the background.

Historical generalization is another limit. Print did not produce one homogeneous modern mind; oral and literate practices did not succeed one another in clean blocks. McLuhan's powerful metaphors require corroboration and sometimes restriction. Their value is greatest when they sharpen a question that further inquiry can test.

The contemporary wound: when an environment ceases to feel optional

A phone remains visibly a device. Its surrounding infrastructure is harder to hold in view: authentication, work schedules, messages, payment, maps, access to services and expectations of constant availability. Some arrangements become familiar enough that alternatives seem cumbersome or even impossible.

A notification occupies seconds, but the expectation of interruption can extend across a day. A feed contains stories of widely different kinds while its scroll, ranking and rhythm form a common environment for encountering them. These are current applications of McLuhan's questions, not accounts of platforms that he himself studied.

Seeing an environment is only the beginning of criticism. It matters who sets its rules, who profits from it, what alternatives are available and what leaving would cost. Williams and Innis help correct a purely perceptual reading here.

The place of McLuhan in the tenth triad is deliberately distinct. Lucretius asks how material things reach the senses; McLuhan asks what technical environments do to perception and association; Shannon will ask what transmission can formally accomplish under constraints and noise. No one of these inquiries contains the others.

We can learn to recognize a medium before it breaks. The more demanding question is whether a condition we have come to inhabit can still be discussed, resisted and changed.

Primary texts and orientation

Primary works: Marshall McLuhan, The Mechanical Bride (1951); The Gutenberg Galaxy (1962); Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man (1964), especially chapters 1–4; Marshall McLuhan and Quentin Fiore, The Medium Is the Massage (1967) and War and Peace in the Global Village (1968); Marshall and Eric McLuhan, Laws of Media: The New Science (1988).

For historical orientation and criticism: Harold Innis, The Bias of Communication (1951); Raymond Williams, Television: Technology and Cultural Form (1974); Philip Marchand, Marshall McLuhan: The Medium and the Messenger; and the collections and institutional records of the University of Toronto and the McLuhan Estate.
