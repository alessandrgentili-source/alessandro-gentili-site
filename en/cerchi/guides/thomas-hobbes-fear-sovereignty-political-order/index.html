A reader's route

The nine movements of the main essay begin with an engraving and follow the argument that gives it life: the insecurity of competing private judgments; the vulnerability of the body; the reasoning of covenant; the invention of political representation; the sovereign's command; the limits of obedience; the struggle over religious interpretation; the experience of civil war; and the modern demand for protection. They can be read continuously. The Atlas desk provides the other half of the guide: a concise life and historical setting, disciplinary circles, major writings, a distinct Cerchi reading instrument, ideas and concepts, a constellation, contemporary limits, reading paths, questions, FAQ and sources. The two forms share a subject, with different uses.

The main essay

1. A sovereign made of people

The frontispiece of Leviathan, published in London in 1651, shows a gigantic crowned figure rising over a landscape of fields and towns. In one hand he holds a sword; in the other, a bishop's crozier. Seen from a distance, he is a single ruler, towering above the buildings he protects. Look closely, however, and his torso changes: it is made of many small human figures, joined into a body that none of them could form alone. The image is a political argument before the book has begun.

A stranger looking at the engraving might suppose that Hobbes wanted people to disappear into a sovereign. The crowded body invites that fear. Yet the composition raises a prior puzzle. How can a multitude of distinct persons, each able to judge, want, distrust and act, become one actor in political life? A group assembled in a town square has bodies and voices, but it does not necessarily have a single will that can make laws, hold treaties, decide disputes or defend the town. The giant is the result of an act of construction. Its unity is neither a natural growth nor a divine miracle. It is made.

The book's full title joins matter, form and power to the commonwealth, both ecclesiastical and civil. Hobbes had learned to think about bodies, motion, causes and the power of words in an age when inherited claims to authority were under unprecedented pressure. In Leviathan, he speaks of the state as an artificial person, endowed with an authorized voice. The idea is easy to distort when separated from its setting. A political people cannot be assumed simply because the word people is uttered. Someone must be entitled to act in its name, and that entitlement requires an account.

The figures inside the sovereign's body also suggest the problem its creation is meant to solve. The individuals who authorize a common representative remain mortal, vulnerable and capable of disagreement. The sovereign possesses immense powers, but those powers arise from people who wished to escape a condition in which no one could count on protection. Their reasons for entering a commonwealth remain intelligible even after its public authority has been established.

The sword and crozier give the illustration an uncomfortable precision. Civil commands and claims made in the name of religion can compete for allegiance. If two rival powers require incompatible acts, the person who must obey faces a dangerous choice. Hobbes's political thought emerged from a period when such choices had become matters of imprisonment, battle and death. The engraving offers the answer he found: an authority capable of speaking for the commonwealth. Whether that answer grants too much to its representative is a question the rest of the book cannot settle for us.

2. When everyone is their own judge

In chapter XIII of Leviathan, Hobbes asks what human relations might become where there is no common power capable of enforcing rules or resolving disputes. The state of nature is a thought experiment with historical points of reference, most painfully the collapse of public order into civil war. It is a condition of insecurity, not a datable prehistoric era through which all societies necessarily passed.

Picture two households who disagree about a piece of land. Each has reasons for believing the claim is theirs. Without a recognized court, either may decide to defend the land, and each must assess the other's intentions. If one household gathers allies to prevent an attack, the other may interpret the precaution as evidence of an approaching assault. What begins with a disputed boundary becomes a contest over the danger posed by the other party. No participant needs to delight in cruelty for the situation to become violent.

Hobbes names three important occasions of conflict: competition, diffidence or mutual mistrust, and glory. Competition concerns gain; mistrust can prompt pre-emptive violence for security; glory makes perceived slights and reputation matters of perilous consequence. Under conditions of roughly equal vulnerability, no one can take their superior position as a permanent guarantee. An individual may be stronger today, yet weaker while sleeping; a conspiracy can overcome a powerful opponent. Equality here concerns exposure to danger and the ability to threaten, not an already existing equal standing before law.

The most disturbing feature of the state of nature is the persistence of war without continuous fighting. Hobbes's comparison with bad weather helps: a storm is defined by a settled disposition of the atmosphere, not by the presence of rain at every instant. So a condition of war exists where people cannot rely on a secure expectation that others will refrain from violence. Even a quiet afternoon can be inhabited by fear. Agricultural work, exchange, industry and sustained cooperation become precarious when anything accumulated may be taken and no shared adjudicator can reliably intervene.

The notorious description of life in this condition includes the words 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short'. Its force comes from the argument surrounding it. Hobbes is describing the human cost of public insecurity, not offering a complete account of what people are in every circumstance. He recognizes affection, the wish for sociability, curiosity, calculation and a desire for peace. His account also needs criticism where he uses colonial reports about Indigenous peoples as illustrations of supposed natural conditions; those passages depend on European assumptions that do not constitute sound evidence for the political standing or lives of the communities described.

Here is the decisive distinction. In the state of nature, people can be reasonable and still remain insecure. Each understands the danger of trusting the other without credible assurance. Their problem is partly the structure of their relations. A public authority matters because private judgment, however sincerely exercised, cannot settle claims that every party has an interest in deciding for itself.

3. The fear that makes peace thinkable

In Hobbes's political anthropology the body comes before the citizen. People are susceptible to pain, illness, hunger, attack and death. They also imagine future harms. A person can fear a danger that has not arrived, anticipate what others might do and alter conduct accordingly. The capacity to imagine another person's threat can increase insecurity; it can also give human beings reasons to establish peace.

The wish to preserve life is central. Hobbes calls the right of nature the liberty to use one's power as one judges necessary for self-preservation. In a condition without common authority, this right can extend very far because no shared judge settles which measures are necessary. The fact that someone acts from fear does not itself make the resulting act wise or morally admirable. Yet fear is capable of teaching something. Whoever knows that another person can injure them knows something about the danger of leaving all disputes to unregulated force.

Reason supplies what Hobbes calls laws of nature: precepts that direct people towards peace where peace is possible and encourage them to keep agreements under conditions that make performance safe. Among the most important are the injunction to seek peace, the willingness to lay down some liberty when others are prepared to do likewise, and the keeping of covenants. Their standing in Hobbes's moral philosophy has been debated—whether they function mainly as prudential conclusions, moral demands or divine laws. Their political role is clear: they show why self-preservation can counsel limits on one's immediate freedom of action.

An agreement reached through fear raises a serious question. Modern readers may be inclined to think that the absence of fear is a condition of valid consent. Hobbes's reasoning is less comfortable. The impulse to escape danger can make agreeing to common protection intelligible. He distinguishes sovereignty by institution, when people establish an authority through covenants with one another, from sovereignty by acquisition, when power is established through submission for protection in circumstances of conquest. The two forms are not morally identical by contemporary democratic standards; Hobbes treats their connection through protection and fear as central to the argument for political obligation.

The political consequence is unsettling. Fear can prompt people to authorize an order that is powerful enough to restrain them. The same motive that draws them away from private violence may expose them to public coercion. Hobbes accepts a very extensive sovereign authority because he judges the alternative—a commonwealth whose core decisions can be reopened by competing private tribunals—liable to collapse back into conflict.

Fear alone, however, does not explain the whole passage. Individuals must also recognize a common reason to end war, understand what reciprocal agreement requires and establish a power capable of making compliance more secure than violation. Hobbes's citizens are not merely frightened animals asking to be ruled. They are people capable of making a political artifice because they can foresee the cost of leaving their relations ungoverned.

4. A promise becomes a public person

The passage from multitude to commonwealth is the most inventive part of Hobbes's answer. A collection of private promises is not enough if everyone remains the final judge of whether the others have performed. Someone must be authorized to speak and act in the name of the many, with powers sufficient to settle disputes and maintain security. This is where Leviathan moves from a theory of agreement to a theory of representation.

Chapter XVI introduces a distinction among person, actor and author. A person may act in their own name or represent another. In the latter case, the represented party is the author of acts performed through the authorized actor. The terminology has affinities with law and the theatre, where one voice or figure may appear on behalf of someone else. Hobbes draws on these resources to give political unity a specific structure. The one who carries the sovereign voice need not be the only person whose actions and interests matter. The sovereign is constituted through the authorization of those who become subjects.

The covenant instituting a commonwealth is, crucially, a covenant among those entering it. They undertake to authorize a common person or assembly. The sovereign is not simply another contracting party who can be sued for breach under the same agreement. The asymmetry helps explain why Hobbes makes the sovereign so difficult to challenge. People who grant the sovereign authority cannot continually retain a rival public judgment competent to undo every sovereign decision; that would defeat the unity the covenant was meant to create.

This is a demanding account of public personhood. It lets Hobbes say that a political people becomes one through representation, although the individuals remain distinct in private life. It also exposes a problem contemporary constitutional systems treat differently. Authorization is often understood as bounded, revisable and subject to institutional checks. Hobbes fears that dividing ultimate authority among competing bodies will produce irresolvable conflict. His argument is not a sketch of representative democracy as we now understand it. Its conception of authorization creates a sovereign with powers far wider than a periodically elected government normally has.

The frontispiece can now be read in a new light. The bodies within the giant supply neither a biological organ nor a silent crowd. They are the persons through whom a political actor is constituted. What makes the giant one is an authorized representative relation. It remains an extraordinary construction: people who may distrust one another make themselves capable of acting together by authorizing a voice whose decisions bind them.

The problem of whom that voice represents does not disappear with the initial act. If public decisions expose some subjects to grave insecurity, the sovereign's claim to represent a protected commonwealth comes under pressure from the purpose for which it was established. Hobbes has his own severe answer to that difficulty; a modern reader has good reason to test it rather than inherit it unchanged.

5. The sword, the law, and the power to interpret

A law cannot settle a dispute if nobody can decide what it means. A judgment cannot protect a victim if no authority can enforce it. An army cannot defend a commonwealth if rival command structures can countermand one another at decisive moments. Hobbes treats these relationships among legislation, adjudication, execution, taxation, defence and doctrine as reasons for undivided sovereignty.

His preferred argument extends beyond a personal taste for powerful rulers. Institutions that must act together may be disabled when each claims a separate and final right to decide. If two authorities can issue incompatible binding orders, the subject faces the conflict that political society was supposed to overcome. Hobbes's response is to concentrate ultimate sovereignty in a single person, whether an individual monarch or an assembly. His account is absolutist, although it does not require that the sovereign must be a king: monarchy is his preferred form, but the logical question is where final authority resides.

There is a real power in the diagnosis. A political order unable to judge or enforce its public commitments leaves citizens exposed to private coercion and competing masters. Effective law can make ordinary life possible in a way a paper declaration cannot. Yet the cure carries an equally real danger. A final authority with no independent public superior can misjudge, abuse or fail to protect. To make government capable of stopping violence is also to create a concentration of force that requires moral and political scrutiny.

Hobbes's theory can be most unsettling when it touches interpretation. Who has the authority to pronounce what the law commands? Who decides whether a public declaration is a private opinion, a breach of peace or a claim of spiritual jurisdiction? In the seventeenth century, rival religious and political authorities could mobilize armed allegiance. In the later books of Leviathan, Hobbes gives sustained attention to scriptural interpretation and the claims of ecclesiastical power because he regards competing authoritative voices as a source of civil disintegration.

The state he constructs is therefore also an institution for managing publicly binding words. A shared peace depends on more than preventing physical blows. It requires a way to settle rival declarations of right, obligation and legitimate command. This part of his theory helps explain why the book's title includes the ecclesiastical as well as the civil commonwealth. It also reveals why freedom of conscience and public expression create hard problems for his settlement. He knows what violent disagreement can do; he is less willing than later liberal thinkers to leave ultimate religious or political interpretation outside sovereign supervision.

The question for a contemporary reader is not whether laws should be enforced. It is how an effective legal order can preserve the ability to contest erroneous interpretations without reopening the violence that law exists to prevent. Hobbes is a thinker with whom constitutionalism must argue, not a convenient ancestor to be made into a constitutional liberal after the event.

6. The body that refuses the command

Hobbes has built an extraordinarily powerful sovereign. Now imagine the sovereign ordering a subject to wound himself, to refrain from food or medicine, or to make no effort to escape a deadly attack. The command may carry all the outward marks of public authority. Yet in chapter XXI of Leviathan, Hobbes states that a person retains the liberty to disobey certain commands that directly threaten their survival. A covenant to abandon the means of defending one's life cannot bind in the way an ordinary political agreement can.

This is one of the places where the familiar label the philosopher of absolutism becomes a poor substitute for reading the work. Hobbes's sovereign possesses very extensive rights to command and punish. The subject's liberty to resist bodily destruction does not necessarily make the sovereign's order unjust in Hobbes's own framework. Two assertions that modern political thought often tries to reconcile stand beside each other: the sovereign may be entitled to punish; the person punished may retain the liberty to resist rather than consent to their own destruction.

The distinction exposes a boundary within political authorization. The person who sought a common peace because life was endangered cannot plausibly be understood to have promised that they will never defend that life again. The claim does not supply a comprehensive modern doctrine of rights, due process or legitimate rebellion. Hobbes strongly resists treating private opinion as a standing authority over public law. Nevertheless, the survival interest that made covenant reasonable also preserves a remainder of agency that sovereign authorization cannot completely absorb.

The liberty of subjects in Hobbes has several dimensions, including freedom where law is silent. Its most dramatic aspect concerns self-defence. The more ordinary aspect matters as well: a commonwealth need not regulate every action, and subjects can pursue a wide range of activities where no law forbids them. This is a different conception of liberty from participation in self-government or freedom from domination. Its strengths and limits must be measured in those terms.

There is an uncomfortable reciprocity here. A common power without force could offer little security. A power that threatens a person's life tests the foundation of that person's political obligation. Hobbes does not offer a simple rule permitting collective uprising whenever an official acts unjustly; the scope and status of resistance remain interpretively difficult. His account is better read as a persistent tension between sovereign right and the inalienable impulse to protect oneself.

For Cerchi this is the point at which the political argument turns back toward the human body. Every artificial political person is constituted by people who can be injured, imprisoned, starved and killed. The survival of that vulnerability marks a limit in any account that treats political unity as complete absorption. The giant remains made of mortals.

7. The two voices that demand allegiance

The sword and crozier return in the third and fourth parts of Leviathan. Hobbes devotes an enormous portion of the book to questions of religion, Christian doctrine, the interpretation of Scripture and the claims of spiritual authorities. Readers who know only the state of nature and the social covenant often pass over these chapters. That omission changes the work. In the world Hobbes inhabited, political peace could not be separated from disputes about who was entitled to speak in God's name.

The English Civil Wars were fought amid conflicts of law, parliament, monarchy and church. People understood their duties through rival religious commitments as well as competing constitutional theories. A subject could be asked to obey one authority in temporal matters and another in spiritual matters; the boundary itself might be disputed. For Hobbes, such a division threatened to restore the conditions of mutually hostile judgment that the commonwealth had been designed to prevent.

His attempt to bring public religious authority under the sovereign's control is severe. He disputes the right of independent ecclesiastical institutions to impose binding public obligations beyond civil authority. He works through biblical language, the nature of revelation and the meaning of the Kingdom of God, often against interpretations powerful in his own day. The book thus has a theological and polemical architecture inseparable from its political construction.

What did Hobbes himself believe about religion? Scholars have debated his sincerity, his orthodoxy, the rhetorical strategies of his scriptural arguments and the relation of his materialism to Christianity. It would be irresponsible to settle those questions with a quick declaration that he was secretly an atheist or a straightforward defender of an existing church. The secure point is the political pressure of rival claims to public command. He wants the community to possess a final public voice where the demand for obedience is concerned.

The argument touches a fault line still recognizable. Religious conscience can resist coercive state power; unchecked religious authorities can also seek coercive power of their own. A society committed to freedom of belief must find ways of protecting conscientious dissent and public peace without giving either a cleric or a government unlimited authority over interpretation. Hobbes shows why the conflict matters, even where we reject his solution.

The image of the sovereign holding both emblems is no incidental decoration. It records the extent of the settlement he proposes: a body politic whose unity is threatened when people are ordered by incompatible voices. A later constitutional order may organize pluralism and institutional limits in ways Hobbes mistrusted. It cannot dismiss the danger posed when rival authorities claim exclusive obedience while denying each other's legitimacy.

8. The historian of disorder

Hobbes's Behemoth, a dialogue on the English Civil Wars composed in the late 1660s and printed without Hobbes’s authorization in 1679, approaches the failure of political authority through the history of an actual conflict. The title evokes another monstrous figure, but the subject has changed. Leviathan imagines the construction of a common power; Behemoth studies the division and collapse of a political order and the passions, doctrines and institutional failures that accompany it.

The relation between the works is illuminating. An argument about the conditions of peace becomes a historical investigation into the causes of disorder. Hobbes looks at education, religious preaching, competing legal claims and the political misjudgments through which allegiance is divided. His historical narrative is partisan and should be read critically, especially where he identifies intellectual or religious opponents as causes of conflict. Yet it reveals an author who understood civil war as more than a battle between armies. A commonwealth can be weakened by the ideas its members have learned to accept about whom they must obey.

Hobbes's personal experience of the crisis matters. He left England for Paris in 1640 and lived on the continent during much of the period of civil war. He returned to England in 1651, the year Leviathan appeared. He was connected over many decades to the Cavendish household and to intellectual networks reaching across England and France. His political position was shaped by danger and royalist associations but does not fit neatly into a simple party label. His theory of sovereignty could also unsettle supporters of the monarchy when it refused to ground political legitimacy solely in hereditary right.

The historian and philosopher together challenge an easy assumption: political order can be lost even in a cultivated society with law, religion, institutions and learned elites. Its breakdown is a possibility produced within historical life. For Hobbes, preventing that breakdown requires stable authority and public education in the grounds of obedience. His confidence that undivided sovereignty is the proper answer remains contestable. His attention to the way organized disagreements can become organized violence is harder to set aside.

The word Behemoth also prevents a tidy conclusion about the giant of Leviathan. Political power is a human construction and can fail. An apparatus may be formidable while its authority is contested; a ruler may command resources without commanding loyalty. The real question is whether the relations through which people accept a common public authority can endure when serious disagreement and mutual fear reappear.

9. The protection that asks for everything

A government announces an extraordinary security measure after a series of violent attacks. The measure restricts movement and permits wider monitoring of communications. Its advocates point to an immediate danger and argue that existing procedures are too slow. Its critics fear that the powers, once granted, will remain after the emergency has passed. Some citizens believe the new arrangements will protect them; others, particularly those already subject to unequal treatment, expect to bear most of the burden. Both groups may be responding to forms of vulnerability that cannot be dismissed as irrational.

This is an illustrative contemporary case created for Cerchi d'inchiostro. Hobbes did not study digital surveillance, modern constitutional review or administrative technologies. His work offers questions that keep the dispute from collapsing into a contest between people who care about safety and people who care about freedom. Safety is among the conditions that make practical freedom possible. The powers used to secure it can themselves become a danger to particular people. The issue concerns the design, justification and limits of the authority authorized to protect.

We may begin by asking what threat has been established and what protection the measure can plausibly provide. We can then ask who grants the power, who is affected, what evidence may be challenged and which independent institutions can review the result. Those last questions belong to modern constitutional practice more than to Hobbes's preferred political settlement. Their importance grows from the same human difficulty that made his project urgent: people need reliable protection against injury, including injury inflicted by those who claim to act for them.

The contemporary wound is protection used as an argument for unlimited authorization. It arises when the necessary work of security is made to justify powers whose scope, duration and effects are removed from public examination. In such circumstances fear can become a political resource, encouraging people to accept a growing distance between the promised end and the means used to pursue it. Fear is not always manufactured; the danger may be real. This makes the argument harder. A real danger does not give every proposed response the same legitimacy.

Hobbes helps us resist one form of innocence: the belief that freedom can be sustained without a political order capable of preventing violence. His own solution makes another innocence impossible: the belief that an authority empowered to secure life will always be harmless to those whose lives it governs. The task of modern constitutional politics is to hold these demands together through institutions capable of protecting the vulnerable while keeping public power answerable and contestable.

The third author in the Freud–Gramsci–Hobbes triad thus changes the location of the question about obedience. Freud examines how authority can be internalized within a divided subject; Gramsci studies how a historical order acquires cultural direction and becomes common sense; Hobbes explains why vulnerable people may deliberately establish a public power strong enough to compel them. None of these explanations replaces the others. Inner conflict does not constitute a sovereign; cultural leadership cannot by itself enforce a judgment; political coercion cannot explain every reason people accept a world as legitimate.

Look again at the engraved giant. The sword has not vanished, nor has the multitude of bodies that authorizes its bearer. The image remains uncomfortable because it holds an achievement and a risk in the same figure. People have made a common political person so that they need not face violence alone. Whether that person continues to act for their protection must remain a question that political life can ask.

The Atlas desk

Hobbes at a glance

Name and dates. Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), born at Westport, near Malmesbury in Wiltshire, and deceased at Hardwick Hall, Derbyshire. English philosopher, historian, translator and participant in debates over natural philosophy, mathematics, language, religion and political authority.

Historical location. The English crisis of monarchy, Parliament and rival religious allegiances in the seventeenth century, culminating in civil war, execution of Charles I, republican government and Restoration. Hobbes spent extended periods in the household and service of the Cavendish family and lived in Paris from 1640 to 1651.

Principal work. Leviathan (1651 in English; revised Latin edition 1668). Its celebrated argument about political peace is inseparable from its accounts of human cognition, language, authorization, law and religious power.

Central political problem. How can human beings create a common power capable of protecting them when each has reason to fear the judgments and actions of others?

Core distinctions. State of nature and civil society; right of nature and law of nature; covenant and mere promise; multitude and political person; authorization and representation; sovereign authority and the subject's inalienable liberty of self-preservation.

Why Hobbes matters

Hobbes takes the security of embodied persons as a starting point for political construction. He asks what happens when no shared judge can settle a dispute and when every private judgment about safety must be backed, if necessary, by private force. The result is an argument for common authority founded on the need to escape mutual insecurity. His answer is severe because he believes divided ultimate power invites civil breakdown. His question endures because political orders depend on their ability to make law, adjudication and protection effective.

A second achievement lies in his theory of the artificial person. Political unity becomes intelligible through authorization and representation rather than through the assumption that a community naturally speaks with one voice. This innovation makes Hobbes a major figure in the history of political personhood even for readers who reject his defence of undivided sovereignty.

A third, easily neglected part of the work concerns religion and language. Political conflict includes rival interpretations of divine commands and competing claims to authority. His insistence on civil control of public doctrine is troubling for modern pluralism, but it reveals why he understood public speech, law and obedience as interdependent. Hobbes matters most when we keep the power of that diagnosis alongside the dangers of his proposed solution.

The human problem

People must act without knowing the intentions of everyone around them. They need other people to survive and flourish, yet they cannot guarantee those others will always honour promises, respect boundaries or agree about justice. Their vulnerability is both physical and anticipatory: a person can suffer violence and can fear what might happen if violence becomes possible. Hobbes asks whether human reason can construct dependable conditions for peace in the midst of such uncertainty.

His argument does not require that every person is evil. The possibility of war can arise among people trying to preserve themselves when no common judge is reliably available. The philosophical difficulty is then how an authority made powerful enough to end this condition can avoid betraying the purpose that called it into existence.

Life, context, and the turning points of the work

1588–1608: education and languages. Hobbes was born in the year of the Spanish Armada and educated at Magdalen Hall, Oxford. He acquired the humanist languages and intellectual habits that later sustained his translations and arguments about the passions, rhetoric and public life. The story that fear accompanied him from birth is a famous biographical anecdote, not an explanation of his mature philosophy.

1608 onward: the Cavendish connection. He entered the household of the Cavendish family, initially as tutor and later as scholar and companion. The connection gave him access to books, travel, political experience and intellectual networks. His service in an aristocratic household complicates any picture of a secluded philosopher constructing politics outside history.

1629: Thucydides. Hobbes published an English translation of Thucydides' history of the Peloponnesian War. The work supplied a sustained engagement with conflict, faction, rhetoric and the fragility of collective judgment. It would be unwarranted to identify every later Hobbesian claim as a direct borrowing from Thucydides; the documented translation makes the historical encounter itself significant.

1630s: natural philosophy and continental encounters. Hobbes's investigations and travel brought him into contact with debates about geometry, motion and the new science. Intellectual connections involving Marin Mersenne and Pierre Gassendi, alongside controversies with Descartes, widened his philosophical field. The familiar claim that Hobbes simply copied a geometric method from Euclid is too narrow for the complex history of his scientific thinking.

1640–1651: departure to France and political writing. Hobbes circulated The Elements of Law in manuscript in 1640 and left England for Paris as the political crisis deepened. De Cive appeared in Latin in 1642. The English Leviathan followed in 1651, a work composed during exile and published while England was emerging from its first civil-war and republican convulsions. Hobbes returned to England that year.

1650s–1660s: system and controversy. De Corpore (1655) and De Homine (1658) completed the separately published parts of his projected Elements of Philosophy. He disputed questions of liberty and necessity, geometry and experimental natural philosophy with contemporaries including John Bramhall, John Wallis and Robert Boyle. A revised Latin Leviathan appeared in 1668. These controversies remind us that his political thought belongs to a broader and disputed conception of scientific understanding.

Late work and death. Hobbes composed Behemoth, his history of the English Civil Wars, during the later 1660s; an unauthorized printing appeared in 1679, while he was still alive. He returned to classical translation in old age, including Homer. He died in 1679 at Hardwick Hall. His long association with the Cavendishes and the changing regimes through which he lived are indispensable contexts for his accounts of political allegiance.

Historical frame. Parliament, monarchy, established church, dissenting congregations, the army and legal authorities contested the grounds of command in an increasingly violent English political world. Continental religious wars and transformations in natural philosophy formed the wider setting. Hobbes's call for undivided public authority was an intervention in that world, not an abstract wish for uniform obedience in every possible society.

The disciplinary circles

Political philosophy and the state. State of nature, covenant, public force, sovereign authority and the conditions under which a stable commonwealth can exist. Hobbes constructs a political order from the circumstances of individual agents rather than assuming a naturally unified body politic.

Law and political personhood. Authorization, representation, artificial personality, civil legislation, judgment and punishment. Hobbes shows how a public actor may be constituted by relations among individuals, while leaving serious questions about the limits of the resulting authority.

Moral philosophy and self-preservation. Natural right, natural law, passions, practical reasoning, obligation, fear, the pursuit of peace and the limits of consent. The moral standing of the laws of nature remains a matter of scholarly dispute.

Religion and public interpretation. The political consequences of rival ecclesiastical claims, biblical interpretation, church authority and conscience. The religious books of Leviathan are essential to the design of the commonwealth.

Natural philosophy and method. Bodies, motion, explanation, geometry, optics and arguments about scientific inquiry. His wider interests and errors in these areas should be read as part of an intellectually active seventeenth-century project, not merely as ornament to political theory.

Language and political psychology. Names, reasoning, opinion, honour, vainglory, imagination and the capacity to anticipate threat. Language can make agreement possible and also multiply conflict through competing claims of truth or authority.

History of civil war. Hobbes translated Thucydides and wrote Behemoth. His historical interests expose the fragility of institutions and the uses of doctrine in political division.

Contemporary security and constitutionalism. A Cerchi application: emergencies, public protection, concentrated coercive power, resistance, judicial review and the possibility of contesting security measures. These belong to the present and must not be passed off as concepts of seventeenth-century constitutional democracy.

The works: a map for reading

  1. The Elements of Law, Natural and Politic (written and circulated in 1640; published in parts in 1650). An early systematic treatment of human nature and political society. It helps trace the development of Hobbes's account of agreement, passions and sovereign authority before Leviathan.
  2. De Cive (On the Citizen, 1642 in Latin; enlarged editions and English translation followed). An early mature account of liberty, political authority and religion, published as one part of his larger philosophical undertaking. Its conceptual history should be compared with the later account of representation in Leviathan.
  3. Leviathan, or The Matter, Forme, & Power of a Common-Wealth Ecclesiasticall and Civill (1651). The central work. Part I studies the human being, sensation, language and passions; Part II constructs the commonwealth; Parts III and IV address Christian politics and the kingdom of darkness. Read chapters XIII–XVIII and XXI with the religious books, not in isolation from them.
  4. De Corpore (On Body, 1655). The treatment of logic, scientific method, metaphysical and physical questions within the projected Elements of Philosophy. Its historical importance exceeds the validity of its particular scientific claims.
  5. De Homine (On Man, 1658). A work covering aspects of human physiology, optics and other questions within the same larger project. It shows why Hobbes cannot be adequately characterized as a political theorist untouched by natural philosophy.
  6. Behemoth, or The Long Parliament (composed late 1660s; first published in 1679). A dialogue on the causes and course of the English Civil Wars. Its interpretation of the conflict is partisan and analytically important, especially beside Leviathan.
  7. The Questions Concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance (1656) and writings from the dispute with Bishop Bramhall. A route into Hobbes's account of freedom, causality, moral agency and religious controversy.
  8. A Dialogue Between a Philosopher and a Student of the Common Laws of England (published 1681). A work on law and political judgment whose chronology and relation to contemporary legal debates require editorial attention.
  9. The English translation of Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War (1629). A foundational example of Hobbes's historical and literary labour, central to his encounter with faction and the dangers of political speech.
  10. Late translations of Homer. Evidence of continued interest in classical poetry and the language of human conflict, and a reminder that Hobbes's writing exceeds the boundaries of political theory.

On textual history. The English 1651 Leviathan and Latin 1668 version are not identical; the latter contains changes and additions. The dates of composition and publication of Behemoth and the early political manuscripts also require distinction. Page numbers and capitalization vary among editions, so references to major arguments should specify part and chapter.

A Cerchi reading instrument: the protection and authorization test

The following is an original Cerchi interpretive instrument, informed by Hobbes's problem but designed for contemporary institutions. It is not one of Hobbes's own analytic schemes. Its four questions distinguish what makes public power necessary from what could render its exercise dangerous.

A worked case. After a serious public-security incident, a city proposes facial-recognition cameras at all major stations. The vulnerability is evident but must be described specifically. The authorization should identify the law, responsible body and purpose, not rely on a generic appeal to emergency. The protection claim requires evidence about effectiveness, false matches, discriminatory impacts and alternative measures. Contestability requires meaningful access to correction, oversight and a limit on retention and use. A city may conclude that some measures are justified, others disproportionate, and still others untested. The instrument does not predetermine that answer.

Where the instrument fails. It cannot replace legal analysis, empirical security assessment or democratic decision-making. Nor can a Hobbesian analogy settle current constitutional rights. It works when it helps identify the decisions that a claim to protection tends to hide, and when it leaves room for the affected public to challenge its premises.

Key ideas

  • Political peace is an achievement of construction. Hobbes asks how persons with competing interests can create a common authority, rather than presuming that a political people exists as one actor by nature.
  • Insecurity may arise without universal wickedness. Mutual suspicion, competing claims and anticipation of attack can create a condition of war among agents who seek to preserve themselves.
  • Fear has an intelligible political role. The prospect of injury and death can make a shared peace worth establishing; it does not render every security policy good.
  • Natural right and natural law differ. The right of nature concerns liberty for self-preservation; laws of nature give rational precepts directed towards peace and keeping covenants where performance is safe.
  • Authorization creates a political representative. The many covenant with one another to authorize an actor or assembly whose decisions constitute the commonwealth's public action.
  • Sovereignty is meant to be effective and undivided. Hobbes fears that rival final authorities would undo the political unity they are meant to secure.
  • The sovereign need not be a monarch. Hobbes preferred monarchy but allowed sovereignty in an assembly; the structural issue is the unity and extent of final authority.
  • Self-preservation leaves an inalienable remainder. The subject may retain liberty to resist direct bodily destruction despite the sovereign's extensive right to command and punish.
  • Religion is politically central. Hobbes treats rival claims to binding spiritual authority as potential sources of civil conflict, not as an afterthought to secular political theory.
  • Civil war is both historical experience and philosophical warning. Behemoth tests the problem of divided allegiance against the collapse of an actual political order.
  • Protection can itself become a source of danger. This is the contemporary tension Cerchi develops from Hobbes, rather than an institutional solution he formulated in modern constitutional terms.

Key concepts

State of nature. A condition without common authority capable of settling and enforcing disputed claims. Its significance is analytical; it is not a universal chronicle of human prehistory.

State of war. A standing condition of insecurity and readiness for conflict, even where combat is intermittent or temporarily absent.

Fear. Anticipation of danger, especially bodily harm and violent death, which may motivate defensive conflict and the search for public peace.

Self-preservation. The desire and practical orientation to maintain one's life; central to the right of nature and to the reasoning that supports political agreement.

Right of nature (jus naturale). Liberty to use one's powers as one judges necessary for preservation in the condition of nature. It differs from a command to undertake every possible act.

Law of nature (lex naturalis). A precept discovered by reason that directs people towards peace and the conditions of mutual security. Its moral and theological status is contested in scholarship.

Covenant. A binding agreement involving the future performance of obligations, whose practical security depends on conditions allowing trust and enforcement.

Multitude. A plurality of persons not yet united in the authority of one representative political person.

Artificial person. An actor whose words and deeds can be taken as the authorized acts of those represented; central to the constitution of the commonwealth.

Authorization. The relation through which persons acknowledge another actor as entitled to act on their behalf. In Leviathan, it is crucial to political unity.

Representation. Acting or speaking for another by authority, allowing a political multitude to be treated as one public person.

Sovereign. The individual or assembly whose final authority represents and governs the commonwealth within Hobbes's theory.

Sovereignty by institution. Sovereignty established through covenants among people who authorize a common representative to escape insecurity among themselves.

Sovereignty by acquisition. Sovereignty established through submission for protection under the power of a conqueror, treated by Hobbes as involving the same fundamental political obligation.

Undivided sovereignty. Hobbes's claim that the essential powers of final public authority must not be separated among mutually competing authorities.

Civil law. The public commands and rules of an authorized sovereign, sustained by the capacity to interpret and enforce them.

Liberty of subjects. The sphere of conduct unregulated by law, together with certain retained liberties, most notably the liberty to resist immediate threats to one's bodily preservation.

Obedience. Compliance with public commands under an authority understood as established for peace and protection; its grounds and limits are central to the theory.

Protection. The secure condition that gives political authority its practical purpose, while leaving serious questions about how obligations end when protection fails.

Leviathan. The name of Hobbes's great work and emblem of the artificial commonwealth: a powerful public body made through the authorization of individuals.

Behemoth. The title of Hobbes's historical dialogue on the English Civil Wars, recalling the breakdown of common political allegiance.

Civil religion / ecclesiastical authority. Questions concerning the public interpretation of religion and the competing claims of church and sovereign power. The phrase 'civil religion' should be used cautiously, since Hobbes's theological project resists a simple modern label.

The constellation

Before Hobbes — conflict, nature and political unity. Aristotle provides a major point of difference: where Aristotelian political association is connected to human nature and living well, Hobbes constructs an artificial public person to escape insecurity. Thucydides, whom Hobbes translated, supplies historical knowledge of faction, war and the fragility of public judgment. Machiavelli is an illuminating predecessor in confronting political action without idealized descriptions of rulers, though their methods and accounts of conflict differ substantially. Scholastic theories of natural law and sovereignty form part of the inherited language that Hobbes reworks. Euclidean geometry and the newer natural philosophy offered models for demonstration and explanation, but claims of a simple direct methodological transfer require care.

Within his century — war, science and disputed authority. Civil war in England was the decisive political event in the making of his mature theory. The circles of Marin Mersenne and Pierre Gassendi connected Hobbes to continental debates in philosophy and science. His disagreements with Descartes, and later with John Bramhall, John Wallis and Robert Boyle, show a writer concerned with mind, causality, geometry and experiment as well as sovereignty. Rival claims from Parliament, monarchy and religious organizations gave his arguments their immediate public stakes.

After Hobbes — contract and resistance. John Locke argues for political society and rights through a different account of natural law and more extensive limits on public power. Rousseau transforms the idea of collective authorization and the general will, without simply adopting Hobbes's artificial person. Kant gives further accounts of right, public authority and autonomy that cannot be reduced to Hobbesian premises. Carl Schmitt's twentieth-century discussions of sovereignty and political unity return to some Hobbesian questions in a drastically changed historical setting; comparison does not amount to approval. Later scholarship by Michael Oakeshott, Leo Strauss, Quentin Skinner, Richard Tuck, Deborah Baumgold, A. P. Martinich and Noel Malcolm provides differing ways of reading Hobbes's arguments, contexts and reception.

Present-day crossings — the value and danger of protection. Constitutional rights, emergency powers, public health restrictions, the authority of courts, border security, digital surveillance and public disagreement about religion are all fields in which Hobbes's questions can illuminate a specific dispute. They require their own evidence and standards of legality. No contemporary device or political system should be treated as something Hobbes foresaw.

The triad — Freud, Gramsci, Hobbes. Freud examines authority, identification and conflict within psychic life. Gramsci studies the cultural and institutional formation of consent and historical leadership. Hobbes asks what collective authority people may rationally establish to end political insecurity. The relation is editorial and comparative. The three thinkers do not share a single theory of obedience or a unified account of freedom.

Legacy: what passes into the present

Hobbes helped transform the grammar of political legitimacy. Political authority becomes something to be explained through the conditions and actions of persons, rather than understood solely through inherited status or a claim about the natural perfection of a polity. His account of authorization and public personhood has had a long afterlife in debates about representation. The force of his argument for sovereign unity has made him a necessary interlocutor for traditions that defend divided, constitutional and limited government.

His political philosophy is inseparable from an ambitious inquiry into language, bodies, human passions, scientific understanding and religion. That larger project prevents an easy contrast between a rational modern state and an allegedly irrational human multitude. Hobbes imagines human beings capable of constructing an order precisely because they can reason about their vulnerability. The answer they produce is formidable, and the dangers of that answer remain a central part of his legacy.

The contemporary wound: when protection licenses every power

Public protection is an achievement that should not be treated lightly. Peace, enforceable rights and ordinary security create room for work, friendship, travel and disagreement. But the language of protection can be used to demand an ever-wider authorization, including measures whose consequences are unevenly distributed and whose duration escapes review.

The wound Cerchi identifies is the substitution of a promise of security for a continuing justification of power. A government may speak in the name of a real threat while refusing to account for the effects of the measures chosen. The more urgent the danger, the greater the temptation to treat objections as proof of indifference to the public good. Hobbes teaches why an effective common power is needed. A critical modern reading must also ask how to protect people against that power when its acts threaten the very lives and freedoms it was established to secure.

Limits and contradictions

  1. The sovereign is too difficult to challenge. Hobbes's defence of extensive, undivided authority grants insufficient room, by modern constitutional standards, for institutional restraints, public accountability and the correction of abuses.
  2. The right of self-preservation remains tense with sovereignty. A subject can resist immediate bodily destruction even while Hobbes claims that the sovereign possesses legitimate coercive rights. The relation has generated sustained disagreement among interpreters; it is not resolved by calling him either a libertarian or an absolutist without qualification.
  3. Fear can rationalize subjection as well as peace. Protection may justify the creation of public authority, while coercive submission under conquest is also accommodated by Hobbes's argument. Modern understandings of free political consent require stronger conditions.
  4. The state of nature is an argumentative construction. Hobbes's examples involving the Americas reflect colonial knowledge claims and prejudices, not reliable accounts of Indigenous political societies. Their use must be criticized, not repeated as empirical evidence.
  5. Religious pluralism receives constrained protection. Hobbes rightly identifies dangerous conflicts between competing authoritative commands; his solution gives the sovereign powers over public religious interpretation that modern pluralist societies have substantial reasons to resist.
  6. Political unity can obscure unequal vulnerability. Hobbes is unusually alert to mortal insecurity but gives less systematic attention than contemporary political analysis requires to gender, class, enslavement, exploitation and the differentiated capacities of people to consent or resist.
  7. A model of security may become a model of dominance. The ability to stop private violence does not settle the justice of public decisions. Hobbes's strongest insight into the need for common protection thus stands beside his most troubling institutional conclusion.
  8. His scientific ambitions require historical limits. Hobbes's arguments in physics, geometry and experimental method were controversial and often unsuccessful. Their history should not be used as a shortcut to validate or invalidate the political theory; the reasoning in each domain needs separate examination.

Curiosities worth following

Why does the frontispiece contain many small figures? It represents the sovereign commonwealth as an artificial person constituted through the multitude. It is a visual inquiry into political unity and representation.

Why did Hobbes translate Thucydides? The translation gave him a sustained literary and historical encounter with war, civic faction and political language. Its publication in 1629 predates his principal works on sovereignty.

Did Hobbes think all people are naturally cruel? His argument about the state of nature does not require universal cruelty. Mutual mistrust, competition and the absence of public adjudication can produce insecurity even among people motivated by preservation.

Does he prefer monarchy? Yes, but he argues that sovereign authority can also reside in an assembly. The essential issue is whether final public authority is undivided and effective.

Why is so much of Leviathan about Christianity? Hobbes saw competing religious and civil claims to obedience as a major source of political instability. His scriptural and theological arguments are part of the work's political architecture.

Is the frontispiece definitely by Abraham Bosse? It is commonly associated with Bosse, but attribution has a scholarly history and is not beyond dispute. The engraving's function in Leviathan is more secure than a simplified account of its maker.

Why does Hobbes use a second monster, Behemoth? The title names his historical account of civil disorder and provides a revealing counter-image to the constructed political unity of Leviathan.

Did Hobbes agree with the monarchy about everything? No. His long aristocratic associations and exile have to be read beside a theory that grounds political authority in protection and authorization, not solely in inherited royal title.

Why is resistance possible under an absolute sovereign? Hobbes argues that certain rights related to bodily preservation cannot be relinquished through covenant. The interpretation of their reach remains debated.

What makes Hobbes relevant to contemporary emergency government? His work asks why security matters, what powers make it possible and how vulnerable people understand political obligation. Applying it today requires modern constitutional and empirical analysis.

Common misreadings to avoid

  • Treating the state of nature as a verified universal stage in human history.
  • Assuming a state of war means nonstop physical fighting.
  • Reading the famous description of human life in the state of nature as the whole of Hobbes's anthropology.
  • Saying Hobbes thinks everybody is naturally evil, selfish in every action, or incapable of affection.
  • Confusing the right of nature with the laws of nature.
  • Calling the covenant a direct contract between each subject and an ordinary accountable ruler.
  • Treating authorization in Leviathan as equivalent to modern electoral representation.
  • Assuming Hobbes requires sovereignty to be held by an individual monarch rather than allowing an assembly.
  • Forgetting the liberty to resist direct threats to bodily preservation.
  • Calling such resistance a fully articulated constitutional right of revolution.
  • Reading Parts III–IV of Leviathan as marginal theological ornament.
  • Reducing the English Civil Wars to an abstract example of disorder, without their particular constitutional and religious conflicts.
  • Treating fear as an admirable political end rather than a circumstance in which peace becomes urgent.
  • Using Hobbes as a timeless endorsement of surveillance, emergency rule or every national-security measure.
  • Claiming that Freud's unconscious, Gramsci's hegemony and Hobbes's covenant are three names for the same explanation of obedience.

Reading paths

If you have fifteen minutes. Read Leviathan chapter XIII on the state of nature and chapter XVI on persons and representation. Compare the meaning of private judgment with the construction of the artificial person. Then read the opening movement of this guide.

If you have an hour. Read chapters XIII–XVIII and XXI of Leviathan in a reliable edition. Track the movement from insecure liberty through natural laws and covenant to sovereign authorization. Pay particular attention to where a subject's liberty persists.

If you have a week. Day 1: the frontispiece and introduction to Leviathan. Day 2: chapters X–XIII on power and insecurity. Day 3: chapters XIV–XVI on right, law, covenant and persons. Day 4: chapters XVII–XXI on sovereignty and liberty. Day 5: selections from Parts III–IV concerning scriptural interpretation and church authority. Day 6: selected dialogues of Behemoth; place the historical narrative beside the philosophical construction. Day 7: return to De Cive and compare its treatment of political society with that in Leviathan, taking notes on what has changed.

For political philosophy. Read Hobbes beside Aristotle's Politics on the purposes of political association, then Locke's Second Treatise on political limits. Keep differences in historical situation and conceptual vocabulary visible. A three-column summary alone will miss the logic of each argument.

For the contemporary question of security. Choose one publicly documented emergency policy. Record the danger cited, the authority that enacted it, the evidence for its expected benefit, those affected, the scope for review and the rules governing expiry. Use the Cerchi instrument as a way of asking questions, not as proof that a modern policy is Hobbesian.

Questions to orient a reading

  1. How does a condition of war persist even when no fighting is occurring?
  2. Why can people seeking only safety become dangerous to one another in the absence of a common judge?
  3. What distinguishes a rational law of nature from the liberty of natural right?
  4. What agreement establishes a commonwealth, and why is the sovereign not simply a fellow contractor?
  5. In what precise sense can many people become one political person?
  6. Why does Hobbes resist dividing the final powers of sovereignty?
  7. What remains of a subject's liberty when the sovereign threatens that person's bodily life?
  8. Why do religious interpretation and public doctrine occupy so much of Leviathan?
  9. Does effective protection require the kind of unlimited authority Hobbes defends?
  10. How might a modern political order provide security and still make the authorities responsible for it answerable?

Nodes to remember

  • The sovereign is made, through authorization, from a multitude of vulnerable persons.
  • The state of nature names the absence of a common power, not a documented universal prehistoric society.
  • Fear can be a reason for peace because people can anticipate danger and recognize their need for security.
  • Natural right and the laws of nature have different functions in the argument.
  • The sovereign represents the public person created through covenants among the subjects.
  • Hobbes argues for extensive and undivided sovereignty but allows it to be held by an assembly.
  • Self-preservation preserves certain liberties even under sovereign power; the resulting tension remains difficult.
  • Religion and public interpretation belong inside the political work.
  • Behemoth examines how political order can collapse in history.
  • Modern readings of security and surveillance are applications requiring independent constitutional and empirical tests.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Thomas Hobbes?

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) was an English philosopher, historian, translator and student of natural philosophy. He is best known for Leviathan (1651), which argues that vulnerable people need an effective common political authority to escape the insecurity of the state of nature.

What is Hobbes's central political question?

How can people who fear one another establish a common power capable of protecting them? Hobbes develops an account of natural right, rational precepts of peace, covenant, representation and sovereign authority to answer it.

What is the state of nature in Hobbes?

It is a condition without a common, effective political authority to settle disputes and enforce judgments. It is primarily an argumentative model of insecurity, with references to civil war and international relations, rather than a universal account of prehistoric societies.

What does the war of all against all mean?

A condition in which everyone must anticipate the possibility of conflict because there is no reliable shared judge. War can persist as insecurity even during intervals when no battle is occurring.

Did Hobbes believe that human beings are naturally evil?

His argument does not require universal malice. Competition, mistrust, concern for honour and the need for self-preservation can generate conflict among people who would prefer a peaceful life.

Why is fear important to Hobbes?

Fear of injury and violent death can lead people to defend themselves aggressively, but it can also help them recognize the benefits of common peace. Hobbes uses that vulnerability to explain why creating political authority can be rational.

What is the difference between natural right and natural law?

The right of nature is a liberty to use one's powers for self-preservation. Laws of nature are rational precepts that direct people towards peace and mutual agreement under appropriate conditions. Their precise moral standing remains a subject of scholarship.

What is the social covenant?

In the case of sovereignty by institution, individuals covenant with one another to authorize a common representative. This creates the political person of the commonwealth and gives the sovereign extensive powers. The sovereign is not simply another party to that founding covenant.

What is the meaning of Leviathan?

It is the title of Hobbes's 1651 work and its emblem of the artificial commonwealth: an immense political person made through the authorization of many individuals. The frontispiece depicts the sovereign figure composed of smaller human bodies.

What does representation mean in Hobbes?

It is the relation through which an actor speaks or acts with authority for others. In Leviathan, political unity arises when people authorize a sovereign person or assembly to represent the commonwealth.

Did Hobbes support absolute monarchy?

He preferred monarchy and defended absolute, undivided sovereignty, but he allowed sovereign authority to be held by an assembly. His principal concern was the effective unity of final political command, not a doctrine requiring a king in every commonwealth.

What is the difference between sovereignty and ordinary government?

Sovereignty concerns final public authority over essential matters such as lawmaking, adjudication, enforcement and defence. Particular officials and offices exercise delegated functions, while the sovereign is the ultimate source of their civil authority in Hobbes's account.

Could a subject resist the sovereign?

Hobbes reserves important liberties of self-defence, including resistance to commands to injure oneself or abandon the means of survival. Such resistance is not identical to a general modern right to overthrow an unjust government; its relation to sovereign authority is a continuing interpretive problem.

What happens when the sovereign cannot protect people?

For Hobbes, the purpose of sovereignty is protection and the obligation of obedience is connected to the capacity to provide it. How this obligation terminates and who judges failure are contested issues in his political philosophy.

Why is religion so important in Leviathan?

Hobbes feared that competing religious and civil authorities could demand incompatible obedience, threatening political unity. Large portions of the work address Scripture, Christianity and the public authority of religious interpretation.

What is Behemoth?

Behemoth is Hobbes's history of the English Civil Wars, composed in the later 1660s and first printed without his permission in 1679, shortly before his death. It complements Leviathan by investigating the breakdown of political allegiance in history.

How does Hobbes differ from Aristotle?

Aristotle connects political association to human nature and the good life. Hobbes constructs an artificial sovereign through the need for common security. Their disagreements concern the origin, purposes and authority of political association, among other matters.

How does Hobbes differ from Locke?

Both use the language of natural rights and agreement, but Locke defends political limits, rights and resistance through a different account of natural law. Hobbes argues that effective public peace requires much more extensive and undivided sovereign authority.

Why is Hobbes relevant to emergency powers and surveillance?

His thought makes the relation between fear, protection and concentrated power unusually visible. Applying it to contemporary policies requires legal, ethical and empirical examination, including whether the measures actually protect people and remain contestable. Hobbes did not theorize digital surveillance.

How does Hobbes relate to Freud and Gramsci in the Cerchi triad?

Freud examines the psychological life of authority and identification; Gramsci studies the historical formation of cultural leadership and consent; Hobbes asks how vulnerable people authorize a common political power. The triad compares different reasons for obedience without claiming that the three authors share one theory.

An editorial closing

In the engraving, a sovereign rises above the town with a sword and a crozier. Its authority looks indivisible from a distance. Up close, it consists of individual bodies, each able to fear injury and each with reasons to seek a peace beyond their own power to secure. That difference in scale is the problem Hobbes makes unavoidable.

His answer gives the common political person a frightening strength. What remains unsettled is whether such strength can continue to serve the people whose vulnerability first made it necessary. A secure public order allows strangers to live together without judging every conflict by force. The work of keeping that order answerable cannot end with the moment it is authorized.
