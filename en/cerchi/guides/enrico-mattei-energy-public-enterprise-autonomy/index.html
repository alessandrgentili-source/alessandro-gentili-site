Introduction — The country in search of its own energy

The man who turned the search for energy into a question of economic independence, industrial capacity and political power.

Political freedom returned to Italy before the means to sustain it had been rebuilt. In the aftermath of the Second World War, factories needed to restart, cities to function and regions to reconnect. Infrastructure and investment were scarce; so was energy, which industry needed in reliable quantities and at prices it could bear.

Much of that energy depended on resources and companies abroad. The young Republic could draw up plans for development, yet the terms on which those plans could be carried out were partly decided elsewhere. Enrico Mattei saw that the problem of energy was also a problem of power. The cost of oil, access to gas, control of pipelines and the conditions attached to concessions all influenced where factories could operate, whether businesses could invest and how many people they could employ.

When Mattei took charge at AGIP in 1945, the public oil company faced the prospect of liquidation or a sharp reduction in activities judged unlikely to succeed. He argued for keeping its expertise intact and continuing exploration. Natural gas from the Po Valley offered an industrial foundation. Pipelines followed; activities expanded; the creation of ENI in 1953 gave the enterprise a new scale and a wider reach.

Mattei wanted a public company able to produce, compete and negotiate. In the Mediterranean and the Middle East he pursued direct dealings with producing countries, seeking more room for Italy to secure energy on its own terms. ENI's activities extended from geological exploration and refining to engineering, industrial organisation and economic diplomacy.

The enterprise built infrastructure and accumulated knowledge. It also concentrated decisions, incurred financial risks and entered contentious relationships with politics and the press. Those tensions are inseparable from its achievements. How could Italy build economic power strong enough to serve the public interest while keeping that power answerable to the public?

Mattei's career gives that twentieth-century question a material history: wells and pipelines, contracts and ministries, industrial jobs and decisions taken far from the places they would affect.

Enrico Mattei at a glance

Enrico Mattei was born in Acqualagna, in the Marche region, on 29 April 1906. He died on 27 October 1962 when his aircraft crashed at Bascapè, in the province of Pavia. An entrepreneur, partisan and public-sector executive, he became a central figure in Italian industrial policy and the first president of ENI in 1953.

His apprenticeship was industrial before it was political. He worked at the Fiore tannery in Matelica, moved to Milan and established a business in the chemical sector. During the Second World War he joined the Resistance in Catholic circles, building relationships that would remain important in the new Republic.

In 1945 he was appointed extraordinary commissioner of AGIP, the Azienda generale italiana petroli, founded in 1926. His appointment came as the government contemplated liquidating the company. Mattei argued instead that exploration should continue and that the expertise already assembled should be preserved. The exploitation of natural gas, followed by the creation of ENI in 1953, marked the decisive stages of his public career.

For Mattei, an energy company could help Italy acquire technical knowledge, develop productive capacity and negotiate with greater freedom in international markets. His work connected industrial economics and energy policy with diplomacy, the organisation of labour and the duties of public institutions. He tried to give Italy a greater say over the material conditions of its development.

Why Enrico Mattei matters

Mattei's industrial project emerged as Italy moved from the immediate work of reconstruction to the longer effort of developing its industries. Energy determined the cost of production, the location of plants, the competitiveness of firms and the viability of new investment. Defending AGIP meant retaining people and capabilities that would have been hard to recover once dismantled.

The groundwork, however, preceded him. Natural gas had been found in the Po Valley before his arrival: the Caviaga 1 well encountered a substantial accumulation in 1944. Subsequent work at Caviaga, Ripalta and Cortemaggiore confirmed the region's potential. Mattei did not discover the resource. His contribution was to help turn it into an industrial programme.

Po Valley methane became the basis of a domestic market. Through ENI, exploration and production could be coordinated with transport, refining, distribution and related industrial activities. Turning the discoveries into a functioning energy system took years of investment and organisation.

Abroad, Mattei sought terms of access to oil that differed from those prevailing under the major international companies. Agreements with producing countries brought ENI into debates about who should share the benefits and risks of natural resources, at a time when newly independent states were asserting a larger economic role.

Expansion alone cannot explain what ENI achieved. Its results depended on exclusive rights, resource rents, finance, technology and political relationships. Mattei matters because his career makes the possibilities of state enterprise visible alongside their costs and conditions, in a particular institutional history rather than an abstract argument for or against state intervention.

The human problem — Dependence without the power to decide

On 26 October 1949, during a debate in the Chamber of Deputies on funding for the Ministry of Industry and Commerce, Mattei defended public exploration for hydrocarbons in the Po Valley. He spoke about industrial production and the social responsibilities of the state in the same argument. Finding fuel mattered; so did the ability to make use of it, even when the investment was costly and the result uncertain.

Post-war Italy could plan factories, jobs and infrastructure, yet much of the energy needed to make those plans work depended on resources and companies abroad. The Republic possessed political authority, but its freedom to act could narrow where supplies or contractual terms were beyond its control. A legal power to choose was of limited use without the material means to carry a choice through.

Mattei's response began with what he could organise: keeping technical expertise at AGIP, developing domestic gas, building pipelines and seeking more than one route to imported oil. None of this promised independence from international trade. Autonomy, for him, was not self-sufficiency. Italy still needed other countries; what he wanted was a stronger position from which to deal with them.

Trade connects modern economies, but the political terms change when one side has few alternatives and little influence over the conditions it must accept. Mattei's history brings the question of self-government down to the level of supply contracts, industrial networks and the decisions a society can actually carry out.

Life in brief

Acqualagna and Matelica, 1906–1929

Born in Acqualagna to a family of modest means, Mattei spent much of his childhood and youth in Matelica. His first jobs led him to the Fiore tannery, where he learned about chemical processes and industrial production and rose to technical director in 1926. Following military service and the tannery's financial difficulties, he left for Milan in 1929.

Milan and private enterprise, 1929–1943

In Milan, Mattei traded in products used by the tanning industry. In the summer of 1931 he opened a small manufacturing operation, equipped at first with a few machines and a laboratory for emulsions. Between 1934 and 1935 he consolidated the business later known as Industria chimica lombarda grassi e saponi, which made paints and industrial auxiliaries; Marcello Colitti dates the opening of the via Tartini plant to 1935.

His business plans sometimes reached beyond Milan. He considered a fish-processing venture in colonial Eritrea in 1936 but abandoned it after encountering operational difficulties. The city also brought him into contact with Marcello Boldrini and circles of social and political Catholicism.

Resistance, 1943–1945

After 8 September 1943 Mattei entered the Resistance, taking on organisational duties in Catholic formations and in structures connected with the Comitato di liberazione nazionale per l'Alta Italia and the Corpo volontari della libertà. The experience deepened political relationships that would matter during republican reconstruction.

AGIP and Parliament, 1945–1953

Appointed extraordinary commissioner of AGIP in 1945, Mattei resisted the prospect of closing down its exploration activities and backed the development of natural gas. He was elected to the Chamber of Deputies for the Christian Democrats in 1948, taking part in debates about national energy resources and the state's economic role.

ENI presidency, 1953–1962

Law no. 136 of 10 February 1953 established the Ente nazionale idrocarburi, with Mattei as its president. Over the following decade ENI expanded its industrial, technological and commercial operations, negotiating in oil-producing countries and developing commercial ties with the Soviet Union. Mattei died on 27 October 1962 in an aircraft crash that also killed the pilot Irnerio Bertuzzi and the journalist William McHale.

What Mattei's life helps explain

Mattei knew industrial work before he came to lead large public companies. At the Fiore tannery in Matelica he encountered production as a set of practical demands: materials, machinery, processes and the people capable of operating them. Trade in Milan then brought him into contact with suppliers, customers and the difficulties of securing supplies. Running his own chemical business exposed him to commercial risk.

These experiences help explain the attention he later paid to technical knowledge and organisational capability. A public enterprise needed more than state ownership to function as an industrial one.

The Resistance added a different apprenticeship. Organising within Catholic formations required trust, coordination and personal responsibility; networks built during those years also accompanied Mattei into the political world of the Republic.

Marcello Boldrini, a professor of statistics and a figure in Italian Catholic economic thought, played an important part in Mattei's intellectual formation. Through Boldrini, Mattei entered circles around Milan's Università Cattolica. In 1942 he attended meetings on post-war reconstruction alongside Giuseppe Dossetti, Amintore Fanfani and Pasquale Saraceno. The responsibilities of the state in economic life were therefore being discussed in his milieu well before ENI existed.

He kept his ties with the Marche. Matelica made him an honorary citizen in 1953, and his later encounter with Aristide Merloni belongs to that regional history. Yet their industrial relationship has to be understood through the documented circumstances of the Matelica factory, not inferred from a shared place of origin.

None of these experiences made Mattei's later decisions inevitable. Together, they help explain how industrial practice, political alliances and the debates over reconstruction came to shape the man who directed ENI.

Italy's reconstruction

In 1945, rebuilding the Italian economy meant restoring production, transport and access to raw materials after the damage of war. Industry was unevenly distributed: northern areas had greater concentrations of manufacturing, while many inland and southern territories faced weaker infrastructure and fewer jobs. Energy supply was therefore a question for the country as a whole.

Po Valley gas offered a domestic source for industry. Oil still had to be obtained largely from abroad. Decisions about supply brought to the surface a larger issue: what should the state do in sectors on which the development of other industries depended?

Italy already had public enterprises and institutions through which the state participated in economic life. Mattei worked within that tradition, using its instruments to assemble an energy group with operational autonomy and ambitions beyond the national market.

The Cold War and the changing demands of oil-producing countries formed the international setting. A decision about Italian factories could no longer be understood solely within Italy: prices, concessions and supplies were negotiated across political alliances and competing industrial interests.

From AGIP to ENI

In the first post-war years, AGIP's future was uncertain. Some political forces favoured winding down or sharply reducing the public company's operations. Mattei argued for preserving the organisation and its expertise while exploration continued. He could make that case partly because there was already an industrial foundation to build upon.

SNAM, the Società nazionale metanodotti, had been established in 1941 to develop the transport and distribution of natural gas; its first connections came into service in subsequent years. The Caviaga 1 well encountered a substantial gas accumulation in 1944, a discovery whose publicity was limited by wartime conditions. Mattei took over AGIP in 1945. Further exploration and discoveries at Caviaga, Ripalta and Cortemaggiore strengthened the prospects of an Italian methane industry.

Gas could supply a domestic market and help finance further investment, though mineral exploration remained risky: capital had to be committed without any guarantee of a commercially useful find.

The establishment of ENI in 1953 brought several energy companies and activities under one public entity. The founding law also gave it specific rights to explore and extract hydrocarbons in parts of the Po Valley. Both the industrial initiative and the legal privileges matter to the account of its subsequent growth. ENI's capabilities did not emerge from managerial determination alone; they were built within an institutional framework that made certain choices possible.

Circles of Ink reading I — Resources and freedom

The gas beneath the Po Valley had been there before Mattei arrived at AGIP. Geological knowledge alone, however, could not heat a furnace or supply a factory. People had to work the wells, finance production and lay the pipes that brought gas to users. This was the passage from an available resource to a usable industrial capacity.

In Parliament in 1949, Mattei defended public exploration for hydrocarbons as an investment that could change Italy's productive structure. He linked exploration to social aims and to the responsibilities of the community asked to finance it. The risk was part of the proposal: exploration might be expensive without yielding a commercially useful deposit. To argue for it as a public undertaking was therefore a political decision, not merely a technical one.

The first Circles of Ink reading follows from this history: an energy resource becomes a condition of economic freedom when a society can govern access to it and decide how it is used. The claim belongs to this guide's interpretation of Mattei's actions and documents; he did not formulate it as a theory. Ownership of a deposit, in itself, settles little; exploration, engineering and distribution determine what that deposit makes possible.

Those gains had limits. Italian gas could reduce particular vulnerabilities, and the search for other suppliers could create alternatives. Neither development made Italy self-sufficient or guaranteed freedom from economic pressure. Mattei enlarged the range of choices available to industry and government; whether those choices served the public interest remained a further question.

Public enterprise as an organisation

ENI was publicly owned, yet it had to perform the work of a large industrial group. Exploration required geologists and engineers, together with managers who had to commit funds without certainty of a commercially useful discovery. Refining, pipeline construction and distribution each required different skills, equipment and systems of management. Bringing these activities together made it easier to coordinate the energy supply chain.

Mattei prized speed and operational freedom, but technical capacity depended on the work of specialists, managers and commercial staff throughout the group. American experts also assisted in shaping its organisation, separating operating companies from central management and support functions.

The result combined a public controlling entity with companies using the legal forms and commercial practices of business. ENI entered into relationships with foreign firms, including American companies, as both competitor and partner. State ownership and market activity could therefore coexist in the same industrial structure.

That combination made two kinds of scrutiny necessary. ENI had to be judged by what it achieved as an enterprise and by how responsibly it used resources entrusted to it in the public interest.

Circles of Ink reading II — Operational autonomy and responsibility

In the oil industry, decisions and results arrive on different timetables. Funds are committed before a field's potential is known; a contract must be negotiated before a supply route can be secured; a pipeline takes time to build. A management team that needs political permission for each operational choice may lose the opportunity before it can act.

Mattei responded by concentrating strategic direction in the ENI presidency. The arrangement gave the group momentum and a recognisable course, while tying much of its judgement to a single leader. Success could be attributed to that decisiveness; the exposure created by it was harder to measure while the enterprise was still expanding.

There was a corresponding problem for the institutions charged with supervision. Law no. 1589 of December 1956 created the Ministry of State Holdings, which became operational in February 1957. It supplied a formal place for oversight of a large public industrial system, but a statutory power and its timely exercise were different matters. The institutions had to understand decisions whose costs or consequences might not become visible until years later.

The second Circles of Ink reading turns on this institutional difficulty. ENI needed freedom to manage its operations, but that freedom did not release it from public scrutiny. Supervision had to be effective without turning every management decision into a delayed request for permission. ENI's history supplies reasons to take both sides seriously.

Methane and infrastructure

Discovering gas in the Po Valley was only the beginning. Factories could use it once wells, processing facilities and pipelines connected the deposits to places of consumption. SNAM became central to that task, and its network expanded during Mattei's years at AGIP and ENI.

Parliamentary production statistics distinguish the national total from the output of companies retrospectively classified within the ENI group. Italian natural-gas production rose from approximately 64.0 million cubic metres in 1946 to 509.6 million in 1950; within those totals, the companies later grouped under ENI accounted for about 12.6 million and 305.7 million cubic metres, respectively. ENI itself was established only in 1953. The SIUSA archival account records the distribution network growing from about 354 kilometres in 1949 to 1,266 kilometres in 1951. The national totals, the retrospective company grouping and the network lengths measure different things. Together, they document the sector’s material expansion without attributing it all to Mattei. (Production series: Italian parliamentary reports, 1958 and 1965; network: SIUSA, ENI historical entry.)

In his speech to Parliament on 8 August 1951, Mattei emphasised the relationship between production, transport and distribution. A resource became useful to industry when the network delivered it. Yet a pipeline also made choices about territory: the places it reached gained opportunities that others might not have.

Expansion sometimes moved faster than ordinary authorisation procedures. Marcello Colitti records that some pipeline sections were built before all the necessary permits had been secured. The spread of the network and the actual distribution of its economic benefits therefore require separate assessments.

Territory and work

A pipeline can change what a territory is able to produce. In the Marche, the encounter between Enrico Mattei and Aristide Merloni shows how a national energy strategy could intersect with a manufacturer's independent choices.

They met in Fabriano in 1953. Mattei wanted to encourage employment in the mountain areas of the provinces of Ancona and Macerata; Merloni was considering how to diversify his business. As Ercole Sori recounts, Mattei initially proposed making fuel-storage tanks for service stations and raised the prospect of an AGIP order.

Merloni chose another product: cylinders for liquefied gas, to which he could apply expertise acquired in manufacturing weighing equipment. He built the factory at Matelica in 1954. A subsequent AGIP order envisaged for the cylinders never materialised, but the business established itself on the market.

The meeting is documented; a continuing shared industrial programme is not. What makes the episode revealing is precisely the distance between an opportunity opened by a large public enterprise and the technological, financial and commercial decisions through which a local entrepreneur made use of it.

For the twelfth triad, the episode introduces the problem of how industrial development takes root in a place. Merloni's own project deserves to be read on its terms.

Building the industrial group

ENI expanded across exploration and production, transport, refining, distribution and industrial engineering. Coordinating these activities within one group allowed knowledge and equipment to circulate across the supply chain and reduced some forms of dependence on outside suppliers.

Nuovo Pignone in Florence offers one measure of what that strategy could achieve. The company was in difficulty when it entered the ENI system; Giorgio La Pira, the city's mayor, was pressing to defend employment. Under ENI, existing industrial expertise was redirected towards energy technologies, including drilling machinery and compressors.

Expansion also took ENI into petrochemicals, notably through major complexes at Ravenna and Gela. The results were uneven. Colitti contrasts the constructive Pignone experience with less successful investments in other sectors, including textiles.

Each additional activity required capital, and industrial scale was no guarantee of overall profitability. Mattei expected that investments would improve the accounts as plants reached full operation. Others within management, among them Eugenio Cefis, worried about indebtedness and financial exposure. Their disagreement belongs to the economic history of ENI as surely as its visible new plants and equipment do.

The nuclear project

Mattei's ambitions were never confined to oil and gas. In the 1950s, when governments and industrial groups were beginning to see nuclear energy as a possible transformation of electricity generation, ENI acquired expertise and participated in dedicated projects.

On 20 November 1958 Mattei spoke at the laying of the foundation stone for the Latina nuclear power station. ENI began construction using British gas-graphite technology. The plant first generated electricity in May 1963, after Mattei had died, and entered commercial operation in 1964.

The sequence matters: participation in a long-term project belongs to his industrial record, while the plant's later operation cannot be counted among the results he personally brought to completion. Latina shows the breadth of the capabilities ENI hoped to build, at a time when nuclear energy carried a powerful promise of technological modernisation.

The Seven Sisters

In the post-war oil industry, a small group of major international companies held formidable advantages. Their influence rested on concessions and capital, but also on technology, refineries, transport and close relationships with producing and consuming governments. Mattei helped popularise the expression Seven Sisters for this concentration of power.

For a newcomer, gaining access to major oilfields and securing competitive terms was difficult. ENI tried to compete through its own exploration and direct negotiation with producing countries.

The competition was real, yet the Seven Sisters were distinct firms with interests that could diverge. They sometimes competed with one another, and the market also allowed agreements between established operators and newcomers. ENI itself cooperated with Western oil companies. The issue was the concrete distribution of access and bargaining power, rather than a simple division between Mattei and a single united opponent.

The agreement with Iran (1957)

On 14 March 1957, AGIP Mineraria, a company in the ENI group, concluded an agreement with the National Iranian Oil Company (NIOC) for exploration and development. The agreement, later approved in Iran, led to the creation of SIRIP, a joint venture in which the Iranian and Italian parties each held 50 per cent.

The ownership shares were only one part of the arrangement. Under the applicable fiscal arrangements, part of the venture's profits was payable to Iran in taxes. Taken together, the dividends due to NIOC and the tax revenues produced a projected overall distribution of net profits of 75 per cent to Iran and 25 per cent to the Italian side. The figures describe different things: 50/50 refers to the equity in SIRIP; 75/25 to the economic benefits envisaged under the combined corporate and fiscal mechanism. They do not describe shares of the oil deposits or profits already realised.

Exploration carried a further asymmetry. A US State Department memorandum dated 20 August 1957, published as document 403 in volume XII of Foreign Relations of the United States, reported that Iran would contribute capital if a commercially viable discovery was made. Should exploration fail, the Italian party would bear the cost.

The cooperation brought together different economic interests: AGIP accepted a significant risk in return for the chance to enter a new oil market, while Iran gained greater participation in the management and proceeds of petroleum activity. The diplomatic record situates this agreement against the then-prevalent arrangement for equal benefit-sharing between producing states and concession holders.

Neither party was assured of the projected returns. The agreement's historical importance lies in the alternative contractual framework it opened for participation, taxation and exploration risk — one that could be considered in the later evolution of international oil agreements.

Egypt and producing countries

ENI's search for supplies and industrial opportunities extended through the Mediterranean and North Africa. Egypt was one setting in which it explored new forms of cooperation with a producing country. These negotiations unfolded amid decolonisation and demands by national governments for a stronger role in managing their own resources.

Their interests differed but overlapped: producing governments sought capital, expertise and more favourable terms, while ENI sought energy supplies and investment opportunities. That overlap made it possible to negotiate arrangements different from traditional concessions, although the terms still have to be understood in their particular legal and economic settings. The Egyptian experience cannot simply be treated as another version of the Iranian agreement of 1957.

ENI also operated and negotiated in other Mediterranean and North African contexts. In a meeting in Washington on 17 March 1962, recorded in a US State Department memorandum of conversation, Ambassador Reinhardt described Mattei's interest in Iraqi oil affairs and his contacts with the Algerian National Liberation Front. According to Reinhardt, the Italian government had restrained some of those initiatives. This is evidence of how an American diplomat understood the activity and its political implications, not independent proof of every intention ascribed to Mattei.

The anti-colonial dimension associated with his policy belongs alongside Italy's own industrial interests. Recognition of the claims of producing countries and the pursuit of commercial advantage were both present in the same negotiations.

The Soviet Union

ENI began making commercial agreements with the Soviet Union in 1958. A larger agreement followed in 1960, exchanging Soviet oil for Italian industrial products. For Mattei, competitive supplies from another source could enlarge Italy's room for manoeuvre.

The Soviet agreements were politically sensitive. Italy belonged to the Western alliance; the Soviet Union was the United States' chief strategic adversary. ENI's arrangements thus troubled some Western diplomatic and commercial interests.

The surviving American record also complicates the image of Mattei as an unvarying enemy of the United States. A memorandum of 17 March 1962 — document 298 in volume XIII of Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963 — discussed encouraging an agreement between ENI and major Western companies. Its authors were sharply critical of the profitability of Mattei's oil operations and of his political influence in Italy.

Those assessments reveal the concerns of actors with specific diplomatic and business interests. They cannot stand alone as an impartial measure of ENI's performance. Mattei's choices moved among national objectives, corporate negotiations and the boundaries imposed by international alliances.

Circles of Ink reading III — Negotiating autonomy

In 1957, the Iranian agreement gave ENI a possible entry into fields from which it might otherwise have remained excluded. The arrangement offered the Iranian side a larger projected share of economic benefits, while AGIP accepted substantial exploration risk. The agreement gave ENI a possible route into new fields, but it would bear a greater risk of loss if exploration failed.

Italy's dependence did not vanish as ENI expanded. The country continued to need imported oil, foreign technology, capital and commercial partners. Mattei was trying to alter the terms on which those relationships were negotiated. Domestic gas and pipelines supplied some of the material means; agreements with producing countries and alternative suppliers offered a wider choice of contracts. Each new option, however, had its own commitments and vulnerabilities.

Circles of Ink uses negotiating autonomy for the ability to bargain over the terms of interdependence, drawing on expertise, practical instruments and alternatives. It is an editorial category, not a theory attributed to Mattei. The Iranian case illustrates both its promise and its cost. ENI could pursue a new source of oil, but there was no guarantee that exploration would succeed or that the projected profits would follow.

An additional supplier might reduce one dependency and create another; a stronger public enterprise might widen national options while concentrating economic power. The measure of autonomy, then, lies in the choices actually secured, the risks accepted to obtain them and the responsibility for their consequences.

Enterprise and the media

Mattei understood that an industrial project of ENI's scale would be contested in public as well as in ministries and markets. How its choices were explained, defended and represented mattered to its legitimacy.

The daily newspaper Il Giorno, founded in April 1956 at Mattei's instigation, was financially connected to the system of public holdings. It brought important writers and journalists together and contributed to changes in the Italian press. Its financial foundations and Mattei's role also provoked political and journalistic disputes about editorial independence.

ENI developed its own extensive corporate communication as the group grew. Such communication could inform the public about what the enterprise was doing. The financing of a newspaper posed an additional question: how should editorial autonomy be protected when a powerful economic actor helped sustain the institution that reported on public affairs?

No individual article can simply be treated as Mattei's instruction. The ownership and funding relationships nevertheless belong to the story. Through them, industrial power could also influence the public account of its purposes, making disclosure and independence matters of institutional responsibility.

Circles of Ink reading IV — Power that must answer for itself

Decisions taken by Mattei as ENI president reached far beyond the company. A pipeline could redirect industrial possibilities; an overseas agreement could touch foreign policy; the use of public finance could affect political relationships. Mattei had helped give Italy the means to act in energy markets. The fourth Circles of Ink reading asks how an enterprise with that reach could be held answerable for its use of power.

There were institutions responsible for supervision. Law no. 1589 of 22 December 1956 established the Ministry of State Holdings, operational from February 1957, with powers of direction and oversight concerning ENI, IRI and other public enterprises. Their existence matters; it would be misleading to describe Mattei as acting outside any institutional framework. What remains to be examined is whether those powers were exercised with sufficient knowledge, independence and timeliness.

A memorandum of conversation from the US State Department, dated 17 March 1962, records Ambassador Reinhardt's account that the Italian government had restrained certain initiatives Mattei pursued in Iraq and his dealings with the Algerian independence movement. It is one diplomat's account, not a complete record of Italian decision-making. At Nuovo Pignone the influence ran in the other direction: Giorgio La Pira's campaign to protect employment helped shape a decision within ENI. These examples show government, local institutions and the enterprise acting upon one another, without establishing that every intervention amounted to effective scrutiny.

Financing poses a harder test. Historians and journalists have examined allegations concerning secret funds, political-party financing and the transparency of ENI's accounts. The sources need to be distinguished: an allegation made at the time, a later reconstruction and a documented finding do not carry the same weight. Yet these questions cannot be put aside simply because the enterprise built valuable industrial capabilities.

Mattei had made a public enterprise capable of acting quickly. Its pipelines and factories were visible; some of the relationships through which decisions were made were harder to scrutinise. Accountability to public institutions and the community had to extend to those less visible decisions as well. Industrial success alone could not settle the question of public responsibility.

The social dimension of enterprise

ENI's expansion demanded trained people as much as machinery. It also created places in which work and everyday life were organised together. Metanopoli, in San Donato Milanese, gave the group's corporate community offices, services and shared spaces. At Borca di Cadore, the village designed chiefly by Edoardo Gellner made architecture part of the company's social presence.

These experiments invite comparison with Adriano Olivetti, who also saw industry as an activity with obligations beyond production. Olivetti's conception of community, however, reached more directly into political and cultural institutions. The two projects should be compared with that difference intact.

The biographical record includes Mattei's visit to Ivrea in 1955 and the later involvement of architect Marcello Nizzoli in ENI work. Valerio Ochetto's account, cited by Michela Maguolo, describes the meetings between Mattei and Olivetti as occasional and without an integrated collaboration. Their contact does not justify inventing a common programme.

The sharper question belongs to their work: what changes when the social responsibilities of an enterprise are understood through the capacity of a national industrial group, and what changes when they are framed through a political conception of community?

Key ideas

Energy is a condition of development. The availability of resources and infrastructure influences a country's productive possibilities.

Industrial capacity supports political decisions. National objectives require material instruments to become operational.

Public ownership does not eliminate economic logic. A state enterprise must deal with investment, expertise, competition and risks.

Infrastructure changes territories. The distribution of energy helps determine opportunities and inequalities.

Economic relationships can be negotiated. Contractual terms and the distribution of risk influence relationships between enterprises and states.

Autonomy depends on the alternatives available. Diversifying resources, expertise and suppliers can expand the capacity to choose.

Public enterprise belongs to an institutional system. Ministries, Parliament, government and supervisory bodies define responsibilities that must also be assessed in terms of their practical effectiveness.

Growth must be assessed through its outcomes. Corporate size, investment, profitability and collective benefits are different measures.

Key concepts

Public enterprise

An economic organisation belonging, in different forms, to public bodies. It can conduct industrial and commercial activities while pursuing aims in the collective interest.

National interest

The set of aims considered relevant to the political community. Defining it requires institutional responsibility and does not automatically coincide with the interests of a single enterprise.

Vertical integration

The organisation of different stages of an industrial supply chain within the same group.

Resource rents

The economic benefit arising from the availability and exploitation of a natural resource.

Concession

A legal title granting specific exploration or exploitation rights.

Exploration risk

The possibility that investment in exploring for mineral resources will not lead to an economically viable discovery.

Joint venture

An enterprise in which different parties, including those of different nationalities, participate according to defined shares and conditions.

Operational autonomy

The capacity to make and implement industrial decisions within defined managerial responsibilities.

Negotiating autonomy

An interpretive category of Circles of Ink referring to the capacity to negotiate relationships of interdependence with sufficient alternatives and expertise.

Democratic oversight

The set of conditions enabling institutions and the community to scrutinise the exercise of public powers and the responsibilities attached to them.

Constellation

What prepared the ground for Mattei

The Italian tradition of state intervention in the economy, the founding of AGIP in 1926, industrial development before the Republic and the debate on reconstruction helped define the setting in which Mattei acted.

Marcello Boldrini and the circles of social Catholicism were important influences in his formation.

His experience of private enterprise added direct knowledge of work and commercial requirements.

Contemporaries, interlocutors and opponents

Alcide De Gasperi belongs to the political context of reconstruction and the consolidation of the new economic institutions.

Ezio Vanoni contributed to the debate on economic planning, public finance and national development.

Marcello Boldrini accompanied Mattei's intellectual formation and industrial growth.

Amintore Fanfani belonged to political circles with which Mattei maintained significant relationships and to the context in which centre-left governments were formed.

Giorgio La Pira was a concrete interlocutor in the Nuovo Pignone episode and the defence of industrial employment.

Eugenio Cefis held an important position in ENI's management and in discussions about expansion strategies and financial risks.

Aristide Merloni allows us to observe the encounter between national energy initiative and entrepreneurship in inland areas through the documented history of the Matelica factory.

Adriano Olivetti opens a different reflection on the political, cultural and social role of enterprise; their personal contacts remained limited and did not constitute a shared project.

Gamal Abdel Nasser belongs to the context of the transformation of economic and political relationships in the Mediterranean.

The major international oil companies were competitors and interlocutors, with relationships varying according to markets and circumstances.

What came afterwards

ENI continued operating after 1962, undergoing organisational, economic and institutional changes.

Mattei became part of the history of Italian public enterprise and debates on industrial policy and energy autonomy.

His memory developed through cinema, literature, journalism and historical research, generating different interpretations of his activities and his death.

Questions in the present

Energy dependence, strategic infrastructure, security of supply, the concentration of economic power and new industrial policies are fields in which his experience can offer a historical perspective.

These connections belong to Circles of Ink's contemporary reading and are not predictions attributed to Mattei.

Relationships within the corpus

Max Weber allows us to examine organisation, authority and rationalisation.

Antonio Gramsci opens the question of consent and the relationship between culture and power.

Adam Smith offers a perspective on institutions and the relationships between economic interest and society.

Machiavelli allows us to reflect on political action within concrete relations of force.

Marshall McLuhan helps us observe, in relation to the history of Il Giorno, the role of communicative environments in shaping public perception.

These relationships are interpretive and do not imply documented historical influences between Mattei and these authors.

Legacy

ENI survived Mattei and retained industrial, technical and organisational capabilities developed during his presidency. The methane network had helped reshape Italian energy supply; agreements with producing countries had expanded the ways an Italian enterprise could participate in international oil markets. The institution continued to change after 1962, as did the economy and the political system around it.

The legacy also leaves open an institutional question: how much independence a strategic public enterprise needs, and through which institutions its decisions should be scrutinised. That question has outlived the conditions in which ENI was founded.

Mattei's name also circulates in contemporary policy. The Mattei Plan for Africa, launched institutionally in 2023–2024, invokes his legacy in a programme involving diplomacy, finance, infrastructure and development cooperation. Its setting, actors and instruments differ from the oil agreements of the 1950s. Sharing a name does not establish that the strategies are the same.

The more useful inheritance is a way of examining concrete choices: what capabilities do they build, who benefits and which public responsibilities follow?

The contemporary wound — A future decided elsewhere

A factory may choose its machinery and hire its workers, yet find its prospects altered by the price of energy or the terms on which an essential component can be supplied. Those decisions may be made far beyond the town in which the work takes place. The problem would have been recognisable to post-war Italian industry, although the scale and complexity of today's production networks are very different.

Mattei tried to change that imbalance through resources, engineers, pipelines and agreements. Those were responses to the conditions of his own time. They cannot be carried intact into an age of climate change, renewable energy, electrification and different technological dependencies. His history is useful because it shows how much political choice rests on material capabilities, and how laborious those capabilities can be to build.

Yet a society can acquire stronger instruments without settling the purposes for which they ought to be used. A pipeline can bring energy to a district; it cannot decide whose needs should come first. A public enterprise may increase bargaining power; that alone does not establish how its choices should be governed. The present problem is therefore double: which capabilities should we build, and through which institutions should we decide how to use them?

Set beside the histories of Aristide Merloni and Adriano Olivetti, Mattei's question changes scale. In Matelica, Merloni turned an industrial opening into a manufacturing venture through his own decisions; his experience asks what it takes for production and employment to endure in a place. Olivetti approached the place of industry from another direction, asking which social and political institutions could give a community a part in shaping its collective life. Their differences matter. Read together, the three histories bring national capacity into contact with work and participation, without becoming a shared programme.

Mattei's unresolved legacy lies in the interval between having the means to act and deciding, together, what those means should accomplish.

Limits and contradictions

Concentration of power

Mattei's personal abilities contributed to rapid decision-making and the coherence of ENI's strategy. The same concentration raised problems of institutional balance and organisational continuity, particularly in view of succession to the presidency.

Exclusive rights and competition

ENI benefited from specific legal conditions, essential to understanding its development. Assessment of its results must take account of the regulatory structure, the available rents and the organisation of the Italian energy market.

Investment and finance

The group's growth required substantial capital, and not all activities produced equivalent results. Statements by the presidency must be distinguished from financial accounts and independent assessments.

The concerns expressed by part of the management about indebtedness and financial exposure document internal disagreement over the pace of expansion.

Enterprise, politics and transparency

Mattei developed relationships with parties, governments and public administrations.

Historical scholarship and journalistic investigations have discussed the financing of political parties through resources connected to the ENI system, also referring to allegations of the use of secret funds and opaque accounting.

The historical question must be approached by distinguishing allegations, testimony and the results of any specific investigations.

Building a strategic public enterprise does not remove the need to make verifiable the financial relationships and decisions involving politics.

Media and consent

The relationship with Il Giorno reveals the problem of media independence when a major economic actor contributes to its financial organisation.

Environmental consequences

The expansion of the hydrocarbon industry produced territorial and environmental transformations. Their effects must be reconstructed by distinguishing decisions taken during Mattei's presidency from subsequent processes and consequences documented over the long term.

Death and memory

On 27 October 1962 Mattei died in the crash of the aircraft in which he was travelling with the pilot Irnerio Bertuzzi and the American journalist William McHale.

The initial commissions of inquiry and judicial investigations concluded that it was an accident. The Pavia proceedings were closed in 1966.

The case was subsequently reopened. The investigation conducted in the 1990s by the public prosecutor Vincenzo Calia supported a reconstruction of sabotage by explosives on the basis of new investigations and expert reports. The proceedings ended without judicially identifying those who carried it out or commissioned it.

The controversy remains present in historical scholarship.

In 2026 Nicola Mariani and Lupo Rattazzi published a critical reconstruction challenging the strength of the sabotage thesis and reassessing the initial investigations and the aeronautical dynamics of the accident.

The conclusions of this study belong to a debate that cannot be resolved simply by replacing one interpretation with another.

The guide records the outcomes of the different investigations and the existence of historical debate without presenting as definitively proven a responsibility that has not been established.

Death is part of Mattei's history, but it must not become the principle through which his entire experience is interpreted.

Revealing details

An early industrial training

At the Fiore tannery, Mattei rose to technical director in 1926. The experience placed industrial practice at the beginning of his career, before his ventures in business.

The Eritrean episode

In 1936 Mattei considered an investment in fish processing in Eritrea, then an Italian colony.

The project was abandoned. The episode belongs to his economic biography and the historical context of the time, without justifying ideological attributions unsupported by documentation.

Matelica and honorary citizenship

His connection with Matelica continued during his public career and was recognised in 1953.

The encounter with Merloni

The events of 1953–1954 show the interweaving of public initiative and territorial entrepreneurship, with industrial outcomes independent of the AGIP order originally envisaged.

The visit to Ivrea

In 1955 Mattei visited Adriano Olivetti. Valerio Ochetto's biographical account, cited by Michela Maguolo, specifies that their contacts did not lead to an integrated collaboration.

Nuclear energy at Latina

The 1958 ceremony documents ENI's interest in a then emerging energy technology. The plant came into operation after Mattei's death.

The six-legged dog

AGIP's symbol became one of the most recognisable signs in Italian industrial history.

Borca di Cadore

The village designed by Edoardo Gellner makes visible the role of architecture in building corporate identity and everyday company life.

Common misconceptions

Mattei founded AGIP. AGIP had existed since 1926. Mattei defended its continuation after the war.

Mattei personally discovered the methane at Caviaga. The initial discovery dates to 1944. His action was decisive in continuing exploration and developing its results.

Mattei was simply opposed to the market. His conception of public enterprise incorporated commercial capacity, competition and industrial expertise.

ENI grew solely because of its president's personal merits. Regulatory conditions, energy resources, finance and the work of technicians are part of the explanation.

The Seven Sisters were a single entity. They were distinct companies, even when they shared dominant positions in the market.

The Mattei formula assigned Iran 75% of the deposits. The figure concerns the overall economic benefits envisaged by a joint venture and its associated tax regime.

Mattei was always opposed to the United States. His activities included both conflicts and agreements with Western companies and interlocutors.

Mattei was concerned only with oil. His project encompassed methane, industrial plant engineering, petrochemicals and nuclear technology as well.

Corporate growth automatically proves profitability. Size, investment and economic results must be analysed separately.

Every detail of Mattei's death has been judicially resolved. Investigations have produced differing reconstructions and have not judicially identified those responsible for any sabotage.

The contemporary Mattei Plan reproduces Mattei's historical programme. The Plan for Africa uses diplomatic, financial and cooperation instruments belonging to a different context from the oil agreements of the 1950s.

Glossary

AGIP: Azienda generale italiana petroli, founded in 1926 and subsequently incorporated into the ENI system.

ENI: Ente nazionale idrocarburi, established in 1953 as a public economic entity.

Hydrocarbons: chemical compounds consisting principally of carbon and hydrogen, forming the basis of oil and natural gas.

Methane: the main component of natural gas, used as a fuel and industrial raw material.

Gas pipeline: infrastructure for transporting natural gas.

SNAM: Società nazionale metanodotti, established in 1941 and central to the development of natural gas transport in Italy.

Refining: the processes through which crude oil is transformed into usable products.

Petrochemicals: the industry that uses components derived from oil and natural gas in chemical production.

Vertical integration: the organisation of different stages of an industrial supply chain within the same group.

Resource rents: the economic benefit arising from the availability and exploitation of a natural resource.

Concession: a legal title granting specific exploration or exploitation rights.

Seven Sisters: the name associated with the major international companies that dominated the post-war oil system.

Mattei formula: an expression used to describe new forms of participation by producing countries in oil activities and benefits.

NIOC: National Iranian Oil Company, Iran's national oil enterprise.

SIRIP: the Italian-Iranian company established within the framework of the oil agreements of 1957.

Equal joint venture: an enterprise in which two parties hold equivalent shares; in SIRIP's case, the Italian side and NIOC.

Exploration risk: the possibility that investment in exploring for mineral resources will not lead to an economically viable discovery.

State holdings: a system of enterprises in which the state holds stakes and through which it intervenes in the economy.

Strategic autonomy: a contemporary category concerning the ability to retain effective scope for action in sectors considered essential.

Negotiating autonomy: an interpretive category of Circles of Ink concerning the capacity to participate in relationships of interdependence with alternatives, expertise and instruments of negotiation.

Frequently asked questions

1. Who was Enrico Mattei?

Enrico Mattei (1906–1962) was an entrepreneur, partisan, public-sector executive and leading figure in Italian energy policy. He led AGIP's revival and became ENI's first president in 1953.

2. Where was Enrico Mattei born?

He was born in Acqualagna, in the Marche region, on 29 April 1906. He spent a significant part of his youth in Matelica.

3. Why does Enrico Mattei matter?

He contributed to the development of Italy's public energy industry, the exploitation of domestic methane, the construction of infrastructure and new international agreements with producing countries.

4. Did Enrico Mattei found ENI?

He was the leading figure in its industrial and political construction. ENI was formally established by the state through Law no. 136 of 10 February 1953, and Mattei became its president.

5. What was Mattei's economic thinking?

Mattei argued for the importance of a public enterprise capable of acting with industrial autonomy in strategic sectors. He linked the availability of energy to economic development and the nation's capacity to negotiate. His thinking is reconstructed through speeches, documents and decisions, including his parliamentary speech of 26 October 1949.

6. What were the Seven Sisters?

They were seven major international oil companies holding dominant positions in the post-war global market. Mattei helped popularise the name.

7. What is the Mattei formula?

It is a name associated with contractual models envisaging greater participation by producing countries in oil activities and benefits. In the Iranian agreement of 1957, an equal joint venture between AGIP Mineraria and NIOC, together with the applicable tax regime, envisaged an overall distribution of net profits of 75% to Iran and 25% to the Italian side.

8. What was the relationship between Mattei and Iran?

In 1957 AGIP Mineraria, part of the ENI group, concluded an agreement with the National Iranian Oil Company for oil exploration and development, with corporate and fiscal terms that were innovative in relation to prevailing models.

9. Why did Mattei make agreements with the Soviet Union?

The agreements enabled ENI to obtain competitive oil supplies through commercial exchanges. They developed during the Cold War and caused tensions with some Western interests.

10. What relationship did Mattei have with Aristide Merloni?

The two met in Fabriano in 1953. Mattei suggested a new industrial activity to Merloni and held out the prospect of an AGIP order. In 1954 Merloni built a factory for gas cylinders in Matelica, subsequently developing the business without the later proposed AGIP order for those cylinders materialising.

11. Did Mattei know Adriano Olivetti?

Yes. A biographical account, cited by a specialist study, documents a visit to Ivrea in 1955. Their meetings were limited and did not produce an integrated collaboration. The comparison between their respective projects remains culturally significant.

12. What was the relationship between Mattei and Il Giorno?

Mattei supported the newspaper's establishment in 1956 through economic relationships connected to the system of public holdings. The episode raises questions about the relationship between enterprise, the media and consent.

13. Was Mattei also involved in nuclear energy?

Yes. ENI participated in the development of the Latina nuclear power station, whose construction began in 1958. Mattei spoke at the foundation stone ceremony on 20 November that year. The plant began producing electricity in 1963, after his death.

14. How did Enrico Mattei die?

He died on 27 October 1962 in an aircraft crash near Bascapè, together with the pilot Irnerio Bertuzzi and the journalist William McHale. The initial investigations concluded that it was an accident; a later investigation supported sabotage without judicially identifying those responsible. The reconstruction remains contested in recent historical scholarship as well.

15. Does the contemporary Mattei Plan revive Enrico Mattei's historical project?

It invokes his name but belongs to a different context. The Plan for Africa of 2023–2024 encompasses economic, infrastructure and diplomatic cooperation initiatives that do not automatically coincide with the oil joint ventures and ENI's industrial strategy of the 1950s.

16. What can Mattei teach us today?

His experience helps us examine economic dependence, the building of industrial capabilities and public oversight of strategic sectors. It also shows that autonomy requires institutional responsibility, transparency and assessment of results.

Method

The guide distinguishes documented facts, historiographical interpretations, Circles of Ink's editorial reading and contemporary applications.

Company sources and Mattei's speeches are treated as historical testimony, without assuming that they automatically certify the results claimed.

Quantitative assessments require relevant accounting, contractual or statistical documentation.

The category of negotiating autonomy is an original formulation by Circles of Ink. It is not attributed directly to Mattei.

The complete collection Scritti e discorsi 1945–1962 has been identified bibliographically but has not been examined in its entirety. Some specialist sources are used as historiographical references rather than as texts fully verified at this stage.

The debate over Mattei's death is presented by distinguishing the outcomes of investigations from subsequent interpretations, without attributing definitive certainty to a contested reconstruction.

Closing — What capacity is for

When Mattei arrived at AGIP in 1945, natural gas had already been found in the Po Valley. What remained uncertain was whether the public company would be allowed to continue the work needed to use it. Over the next seventeen years, pipelines, technical knowledge and the organisation that became ENI made energy supply part of Italy's industrial reconstruction. Abroad, the Iranian agreement of 1957 made access possible on terms that also placed considerable risk on the Italian side.

The record resists an uncomplicated celebration. ENI acquired capabilities that the country had lacked, while its expansion required capital and depended heavily on a forceful presidency. Its relationships with politics and the press exposed difficulties that industrial success alone could not settle. A larger capacity to act made the quality of public oversight more consequential, not less.

From there, the twelfth triad enters the territory of work and civic life. Merloni's factory at Matelica shows an industrial possibility being taken up through an entrepreneur's own choices. Olivetti's experience turns attention towards institutions in which a community might have a say over the life shaped by enterprise. Neither story supplies a retrospective justification for Mattei. Together, they sharpen the distinction between acquiring the means of development and choosing its ends.

When a society gains the means to decide its own future, is it also capable of choosing what kind of future to build?
