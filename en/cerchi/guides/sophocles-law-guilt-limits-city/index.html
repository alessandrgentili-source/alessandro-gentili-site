Introduction — A Handful of Dust

The tragedian who brought authority face to face with what it cannot command.

Polyneices’ body lies outside the walls of Thebes. Creon has forbidden his burial. Having fought against his own city, he must remain exposed, denied the honours owed to the dead. Guards watch over the corpse. Yet someone has already performed the barest rites of burial, scattering dust upon the body.

The guards brush the traces away. They wait.

When the guards see Antigone beside her brother’s uncovered body, she cries out in grief. She scatters earth over him and pours libations. They seize her.

Brought before Creon, she faces a question that ought to settle the matter quickly: did she know about the prohibition? Yes, she answers. And when he asks whether she dared to break it, she owns her act.

The facts are established. The person who broke the order stands before the ruler and acknowledges what she has done. Yet the trial, in the broadest sense we can give that scene, begins precisely where it appears ready to end.

Creon has political reasons for his decree. Thebes has just survived a war between Eteocles and Polyneices, Oedipus’ sons, who died fighting each other. One defended the city; the other attacked it. The new ruler considers it essential to distinguish loyalty from treachery in public.

Antigone recognises an obligation the decree did not create and, in her eyes, cannot abolish: the religious and familial duty owed to the dead. Creon's edict crosses a boundary she believes no ruler has the right to cross.

Antigone will not recant. Creon sees her defiance as a challenge he cannot leave unpunished. Between them stand a sister who was afraid, a son who hears what the city is saying, and a prophet whose warning will come after the king has already ordered the punishment.

From the handful of dust swept away by the guards, the action will lead to a cave sealed in stone. Creon will reach Antigone there after changing his mind. She will already have taken her life; his son will die beside her, and the news will drive Eurydice to her death as well.

In another play, Oedipus the King, the ruler of Thebes confronts a different danger. Plague has devastated the city. The oracle demands that the pollution arising from the murder of the former king, Laius, be identified and removed. Oedipus pledges to find the culprit. He questions witnesses, compares their accounts, reconstructs events. What he discovers concerns him more intimately than he could have imagined.

The two stories are less opposed than they first appear. Oedipus, too, will meet the first accusations with suspicions of conspiracy; Creon, too, will eventually change his mind. In Sophocles, resistance to truth runs through the man who wishes to preserve order as well as the man who declares his intention to discover it.

Around them Sophocles builds a theatre in which power meets the frailty of human judgement. Laws cannot encompass every obligation; actions disclose their meaning only afterwards; bodies are abandoned, and a city may deny people even the right to mourn their dead.

The dust Antigone scattered over her brother can be brushed away. By the end, no one will find it nearly so easy to erase what was done in the name of the city.

Why Sophocles matters

Greek theatre inherited stories its audience already knew. Oedipus had killed his father and married his mother. The house of Thebes bore a long history of violence and misfortune. The heroes of the Trojan War had known achievement, betrayal, and rivalry.

Sophocles works with that shared inheritance. His audience may know the ending before a character discovers what is happening to him.

One of the finest qualities of his dramatic art arises from this distance.

In Oedipus the King, spectators watch the questioning with knowledge that the ruler has yet to acquire. When Oedipus curses the murderer of Laius, his words carry a meaning he cannot hear. The inquiry is rigorous; the audience senses its danger.

In Antigone, a seemingly clear political order reaches a point where the ruler's distinctions no longer suffice. Polyneices fought against Thebes, but he is also a brother and a dead man to whom certain rites are owed.

The playwright tests our ability to understand what our actions set in motion.

His importance reaches beyond literature into moral philosophy, political thought, legal theory, and the study of knowledge. The conflicts in his plays resist settlement by argument alone: they have consequences for bodies, families, and the city.

Sophocles has been read by Aristotle, Hegel, Freud, twentieth-century philosophers, and contemporary scholars. Their interpretations often disagree. Such disagreement also reveals the plays’ capacity to sustain different questions, provided we do not forget the world from which they came.

The human problem: acting without the whole truth

A decision begins with what we know, and what we believe we know. Its consequences reach people, relationships, and circumstances that may escape our foresight.

In Sophocles, that distance becomes unbearable.

Creon knows his reasons for the decree and possesses the power to enforce it. He fails to recognise the weight of the religious and familial obligations it violates. When Haemon and Tiresias offer other reasons, he initially takes them for attempts to weaken him.

Oedipus needs to know. The inquiry he undertakes to save Thebes forces him to question the story of his own life.

In Women of Trachis, Deianeira acts in the hope of winning back Heracles’ love. The result is terrible suffering, inflicted by a substance whose true nature she does not know.

These situations differ and call for different judgements. Together they make palpable the distance between intention, knowledge, and consequence.

Sophocles also carries the problem into the life of the city. A ruler's decision, the abandonment of a wounded man, the refusal of burial, or the welcome offered to a stranger become occasions on which a community reveals what it believes it owes to others.

The human question running through these works is demanding: how can we answer for actions whose significance exceeds what we understood when we acted?

A life in outline

Sophocles was probably born around 496 BCE in Colonus, in Attica, and died in 406 BCE. His long life encompassed Athens’ rise after the Persian Wars, the maturity of its imperial democracy, and almost the whole Peloponnesian War.

In 468 BCE he won his first recorded victory in the dramatic competitions, defeating Aeschylus. Ancient sources attributed more than a hundred plays to him; seven tragedies have come down to us intact. The surviving record also preserves a substantial portion of the satyr play The Trackers and fragments of other works.

Sophocles took an active part in civic institutions. In 440 BCE he served as a general during the conflict with Samos, in an Athenian command that included Pericles. An episode reported by Aristotle (Rhetoric III.18, 1419a) places him among the probouloi whose role came under scrutiny in the political crisis preceding the rule of the Four Hundred. The testimony matters, but it cannot establish his overall political outlook.

Aristotle credits Sophocles with introducing a third actor (Poetics 1449a), though the ancient tradition is not unanimous. What matters dramatically is what the technique made possible: scenes in which information passes between several voices, complicating conflict and unsettling a ruler’s certainty.

The plays must be dated with care. Philoctetes was performed in 409 BCE, while Oedipus at Colonus was staged posthumously in 401 BCE. Antigone is generally placed in the 440s BCE, though its precise date is less secure. The dates of many other plays remain disputed. We cannot arrange the three Theban stories according to the events of Oedipus’ life and then mistake that sequence for their order of composition.

A life that illuminates the work

Sophocles belonged to a city that celebrated theatre as part of its religious and civic life.

That belonging matters. His plays portray rulers, wars, public decisions, excluded people, and threatened communities. Their author knew a world in which political life unfolded through assemblies, magistracies, religious observances, and military campaigns.

The theatre belonged to that same world. During the major Dionysian festivals, audiences watched mythical stories in which they might recognise questions about their own collective existence.

Sophocles’ participation in public life prevents us from imagining him as an artist detached from institutions. Yet we lack the evidence to turn each tragedy into a declaration of his personal politics.

Perhaps the most compelling biographical connection lies in Oedipus at Colonus.

The play brings an ageing exile to the place where Sophocles himself was born. Oedipus arrives blind, led by his daughter Antigone, and asks for protection. Theseus, king of Athens, chooses to receive him.

Colonus becomes the site of a transformation. A man once associated with pollution may be welcomed and, through his mysterious death, become a source of protection for the city.

The geographical coincidence is striking. It does not warrant reading the play as the ageing poet's personal confession.

Instead, the tragedy imagines something a city might become: a community capable of recognising a stranger marked by misfortune and taking responsibility for his protection.

Athens and theatre as a civic institution

Fifth-century Athenian democracy rested on the direct participation of a defined part of the population: adult male citizens. Women, enslaved people, and many resident foreigners were excluded.

Tragedy grew within this society, but its forms and subjects cannot be reduced to a straightforward expression of democratic ideology.

Its stories often unfolded in cities ruled by kings. Their conflicts involved heroic families, oracles, gods, and obligations of kinship.

Yet the audience belonged to the Athenian polis. Questions about the legitimacy of command, justice, violence, and public speech could acquire meanings beyond the mythical action.

Theatre and community

At the City Dionysia, tragic performances formed part of a public festival dedicated to Dionysus.

The ritual character of the festival and the competitive nature of the theatre were intertwined with the city's prestige. Watching was a collective experience, though differences of social and political standing remained.

Tragedy gave shape to conflicts a community could recognise without being required to settle them on the spot.

Antigone's story made the burial of an enemy a public question. Oedipus the King placed authority under an obligation to pursue a truth that might undo it. Philoctetes challenged the means a military expedition was prepared to use to obtain what it needed.

The plays did not replace political debate. They made its difficulty palpable.

The chorus and the city

The chorus is essential to tragedy.

It enters the action, comments, fears, hopes, and interprets. It may share the values of the community it represents and change its judgement as events take an unforeseen turn.

In Antigone, the chorus of Theban elders witnesses the conflict's growing rigidity. It is not a modern public opinion, nor should it simply be made the voice of democracy.

Its presence reminds us that more people are implicated than those who confront one another directly.

The city remains on stage even when the quarrel seems confined to a family.

Disciplinary circles

Literature and dramatic form

Sophocles is first and foremost a dramatic poet. Meaning arises from action, language, the succession of scenes, the chorus, and the unequal distribution of knowledge between audience and characters.

The distance between what spectators know and what the protagonist does not is especially significant in Oedipus the King.

Moral philosophy

The plays raise questions about intention, ignorance, and the consequences of action.

Responsibility often comes into view once an act has produced irreversible effects. Sophocles presents situations later philosophy can interpret, without setting out a formal moral system.

Political philosophy

Creon, Oedipus, and Theseus exercise different kinds of authority.

Command encounters religious obligations, incomplete knowledge, supplication, and requests for protection. The quality of political judgement becomes visible in the lives of those who bear its effects.

History and religious anthropology

Oracles, ritual pollution, burial, supplication, and hero cult belong to the religious world of ancient Greece.

Understanding these practices is essential if we are to read the plays without replacing their categories with those of modern law or psychology.

Law and justice

The clash between command and obligation, the attribution of responsibility, and the treatment of transgression have made Sophocles important in the history of legal interpretation.

Such comparisons are illuminating only when we distinguish the tragic world from modern theories of punishment.

Psychology and psychoanalysis

The modern reception of Oedipus the King, especially through Freud, profoundly changed the play's cultural afterlife.

That interpretation belongs to the history of its influence and must be distinguished from ancient Greek conceptions of identity, kinship, and guilt.

Institutional ethics

In Philoctetes and Oedipus at Colonus, the wounded, the excluded, and the exiled become tests of the relationship between community and person.

These works may enrich contemporary reflection on institutions and their obligations, so long as the historical distance remains clear.

In mythical narrative order, the three stories of the house of Thebes run Oedipus the King → Oedipus at Colonus → Antigone. They are not a dramatic trilogy conceived and performed as a single cycle: each is an independent play, composed at a different moment.

That distinction matters for Creon as well. He appears in different circumstances and does not retain one unchanging position. In Oedipus the King, he resists Oedipus’ accusations and, at the end, will not decide the question of exile without consulting the god. In Antigone, he exercises power and imposes a punishment. In Oedipus at Colonus, he tries to return the exile to Theban control, going so far as to seize his daughters. A guide that reduced him to the tyrant of Antigone alone would miss an essential part of Sophocles’ theatre.

The securely dated performances are Philoctetes (409 BCE) and Oedipus at Colonus (401 BCE, posthumous); the chronology of the other plays remains a matter of scholarly debate.

Antigone — Who may command the dead?

Eteocles and Polyneices have killed each other outside Thebes, fighting on opposing sides. Creon grants one an honourable burial and leaves the other exposed. The distinction concerns the city that defended itself and the enemy who attacked it, yet the sentence also falls upon the sister of both dead men.

Antigone asks Ismene to help. Her sister hesitates, afraid of the decree and of her own position as a woman in a family already devastated. Antigone goes alone. The guards find a thin covering of dust on the body but do not know who placed it there; they remove it. They catch Antigone scattering fresh earth and pouring libations, and take her to the palace (Antigone, approximately lines 245–440).

Before Creon, Antigone does not deny what she did. She knew the prohibition and defied it. In lines 441–470, she refuses to accept that a human decree can annul the unwritten religious obligations she acknowledges towards her brother. Their confrontation begins once the facts are settled: each grants a different authority to the duties at stake.

The burial of Polyneices is not interchangeable with some general act of protest. In scattering earth over the corpse, Antigone restores at least the minimum funeral rite. Creon wants the offence against the city to remain visible even in death.

Ismene and the limits of solidarity

When Ismene learns of the sentence and asks to share her sister's fate, Antigone refuses. Their exchange in lines 536–560 has a harshness that an exclusively heroic reading may obscure. Antigone will not grant Ismene a share in the act she declined to perform. Loyalty to her dead brother coexists with a gesture of separation from her only living sister.

Haemon and the voice the ruler cannot hear

Haemon initially addresses his father with respect. Yet he reports what is said in Thebes: many citizens pity Antigone, but dare not speak openly. Creon responds as though an objection to the substance of his decree were a challenge to both his sovereignty and his paternal authority. Their dialogue (approximately lines 631–765) raises the political problem of listening and a familial one as well. Haemon speaks of the woman he is to marry; Creon speaks to the son he expects to govern.

Punishment and pollution

Creon decides to wall Antigone up alive in a stone chamber, leaving her a scant supply of food. In explaining the choice (lines 773–780), he hopes to prevent the city from incurring the ritual pollution that would attend a death directly inflicted by his hands. The cruelty lies partly in this show of caution. The ruler seeks to distance his authority from a death for which he has arranged the conditions.

Punishment is therefore part of the tragedy in the very manner of its administration. Antigone is held responsible for burying her brother; Creon would prefer not to bear the full responsibility for her end.

Tiresias, the chorus, and the order of the final acts

Tiresias announces that the refusal of burial has disrupted the sacrifices and disturbed the city's relationship with the gods (approximately lines 998–1090). Creon accuses the seer of venality. After Tiresias leaves, it is the chorus that persuades the ruler to act.

The chorus advises him first to free Antigone and then to bury Polyneices (lines 1091–1114). The messenger later reports that Creon went first to the dead man, performed the burial rites, and only afterwards reached the chamber (approximately lines 1192–1225). Both sequences are recorded in the play. We do not know that reversing the order would have saved Antigone; we do know that the attempt to put things right began after the catastrophe was already under way.

Antigone has taken her own life. Haemon will die after attempting to strike his father; Eurydice will die upon hearing of her son's death. At the end, a ruler remains who can name his ruin but cannot undo the acts that brought it about.

Creon — Power and the failure to listen

Creon takes charge of a city emerging from civil war. His fear that the distinction between those who defended Thebes and those who attacked it might disappear belongs to the political problem of the community's survival. For him, the decree proves that a new order exists.

Over the course of the play, that criterion hardens. Antigone invokes duties towards the dead; Haemon reports the city's murmurs; Tiresias announces a crisis in the sacrifices. Each voice brings knowledge Creon cannot produce for himself. He answers by recasting the disagreement as an attack on his authority: the young woman is insubordinate, his son is ruled by a woman, the seer must have interests of his own to protect.

The most disturbing turn is the order concerning the chamber. Leaving food for Antigone in the hope of avoiding the pollution of religious guilt (lines 773–780) reveals the frailty of Creon's justification. His command wills a death and, even as it orders that death, seeks distance from the person responsible for it.

A contrast with Governments Without Signatures

In the essay Governi senza firma (Governments Without Signatures), Alessandro Gentili follows forms of power capable of producing effects while obscuring who actually decides. An identifiable government, the essay suggests, can at least be challenged and replaced: where someone signs, there is somewhere to demand an account.

Creon exposes the limit of this condition. His responsibility is entirely visible. He stands before everyone and defends his decree. Yet visibility alone does not make the order susceptible to effective challenge. Antigone knows who commanded; Haemon knows whom to address; Tiresias knows the palace door. The opportunity to speak does not become an opportunity to change the command in time.

The comparison yields a useful distinction: identifying who made a decision and making that decision open to correction are connected but different problems. An unsigned apparatus threatens the first; Sophocles' Creon shows how solving the first can leave the second untouched.

Haemon and historical youth

Giovani e senilità del potere (Youth and the Senility of Power) describes a governing class that may lose its capacity to imagine and establish something new, whatever its members’ chronological age. Creon, newly arrived in power, is no literal embodiment of that senility. His failure is more particular: he cannot make room for a political truth that does not originate with him.

Haemon asks his father to consider what others see; Creon mistakes flexibility for defeat. The link to the essay therefore concerns one condition of beginning anew. Without the capacity to receive a reason from elsewhere, even a new political order may be closed from the outset. The difference remains: the essay examines a civilisation and its ruling classes; Sophocles places a father and son face to face, hours before an irreversible decision.

Oedipus the King — An inquiry that reaches its author

Plague has brought the people of Thebes to the palace doors. Oedipus sends to consult Delphi. The answer demands that the city be purified of the murderer of Laius. The ruler undertakes the inquiry that should restore the people's health and the city's stability.

In lines 216–275 he publicly pronounces an order against the still-unknown killer. No one is to receive him or speak to him, and a curse is to fall upon him. Oedipus announces a sanction that the audience knows will return upon its author.

Truth does not immediately find a ruler willing to hear it

Oedipus' resistance deserves as much attention as his determination to know. Tiresias, summoned to the palace, hesitates to speak. When he accuses the king, Oedipus accuses him in turn of complicity in the murder (approximately lines 345–365). He soon suspects Creon of plotting to seize the throne. In their confrontation (approximately lines 530–645), the pursuit of truth coexists with the ruler's determination to protect his position.

Here Oedipus resembles the Creon of Antigone: both receive intolerable news from Tiresias and both question his loyalty. The difference that emerges later lies in the routes by which knowledge arrives and what follows. Oedipus is overtaken by testimony he can no longer assemble into any other account; Creon recognises the error in his command but attempts to reverse it after the punishment has been set in motion.

The messenger and the shepherd

Jocasta recalls the killing of Laius at a crossroads, and suspicion begins to trouble Oedipus. A messenger from Corinth announces the death of Polybus, whom Oedipus believed to be his father. The news ought to reassure him about the oracle; instead it opens the way towards the discovery of his birth. The Corinthian tells how he received the infant from a Theban shepherd.

The old shepherd is questioned with increasing severity. Oedipus threatens him to make him speak (approximately lines 1140–1185). At last the testimony makes recognition unavoidable. The king learns that he killed Laius without knowing he was his father and married Jocasta without knowing she was his mother.

Truth has been assembled through people whose status and knowledge differ. It does not spring from the ruler's superior insight. Indeed, the decisive details come from people whose testimony he must demand or compel.

The Creon who appears at the end

After Jocasta's death and Oedipus' self-blinding, Creon returns to the stage. Oedipus asks him to send him into exile. Now the person to whom others turn for a decision, Creon wants first to establish the will of the god (in the closing part of the play). This is a different figure from the Creon who, in Antigone, will claim that his decree itself supplies the legitimacy of his punishment.

Evidence and the field of the visible

In Chi decide il Sole? (Who Decides the Sun?), an essay on the contemporary governance of visibility, the question is what an infrastructure selects and makes knowable before judgement begins. Oedipus inhabits no such technical order; the comparison rests on something more precise. When the messenger from Corinth arrives, information that seemed to absolve Oedipus changes the very questions the king is able to ask. Taken alone, the fact reassures him; placed beside an earlier testimony, it becomes disturbing.

The scene reveals how judgement depends on the order in which evidence emerges and on whose testimony is heard. The essay locates this problem in the architecture of the visible; Sophocles distributes it among human witnesses, memory, and the oracle's words. That is where the comparison ends, leaving its distinct objects intact.

Tiresias, sight, and pollution

The blind prophet knows the history of which the sighted king is ignorant. Tiresias’ blindness and Oedipus' self-blinding at the end of the play make knowledge and its cost matters of bodily experience.

The miasma weighing upon Thebes belongs to a ritual order. To understand it, we cannot simply substitute the modern notion of subjective guilt. Laius has been killed, and Thebes suffers the pollution before Oedipus learns the identity of the man he killed. The agent's defence will be heard later, in another play.

Guilt, ignorance, and responsibility

In Oedipus the King, we witness the outcome of the investigation. To learn how Oedipus himself judges what he did, we must also read Oedipus at Colonus. There, many years later, the old man defends himself before people who fear his name.

He tells the people of Colonus that the actions of his life were suffered rather than willed (approximately lines 258–274). When Creon reproaches him with killing his father and marrying his mother, Oedipus answers that he did not know who the man was against whom he struck, and did not know the identity of the woman he married (approximately lines 960–999). His defence keeps separate the deed, his knowledge of the people involved, and the condemnation others believe they may impose upon him.

He does not deny that Laius died by his hand. He challenges the judgement that would treat the act as though he had known he was killing his father. Ritual pollution, the history of the family, and an assessment of the agent do not coincide.

This is more precise than saying that 'tragedy goes beyond guilt'. The deed occurred; the unknown identity matters; shame and exclusion remain; and the man carrying the history of Thebes still asks to be heard.

Three situations that cannot be merged

Antigone knows the decree, deliberately performs the forbidden rite, and claims religious authority for her act. Deianeira, in Women of Trachis, means to bring about one result but causes another, devastating one, because she does not know the nature of what she is using. Ajax, in the prologue of his play, acts under a delusion caused by Athena: believing he is attacking the Achaean commanders, he slaughters animals instead. His experience introduces an ignorance unlike that of either Oedipus or Deianeira.

These stories cannot be arranged as degrees of liability under a single modern criminal law. Yet they make a useful distinction possible: knowing whom one is striking, intending the effect one brings about, and being in command of one's perceptions while acting are different questions.

Later philosophical reflection

In the Nicomachean Ethics (Books III and V), Aristotle distinguishes voluntary action, ignorance, and different kinds of error and injustice. In the Poetics, hamartia enters a different discussion, concerning the conditions of tragic reversal. The two inquiries should not be collapsed. Aristotle's theory postdates Sophocles, and the precise relationship between hamartia and Oedipus remains a matter of interpretation.

Nor is hybris a universal key. Significantly, in Antigone, Creon himself accuses Antigone of excess (approximately lines 480–483). The label is part of the conflict, spoken by one of its participants. Treating it as the play's neutral verdict would already be to judge in his favour.

From guilt to punishment

Who determines the punishment? By what authority? What may be punished: disobedience, intention, a person's identity, the body after death?

Antigone presents a ruler seeking to punish an acknowledged transgression while distancing himself from the pollution of his own punitive act. Oedipus the King presents a public sanction directed at an unknown person who will turn out to be the ruler himself. Oedipus at Colonus gives us the answer of the man who has been judged and driven away, and who insists on the difference between knowing and not knowing.

This is where Triad 14 can proceed towards two historically new questions. Foucault will investigate the practices and forms of knowledge that accompany the power to judge; Beccaria will ask what public, necessary, and proportionate limits should govern the power to inflict punishment. Neither perspective can be projected back into fifth-century BCE Greece.

Philoctetes — The wounded man the community needs again

Philoctetes lives on the island of Lemnos. The Achaeans abandoned him on their way to Troy because his wound and his cries of pain had become unbearable to his companions. Now they need what he possesses: the bow of Heracles. Odysseus brings Neoptolemus, Achilles' son, and asks him to obtain it through deceit.

The abandonment on Lemnos and the hero's eventual return to Troy were already part of epic tradition: the Iliad recalls Philoctetes on the island (II.718–725), while the summary of the Little Iliad transmitted by Proclus recounts his return. Sophocles reshapes this material around Odysseus' deception, Neoptolemus' choice, and the wounded man's resistance.

Odysseus' opening argument treats the abandoned man's suffering as an operational obstacle. Neoptolemus initially accepts the plan, but his encounter with Philoctetes changes his understanding of the mission. He watches and hears the man's attacks of pain, his mistrust, his loneliness—the man to whom he has promised loyalty. At last he returns the bow (Philoctetes, approximately lines 1280–1293), defying Odysseus' instructions.

What is really needed to take Troy?

The prophecy of Helenus, reported in the play, is less straightforward than a slogan about the necessity of the bow. Scholars debate the relationship between the weapon, Philoctetes' personal presence, his participation in the expedition, and the healing he has been promised. The closing scene, with the appearance of Heracles, brings the action back within a divine design without undoing what Neoptolemus has learned beside the wounded man.

The tragedy's interest centres on a person's resistance to the role assigned him by others. At first he was considered useless and left on Lemnos; later he becomes indispensable. At neither moment can his worth be measured wholly by what suits the Achaeans.

The distance between the centre and its consequences

The essay Le periferie del potere (The Peripheries of Power) considers places where decisions made elsewhere become everyday experience. The analogy with Philoctetes is limited but legible: the decision to abandon him comes from an army on the move, while its consequences are borne for years by a man left alone with his wounded body. When the centre returns to claim what it has lost, it finds someone who remembers. His wound did not cease to exist while the army was away.

Oedipus at Colonus — Finding a city

Oedipus arrives at Colonus, led by Antigone. The grove is sacred to the Eumenides. The inhabitants tell the stranger to leave; when they learn his name, they fear the pollution he might bring. Oedipus answers that his conduct must be judged in light of what he did not know (Oedipus at Colonus, approximately lines 258–274).

Theseus, king of Athens, knows what exile means and recognises something of the human condition in his guest's story. He, too, was raised away from home and has faced dangers, he says (approximately lines 551–568). He chooses to protect Oedipus.

Hospitality and force

Theseus' choice will be tested. Creon arrives from Thebes and tries to gain possession of Oedipus, having Antigone and Ismene abducted. Theseus denounces the violation of the obligations governing supplication, the presence of a stranger, and the city's justice (approximately lines 897–1040). Asylum becomes something a ruler must defend in practice, not a generous sentiment.

Here Creon is yet another figure. The needs of his own city lead him to violate the protection granted by Athens. Comparison with the Creon of Oedipus the King, who deferred a judgement about exile until the will of the god had been sought, prevents a simplistic, linear psychology of the character.

The son who asks for burial

Polyneices comes in turn to beg his father's help. Oedipus rejects him and curses the struggle between his sons. Before departing, Polyneices asks his sisters to ensure that he receives funeral rites if he dies. Antigone begs him, without success, to abandon his campaign against Thebes (approximately lines 1399–1446). The future conflict over burial is already present in the family history, although the two tragedies were not written as chapters of a single trilogy.

The secret of the ending

Oedipus dies mysteriously. Theseus must guard the place and pass on its secret according to precise instructions: the exile's presence, now sacred, promises protection to the city that sheltered him. Antigone and Ismene cannot learn everything they wish to know.

The hospitable city also has a political and religious interest in keeping that secret. The play neither erases Oedipus' suffering nor resolves every tension in reconciliation. It presents an authority that accepts an obligation towards a stranger, then assumes the cost, the defence, and the mystery of that obligation.

Ajax, Electra, and Women of Trachis — Other forms of tragedy

Ajax: an enemy's pity

The action opens with Athena and Odysseus outside Ajax's tent. Furious because Achilles' arms have been awarded to Odysseus, Ajax had intended to attack the Achaean commanders. Athena turned his fury against the livestock. Ajax believes he has slaughtered his enemies (Ajax, lines 1–133). Odysseus looks upon the humiliated man, and it is he, the rival, who feels pity. The play moves towards Ajax's suicide and the dispute over his corpse, in which Odysseus will defend his right to burial.

Athena, the failure of perception, suffering, and the dignity of burial introduce a conflict that cannot be fully contained by the thesis of political command. Ajax asks what remains of honour when a human being acts under a god-induced delusion, and when his adversary recognises his own vulnerability in witnessing that person's fall.

Electra: the cost of remembering

Electra awaits the return of Orestes and revenge for the murder of Agamemnon. Her grief does not belong to the past alone: it governs the household's present, determines her relationship with her mother, and makes waiting a way of life. Her brother's return leads to the deaths of Clytemnestra and Aegisthus.

The play sets against the claims of revenge the blood relationships that revenge must sever. There is no single mistaken ruler here. Inherited violence seems to offer its own continuation as the only available form of redress.

Women of Trachis: what Deianeira did not know

Deianeira places her hopes of recovering Heracles in a garment soaked with the blood of Nessus. She does not know that the supposed love charm is poison. The hero's body is ravaged, and the woman understands the meaning of her act too late.

Heracles' suffering and Deianeira's belong to different experiences. He undergoes its physical effect; she realises she caused it without intending to. The play tests any reading of Sophocles that concentrates exclusively on political power. Family, desire, and ignorance have a force of their own here.

Original framework — The four thresholds of limitation

An interpretive proposal from Cerchi d’inchiostro

These four thresholds are not a theory devised by Sophocles. They are a way of reading four plays through the problem of authority. The other three, especially Women of Trachis and Ajax, remain essential checks: Sophocles' theatre cannot be exhausted by the government of the city.

Each threshold distinguishes an act, the criterion by which it is judged, and the point at which that criterion proves insufficient.

First threshold — The ruler who claims authority over the dead

Antigone · lines 441–470; 773–780

Act: Antigone covers her brother's body; Creon orders her to be immured alive. Criterion: the decree distinguishes the defender of the city from its enemy. Limit: the political distinction is extended beyond death, while the ruler seeks to evade the ritual pollution of the punishment he himself has ordered.

The scene allows us to distinguish two things: having the power to forbid burial and possessing sufficient authority to deny every obligation towards the dead. The problem is therefore how far command can extend, as much as it is a problem of disobedience.

Second threshold — A sentence pronounced against an unknown man

Oedipus the King · lines 216–275; 345–365; 1140–1185

Act: Oedipus proclaims a curse upon Laius' murderer. Criterion: identifying the culprit should restore the city to ritual purity. Limit: the sentence is pronounced before the man pronouncing it knows his own place in the story. Oedipus, too, defends himself by accusing those who bring unwelcome news.

This threshold does not set a good king who seeks truth against a bad one who rejects it. It reveals inquiry as an exercise of authority, whose worth also depends on its capacity to reach the person directing it.

Third threshold — Necessity encounters a person

Philoctetes · prologue; lines 1280–1293; final scene

Act: the army returns to demand the bow of the man it abandoned. Criterion: without the weapon, and under disputed conditions of the prophecy, taking Troy appears impossible. Limit: Neoptolemus discovers that the mission's required means cannot be separated from the will and suffering of the person who possesses them.

He returns the bow and breaks Odysseus' plan. The new distinction concerns the necessity of an objective and the legitimacy of the means used to pursue it. Heracles' appearance will resolve the action within a religious order the young man does not control.

Fourth threshold — Asylum that must be defended

Oedipus at Colonus · lines 258–274; 551–568; 897–1040

Act: Theseus grants Oedipus protection; Creon tries to seize the exile and his daughters. Criterion: the Athenian ruler recognises the obligations of supplication, while the Theban invokes his city's interests. Limit: hospitality has substance only if a community will defend it when defence becomes costly.

It is the inhabitants of Colonus who initially fear the stranger and would send him away. Theseus chooses to welcome him, without overlooking the sacred and political benefit that Oedipus' eventual death may bring to Athens.

What the four thresholds disclose together

Antigone's punishment, the ban against the unknown murderer, the lie demanded of Neoptolemus, and the asylum defended by Theseus share a structure: a reason advanced by the person acting encounters someone who must bear its cost. The reasons differ, as do the available knowledge and the opportunity to correct the act.

The fourth threshold complicates the first. In Thebes, Creon extends his power to the dead; at Colonus, Theseus assumes an obligation towards a stranger. Ajax and Women of Trachis prevent us from mistaking that distinction for a principle capable of explaining every part of Sophocles.

A map of responsibility

Another dimension: obligations towards those who do not decide

Alongside what an agent knows, Sophocles places those on whom other people's choices fall: Polyneices unburied, Philoctetes abandoned, Antigone entombed, Oedipus exiled. Judgement about responsibility must see both sides: the person who acts and the person reached by the act.

Key ideas

A transgression may be beyond dispute while the authority behind the command remains contested. In Antigone, no one disputes who scattered earth over Polyneices' body. The conflict concerns the force of the decree.

Punishment remains part of the decision even when power seeks to distance itself from the outcome. Creon's cave and scant provision of food expose this contradiction.

An inquiry must be capable of reaching the person who conducts it. Oedipus' curse upon Laius' killer turns back on him once the witnesses' accounts are assembled.

Acting in ignorance takes different forms. Oedipus does not know the identities of those involved; Deianeira does not know what the supposed charm will do; Ajax suffers a delusion imposed by a goddess.

Collective necessity does not, by itself, determine the quality of the means chosen. Neoptolemus discovers that the expedition's success does not simply entitle it to treat Philoctetes as an object.

Hospitality can be an exacting political choice. Theseus protects the exile even when Creon attempts to take him away.

Key concepts

Polis

The city understood as a community of institutions, cults, bonds, and conflicts. In Sophocles, the destruction of a family frequently implicates the civic order.

Nomos and agrapta nomima

Nomos may mean a law or an accepted custom. The unwritten ordinances invoked by Antigone belong to religious duties that do not derive from the ruler's decree (Antigone, lines 450–460).

Philia

The bond with loved ones, companions, and those counted as 'friends'. Creon and Antigone also struggle over who is owed loyalty—even beyond death (Antigone, approximately lines 182–190 and 520–523).

Dikē

Justice, judgement, or a just order, according to context. In Oedipus at Colonus, the word also belongs to the dispute between Theseus and Creon over the violation of asylum.

Miasma

Ritual pollution. It can affect a community without amounting to a modern judgement of subjective guilt. It matters in Oedipus the King and in Creon's explanation of Antigone's punishment.

Hamartia

A Greek word for error or fault. Its role as a category of tragedy was subsequently developed by Aristotle (Poetics, 1453a). It should not be attributed to Sophocles as a technical formula that explains all his plays.

Hybris

Excess or aggression, with meanings that vary according to the circumstances. In Antigone, Creon himself accuses the heroine of excess; the word must be read in the voice that speaks it.

Tychē

Fortune and unforeseen turns of events. In Oedipus the King, the relations between apparent chance, origins, and identity prevent the story from being read as a simple scheme of guilt and punishment.

Supplication

A ritual request for protection. In Oedipus at Colonus, it requires the city to decide what welcome to extend to a stranger.

Recognition

A change in knowledge that transforms a relationship or an identity. Aristotle's discussion of anagnorisis helps illuminate Oedipus the King, without replacing its dramatic construction.

Constellation

The relationships gathered here are of different kinds. Some are literary precedents; others belong to a shared civic culture; still others are documented instances of reception. Contemporary comparisons are editorial readings, not claims of historical descent.

Precursors

Homer — The return of a body. In the Iliad (Book XXIV), Priam enters Achilles' tent to ask for Hector's body. A living enemy begs his adversary to return the dead. The connection with Ajax and Antigone concerns the point beyond which vengeance must stop.

Aeschylus — Judgement interrupts revenge. In the Oresteia, the establishment of Athena's court gives a public form to a cycle of family killings. Sophocles' Electra preserves the weight of revenge within a different dramatic structure; Antigone offers no equivalent institutional settlement.

The city and its interlocutors

Thucydides — War and the meaning of words. In his account of civil war at Corcyra (History of the Peloponnesian War III.82–83), Thucydides describes how faction alters the public meanings attached to words. Creon imposes on Thebes a distinction between friend and enemy that he intends to extend even to burial. The comparison does not make the two works accounts of the same event; it places them before the vulnerability of civic judgement when language is enlisted in war. A different comparison concerns the unwritten obligations recalled in Pericles' Funeral Oration (II.37): they are not the same as Antigone's religious duties, but the distance between them is instructive.

Euripides — Tragedy within the family. Medea offers a precise comparison with Women of Trachis. In both, a damaged marriage has devastating consequences, yet Deianeira acts in ignorance of the lethal power of her means, while Medea knows the violence she is preparing. The distinction prevents a facile identification of suffering with responsibility.

Afterlives, interpretations, disagreements

Aristotle — Recognition and reversal. The Poetics takes Oedipus the King as a major example of a plot in which recognition transforms the protagonist's fate. This technical account of tragedy belongs to a later period than Sophocles himself.

Hegel — Two ethical orders in conflict. In the Phenomenology of Spirit, Antigone becomes the occasion for a reading of the tension between family and political community. Our focus on the reach of punishment is narrower: it attends to the decree, the body, and the cave without claiming to exhaust the ethical collision.

Freud — The psychoanalytic afterlife of Oedipus. The tragedy became central to a theory of desire and kinship. The myth as Freud reread it and Sophocles' dramatic action are distinct historical objects, both important.

Michel Foucault — Truth distributed among witnesses. In Truth and Juridical Forms, Foucault reads the inquiry in Oedipus the King as the progressive assembly of fragments of truth: oracle, ruler, messenger, and shepherd. A low-status witness can speak a truth that overturns a king's confidence. The guide to Michel Foucault takes up this reading. Foucault's historical inquiry into legal practices remains distinct from the play's religious order.

Judith Butler — The claims of kinship. Antigone's Claim examines the norms of kinship and recognition through its protagonist. This contemporary reception deserves attention without turning Antigone into a spokesperson for modern theories.

The Cerchi d’inchiostro corpus

Manzoni — When accusation becomes procedure. The History of the Infamous Column (Storia della colonna infame) offers a more precise connection than a general affinity between power and suffering: trial, suspicion, and failures of judgement become documented history. The setting is that of early modern institutions. The connection with Beccaria belongs to Manzoni's familial and intellectual history—he was Beccaria's grandson. The third guide of the triad, on Cesare Beccaria, takes up that connection.

The present

Governi senza firma explores power that is difficult to attribute; Creon reveals how difficult it can be to correct power whose author is perfectly identifiable. Fenomenologia del potere studies language that trains people to obey; in Antigone, that language takes the explicit form of a decree and a sentence. Chi decide il Sole? examines the architecture of the visible; Oedipus the King distributes knowledge among witnesses whom the ruler initially rejects and later questions. These are comparisons between distinct problems, not evidence that Sophocles foresaw modernity.

Legacy — The many lives of Sophocles

Antigone has moved through political and moral philosophy, thought about kinship, legal theory, and arguments about civil disobedience.

Its protagonist has been read as a figure of religious piety, familial duty, political resistance, and the limits of norms of recognition.

These readings do not always agree: George Steiner traces their long and often contested history in Antigones (1984). Their divergence makes it necessary to return to the play, in which Antigone's position also contains moments of rigidity and isolation.

Oedipus the King has profoundly influenced the theory of tragedy and psychoanalysis. Its structure of inquiry continues to interest philosophers of knowledge and readers concerned with the relationship between truth and power.

Philoctetes offers a dramatic experience of bodily vulnerability and exclusion that may be brought into conversation with contemporary moral questions without erasing the work's mythical world.

Oedipus at Colonus sustains reflection on hospitality, old age, and the relation between the memory of guilt and the possibility of being welcomed.

The range of its reception shows how Sophocles continues to generate different questions. Every interpretation must nevertheless encounter the text's resistance: what cannot be turned into a modern category without something essential being lost.

The contemporary wound — The unnamed dead and the order that cannot be undone

An interpretation from Cerchi d’inchiostro

Polyneices' body lies exposed outside Thebes because Creon wishes political enmity to survive its object. Antigone returns to scatter earth over her brother. From that scene comes a question that travels across time in changing forms: what may a community do to a person's body after life has ended?

A responsibility documented in the present

The International Committee of the Red Cross devoted 'Humanity after Life: Respecting and Protecting the Dead' (3 April 2020) to dignified treatment of the dead in war and other situations of violence, and the legal factsheet 'Legal factsheet on missing persons and their families' (15 November 2023) to missing people and their relatives. The documents describe obligations concerning the search for missing persons, respectful handling of remains, and efforts to identify them.

The difference from Antigone's world matters. Creon deliberately leaves an enemy unburied as a mark of condemnation. Today the problem may involve scattered bodies, remains difficult to identify, fragmentary information, and investigative and forensic work governed by specific legal rules. Every reasonable effort must be made to establish identity; failure to achieve it does not, by itself, prove a breach of duty. The wish of families to know what happened to those they love remains at the heart of these protections.

The theatre offers no technical solution. It helps us understand that what is done with remains also reaches the living: the time spent by a mother searching, a grave without a name, the relationship between an authority and what it owes the dead.

From an identifiable author to the possibility of redress

Governi senza firma examines the dispersal of responsibility across institutions; Creon shows how little it may achieve, by itself, to find the person who decided. Everyone in the play knows which ruler issued the decree. Yet messages reach him without producing a timely change.

The present-day question can be put more precisely: who has the power to revise a decision when new evidence emerges, and by what routes can the person affected reach that authority? Identifying who signed is the first step; a real possibility of remedy is the second. Tragedy shows the distance between them.

Words that precede punishment

In Fenomenologia del potere, the language of institutions trains people in adjustment and caution. In Antigone, obedience is demanded openly, but Creon's speech likewise establishes a classification: friend of the city, enemy of the city, worthy or unworthy of honour. When a political category determines the fate of a corpse, the name assigned to a person becomes part of the treatment they receive.

These are historically different modes of command. The common question concerns the words that justify an action and the effects that follow.

What remains outside the frame

Chi decide il Sole? examines how the selection of information shapes the conditions of contemporary judgement. The Corinthian messenger in Oedipus the King brings apparently reassuring news: Polybus has died without being killed by his supposed son. Only when this information is connected to the origins of an infant entrusted to a shepherd does its meaning change. The analogy concerns relations between pieces of information, while the devices that order them remain utterly different.

Haemon's image also calls for caution. Giovani e senilità del potere is concerned with the historical capacity to create new forms; in Thebes, a son, caught in an immediate family conflict, tries to make the city's hidden voice heard by the ruler. The distance between the works rules out equivalence but leaves open a question about the maturity of those who wield force: can a power that is still young have already stopped learning?

The modern world possesses procedures, forensic techniques, institutions, and remedies unknown to Sophocles' characters. These are important achievements, but they do not remove the need to establish who receives an objection, who is able to act, and when. Antigone and Creon arrive at the cave at different moments; a decision may change and still fail to reach the person who needed it.

Limits and interpretive controversies

Theatre without a treatise

Sophocles' tragedies are not chapters of a political or moral theory. The reading of 'authority confronting its limits' principally organises four plays. Electra, Ajax, and Women of Trachis compel us to recognise the independent force of family revenge, divine intervention, suffering, and ignorance.

Antigone's reasons

Antigone does not always speak in the same way. In lines 904–920, she argues that a brother cannot be replaced once both parents have died, whereas a husband or child might be. The argument introduces a criterion of familial uniqueness that complicates her earlier appeal to unwritten divine ordinances. Some philologists have challenged the authenticity of these lines; the passage survives in the manuscript tradition and was already known to Aristotle (Rhetoric III.16, 1417a). The debate remains open and bears on the heroine's characterisation.

The returning character

Creon is a different kind of ruler in each of the three Theban plays. His function depends on the dramatic situation. We should not infer an unchanging psychology in Oedipus the King and Oedipus at Colonus from Antigone alone.

Oedipus, the sacred, and modern reception

Oedipus' defence of himself in Oedipus at Colonus belongs to a world of oracles, ritual pollution, and supplication. Freud, Foucault, and interpretations grounded in criminal law belong to later intellectual histories. Their readings matter, but they address questions of their own.

Biographical evidence and translation

Ancient testimonies about Sophocles' life vary in reliability. Likewise, terms such as nomos, philia, hybris, and miasma must be read in context and in the voice of the person speaking. Textual notes and modern translations need to be used with a distinction between philological decisions, critical interpretations, and hypotheses.

Contemporary evidence

The humanitarian questions surrounding the dead and the missing have their own legal sources today. Comparison with Antigone can open a reader's attention, but contemporary legal rules come from conventions, practice, and specialist documents, not from Greek tragedy.

Illuminating details

Sophocles took part in Athenian public life. His civic experience makes his theatre of power and the city particularly interesting, without entitling us to identify his characters directly with his biography.

Audiences often knew Oedipus' story. The force of the investigation did not depend on a surprise ending alone. Spectators could watch the king's search already knowing essential parts of the truth.

In Ajax, an enemy feels pity. Odysseus, who received Achilles' arms, watches Ajax's madness in the prologue and recognises their shared vulnerability; later he will defend Ajax's burial.

Philoctetes' wound interrupts the action itself. His pain changes the relationships between characters and the rhythm of the play.

Oedipus at Colonus was performed after Sophocles' death. The play is linked to a place significant in his life, but must be read first through its dramatic structure.

Only part of his work survives. The loss of most of his plays limits our overall view and calls for care in reconstructing his artistic development.

Common misreadings

  1. Antigone is simply a heroine of modern individual autonomy. She acts primarily within religious and familial obligations; her relationship with Ismene and lines 904–920 complicate any idealisation.
  2. Creon is identical throughout the Theban plays. He occupies very different positions in Oedipus the King and Oedipus at Colonus from the one he holds in Antigone.
  3. Oedipus always listens, whereas Creon always refuses. Oedipus accuses Tiresias and Creon of conspiracy and forces the shepherd to answer him.
  4. Ignorance means the act never happened. Oedipus does not deny Laius' death; he contests the judgement passed on a deed committed without knowing the victim's identity.
  5. Ajax's madness is the same as Deianeira's error. Athena intervenes in what Ajax perceives; Deianeira does not know the properties of the substance she uses.
  6. Philoctetes is only the indispensable weapon. The man himself, his will, and the content of the prophecy are part of the dramatic tension.
  7. The three Theban plays form a trilogy in narrative order. They are independent works from different moments in Sophocles' career.
  8. Freud's reading coincides with the ancient author's intention. Freud belongs to the modern reception of Oedipus the King.

Notice how that person responds.

Read Antigone, Oedipus the King, Philoctetes, and Oedipus at Colonus in full.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Sophocles?

Sophocles was an Athenian tragic poet of the fifth century BCE. Alongside Aeschylus and Euripides, he is one of the three major Greek tragedians from whom complete plays survive. Seven of his many tragedies have come down to us intact.

What are Sophocles' major works?

The seven surviving tragedies are Ajax, Antigone, Women of Trachis, Oedipus the King, Electra, Philoctetes, and Oedipus at Colonus.

What is the central conflict in Antigone?

It is the clash between Creon's decree forbidding the burial of Polyneices and the religious and familial obligations Antigone recognises. The play questions the grounds of authority and the consequences of its rigidity.

Why does Antigone disobey Creon?

She believes that duties towards her dead brother and unwritten divine ordinances carry an authority that a ruler's decree cannot abolish.

Does Creon stand for just or unjust law?

Creon defends a political distinction between those who protected the city and those who attacked it. The play shows how that reason becomes destructive when he refuses to acknowledge other obligations or listen to objections.

What does Oedipus the King mean?

The play stages a search for truth that leads Oedipus to discover his identity and the significance of acts committed without full knowledge. Knowledge, ritual pollution, and human limitation are central.

Is Oedipus guilty if he did not know?

Oedipus killed Laius without knowing he was his father and married Jocasta without knowing she was his mother. In Oedipus at Colonus, he explicitly defends the involuntary character of these aspects of his history (lines 258–274 and 960–999). The religious world of tragedy nevertheless recognises pollution affecting the city. A responsible answer must distinguish the deed, the identities he did not know, his own assessment of what he did, and its consequences for others.

What does hybris mean?

In Greek culture, the word concerns forms of excess or aggression. It can help us understand conduct that crosses recognised limits, but it cannot explain every tragedy by itself.

What happens in Philoctetes?

The Achaeans try to recover the bow of Heracles from Philoctetes, a warrior they had abandoned years earlier because of a serious wound. The conflict involves deception, military necessity, and personal dignity.

Why is Oedipus at Colonus important?

It portrays the ageing, exiled Oedipus being welcomed by Theseus in Athens. The play examines supplication, hospitality, the sacred, belonging, and burial.

How do Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides differ?

They were Athenian tragedians with distinctive dramatic and poetic methods. A meaningful comparison must begin with their works and with the different ways they stage conflicts among human beings, cities, gods, and inherited traditions.

What is the connection between Sophocles and Freud?

Freud draws on the myth and play of Oedipus in developing psychoanalytic theory. His interpretation belongs to the work's modern reception and is not identical to its meaning in ancient Greece.

What is the connection between Sophocles and Foucault?

Foucault reads Oedipus the King in the lectures collected as Truth and Juridical Forms, examining the relationship between knowledge, testimony, and political power.

Why does Sophocles still matter today?

Consider the punishment imposed on Antigone: Creon leaves food in the chamber where she is walled up, hoping to avoid directly polluting the city (lines 773–780). The reader encounters a decision that seeks to evade its own cost. Or consider Oedipus' defence in Oedipus at Colonus: he performed an act without knowing the identity of the person he struck. These scenes allow precise questions about authority and judgement, without turning Greek theatre into a source of modern law.

Editorial conclusion — The time left to change

The chorus has just listened to Tiresias. It urges Creon to open the chamber where Antigone is confined and then give Polyneices burial. The ruler, who until moments ago treated dissent as a challenge to his authority, agrees.

The messenger traces his steps. Creon first goes to Polyneices' body, performs the rites, and has a mound raised over it. Then he reaches the chamber. Antigone is dead. Haemon is there: he turns his sword against his father, then turns it on himself.

The decision has been corrected. The correction has come too late to save the person who needed it.

The tragedy does not prove that a different order of those last acts would have averted the deaths. It shows, with terrible precision, the weight of time between an objection finally heard and an order changed. Creon eventually understands. He has begun to yield to the reasons brought by others, and that is why he knows how irreparable the consequences of his resistance have become.

A man able to impose a punishment had believed he could also control the manner in which that punishment would implicate him. The ration of food in the chamber was meant to keep pollution away from the city; mourning reaches the palace instead. Creon returns among the living bearing the deaths of his own family.

His last power is no longer the power to command. It is the terrible, insufficient power to recognise what he has done.
