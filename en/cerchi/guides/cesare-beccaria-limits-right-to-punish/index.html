Introduction — A Punishment That Cannot Be Undone

Law, evidence and the measure of punishment: how a society can protect public safety without yielding to arbitrary power.

In January 1792, in Milan, a commission in Austrian Lombardy is working on a new criminal code. A minority opinion on capital punishment, conventionally dated to the commission’s meeting of 24 January, bears the signatures of three commissioners. One is Cesare Beccaria. Almost twenty-eight years have passed since the anonymous appearance of Dei delitti e delle pene (On Crimes and Punishments), the book that made his name known across Europe. The author now sits within the administration itself, facing a question that philosophical debate can formulate with clarity but which the law must settle for actual people and actual courts.

The majority wishes to preserve a wider place for capital punishment. Beccaria, Francesco Gallarati Scotti and Paolo Risi argue instead for its abolition in nearly all cases, retaining one reservation: a prisoner who, despite being securely held, still possesses the connections and capacity to help overthrow the state. Their Voto, or minority opinion, rests on the argument that death is ordinarily less necessary and less effective than a lasting punishment. It adds a further consideration, one that should give any court pause. Judges decide on human evidence; an execution, once carried out, cannot be recalled. A later review may establish that a person was innocent. It cannot bring that person back to life.

In his treatise of 1764, Beccaria had attacked torture, the savagery of exemplary punishments and the claim that the social contract could confer a right to impose death. In 1792 that contractual argument is not repeated: the signatories acknowledge the sovereign's authority to prescribe death if it is judged necessary. The distance between the two texts makes the question of evidence more demanding. Even a scrupulous procedure can go wrong. How much power should a legal order grant itself when it knows that its knowledge can never be absolute?

To understand the question, we must return to Milan in the 1760s: to a circle of young aristocrats arguing about money, trade, administration and trials; and to a small book that changes the language in which Europe discusses punishment. Its author does not ask society to abandon the task of defending itself. He asks it to explain by what right it inflicts suffering, to limit the amount it inflicts, and to justify its necessity in public.

The difficulty is already present in the word on which his project depends: security. Security must protect citizens from private violence. In the mouth of authority, it can also demand obedience without sufficient proof that what is demanded is necessary. Beccaria obliges us to follow that word into the acts it authorises: a statute, an interrogation, a sentence, a life subjected to punishment.

Cesare Beccaria at a glance

Cesare Beccaria Bonesana was born in Milan on 15 March 1738 and died there on 28 November 1794. Educated at the Jesuit college in Parma, he graduated in law at Pavia in 1758. He entered the world of the Lombard Enlightenment, working closely with Pietro and Alessandro Verri and the Accademia dei Pugni. In 1764 he anonymously published Dei delitti e delle pene (On Crimes and Punishments) in Livorno. It became one of the most influential works in the history of European penal thought.

The book proposes that punishment should rest on what is necessary to preserve society. It seeks to free punishment from cruelty and caprice by subjecting it to public, proportionate and predictable laws. It opposes judicial torture and capital punishment within the ordinary operation of civil government. Preventing crime, it argues, is a more intelligent undertaking than multiplying spectacles of suffering.

Beccaria also wrote about currency, commerce, style and economic science. From 1769 he taught political economy in Milan; from 1771 he spent many years in the administration of Austrian Lombardy. The Enlightenment thinker and the public official belong to the same life, although the two roles did not always sit comfortably together.

Where to begin: Dei delitti e delle pene (1764, subsequently revised); Del disordine e de' rimedi delle monete nello Stato di Milano nell'anno 1762 (1762); Ricerche intorno alla natura dello stile (1770); the lectures published posthumously as Elementi di economia pubblica (1804); and the Voto on capital punishment (1792, with Gallarati Scotti and Risi).

Why he still matters

European discussion of criminal justice had critics of cruelty, theorists of the social contract and advocates of procedural reform before Beccaria. The achievement of his book lies in gathering problems that had often been treated separately and making them answer to a common political question. If society exists to make life together more secure, by what authority may it inflict suffering beyond what is needed to preserve that security?

The question reaches the legislator who determines the penalty, the judge who decides what happened, the procedure through which evidence is gathered, and the authority that executes the sentence. It reaches the language of justification itself. An old practice can be called necessary simply because it has always been performed. Beccaria insists that custom submit to reasons capable of being examined in public.

Modern procedural safeguards and constitutional protections have a history of their own. It would be misleading to transplant them wholesale into his treatise. Beccaria wrote before their later legal articulation. Yet in his pages the direction of the question addressed to power changes: confidence in a well-intentioned ruler gives way to the demand that a law and a punishment be justified to those who will bear their consequences.

The human problem: who guards the limit?

Every community must confront the harm caused when the conditions of living together are violated. A victim of theft or violence wants protection, redress and a credible response. Public authority arises in part from the promise that this response will no longer depend on the victim's strength, the wealth of a family or the desire for private revenge.

The promise carries a difficulty within it. The state must possess coercive means if it is to restrain the coercion of others. If rulers are free to decide what counts as a crime, how to establish it and how much pain to inflict, protection loses its meaning. Force changes hands, while the individual's exposure to someone else's whim remains.

Beccaria seeks a principle that will constrain power precisely when power is persuaded of its own righteousness. That principle is necessity, expressed through general laws, proportionate penalties, procedures open to scrutiny and preventive measures. A society may punish; to do so legitimately, it must also know where to stop.

A life in outline

1738–1758. Born into the Milanese patriciate, he studies in Parma and earns his law degree at Pavia. His education takes place in the world he will later challenge: a body of learning grounded in tradition, authority and language largely inaccessible to ordinary citizens.

1760–1762. A family conflict over his marriage to Teresa Blasco marks his entry into adult life. Reading Montesquieu's Persian Letters helps redirect his philosophical interests. Within Pietro Verri's circle, he joins discussions of Milanese economic affairs and publishes his pamphlet on currency.

1763–1766. Among the members of the Pugni, he examines the workings of criminal justice. Dei delitti e delle pene appears anonymously in 1764. Further editions, controversies and translations follow. His visit to Paris with Alessandro Verri in 1766 confirms his European reputation and opens a personal breach with his Milanese friends.

1768–1771. He is appointed to the chair of cameral science and economics at the Scuole palatine; lectures begin in January 1769. He publishes Ricerche intorno alla natura dello stile in 1770. In 1771 he enters public administration.

1771–1794. For more than twenty years he undertakes government work on economic questions, provisioning, money and reform. He serves on the criminal-law commission and signs the minority opinion on capital punishment in 1792. He dies in Milan in 1794.

A life that illuminates the work

Beccaria approaches criminal justice with interests formed outside the courtroom: moral philosophy, the passions, economics and the search for regularities in human conduct. Alessandro Verri brings to the discussions experience closer to legal practice, acquired through his knowledge of trials and prisons. Pietro Verri urges Beccaria on, accompanies the writing and intervenes extensively in the book's structure. Philological research has revealed how substantial this collaboration was. It does not erase Beccaria's recognised authorship; it gives that authorship a more intricate historical shape.

His interest in calculation also has a precise setting. In Tentativo analitico sui contrabbandi, Beccaria examines the relationships among duties, the value of goods and the advantage to be gained from smuggling. The aim is to understand what a rule actually causes people to do. The same impulse leads him, when writing about punishment, to distrust spectacular threats that leave conduct unchanged. Economic calculation, however, works with stated variables. Suffering and the value of a human life resist conversion into a universal legal equation.

His subsequent career offers another test. Asking in 1764 whether a citizen could have surrendered the right to his own life is one thing; submitting an opinion to a sovereign about to approve a criminal code is another. The Voto of 1792 retains the criticism of capital punishment as an ordinary sanction but does not repeat that early contractual argument and allows an extreme political exception. Administrative experience makes the conflict plain: the necessity invoked to protect the state may contend with the necessity of limiting what the state can do.

Milan, the Accademia dei Pugni and Il Caffè

Beccaria's Milan belongs to Lombardy under the Habsburg monarchy. Its Enlightenment does not develop in a laboratory detached from power. It encounters an administration pursuing reforms, eager to understand and regulate trade, taxation, justice and population. The language of public utility can open spaces of freedom, but it can also strengthen the government's capacity to intervene. This double possibility will accompany the history of reform.

The Accademia dei Pugni, gathered around Pietro Verri, is a lively forum impatient with a culture of unchallengeable authorities. Its members have varied intellectual backgrounds and are willing to read economic problems alongside philosophy, or criminal procedure alongside social custom. Alessandro Verri serves, among other roles, as a Protector of Prisoners (an office concerned with prisoners and judicial practice) and brings practical legal experience into the discussion. Published between 1764 and 1766, Il Caffè makes the group's approach visible to a broader audience through short essays, civic criticism and the circulation of ideas.

Dei delitti e delle pene is developed in this milieu, before and during the journal's beginnings. Beccaria's autograph manuscript, arranged in four parts, is reworked by Pietro Verri in a copy that redistributes its arguments into thirty-nine sections. In comparing the two manuscripts, Gianni Francioni counted fifty-four additions present in Verri’s copy but absent from the autograph; he expressly cautioned that some might derive from lost drafts by Beccaria. Their absence from the autograph therefore does not establish Verri’s authorship of every addition. For certain passages, including the end of the chapter on capital punishment and the prefatory A chi legge, the hypothesis of an original contribution by Pietro Verri is particularly strong, although it remains a philological judgement. The collaboration altered the book and its public form. It does not justify casually replacing the author's name.

In 1765 the monk Ferdinando Facchinei attacks the work as a danger to religion and sovereign authority. Pietro and Alessandro Verri write an anonymous response which, because of its particular form, will also be wrongly attributed to Beccaria. Defending the book becomes a struggle over editions, attributions, prudence and courage. A reforming argument depends on a network if it is to reach its readers and withstand the opposition of its age.

Fields of inquiry

Criminal law. Who makes the rule, who establishes the facts, on what evidence and with what penalty? Beccaria examines the structure of the right to punish.

Political philosophy. The social contract and the demand for security give rise to a common power. The freedom surrendered must be the minimum necessary. The sovereign is not entitled to do everything it can physically accomplish.

Moral philosophy and the psychology of the passions. Fear, habit, the hope of impunity and human sensibility help explain why the effectiveness of punishment does not rise with its cruelty.

Political economy. Crime and punishment unfold within a society of incentives, exchanges, interests and material circumstances. Administration must consider consequences that prohibition alone cannot govern.

Institutional history. To reform is to change rules, responsibilities, the visibility of procedure and practices of government. The passage from ideas into institutions is never automatic.

Rhetoric and the theory of style. The force of an argument depends on the relationship among ideas, sensations, clarity and attention. The prose of Dei delitti e delle pene itself moves between reasoning and indignation.

The anonymous book and its editions

The manuscript of Dei delitti e delle pene leaves for Livorno on 12 April 1764. It reaches the printing house of Marco Coltellini, directed by Giuseppe Aubert. The book is published without its author's name and circulates rapidly. Anonymity reflects the prudence of a circle challenging judicial customs still defended by powerful institutions. The history of reprinting also shows how difficult it was for an author to control the material dissemination of a work.

Its publication history is not a succession of equally authoritative authorial revisions. Beccaria's autograph, arranged in four substantial parts, is followed by Pietro Verri's editorial reworking into thirty-nine sections. This structure carries into the first printing of 1764. The so-called second edition, also dated 1764, is an unauthorised printing by Buonducci in Florence with a false place of publication, ‘Monaco’. It should not be treated as a new textual state authorised by Beccaria. The expanded third edition of 1765 and the further revised and enlarged fifth edition of 1766, by contrast, belong to the sequence prepared with his involvement. The fifth consists of forty-seven sections.

The imprint requires equal precision. The fifth edition is catalogued by the Biblioteca Braidense as ‘Harlem’ [Livorno], 1766. On the Illuminismo lombardo website, Gianni Francioni establishes the critical text of that same version. In a study of the printing history Francioni mentions a different fictitious imprint, ‘Lousanne’. This bibliographical discrepancy should not be resolved without comparing copies. It does not affect the identification of the critical text used in this guide.

Textual references in this guide. Roman numerals refer to the fifth Italian edition of 1766, in the critical text established by Francioni and made available through Illuminismo lombardo. André Morellet's French translation, issued in Paris towards the end of 1765 with a publication date of 1766, rearranges sections and arguments. Later Italian editions followed his different order. As a result, ‘chapter XXVIII’ and ‘chapter XVI’ can, in different editorial traditions, both refer to the discussion of capital punishment. A precise reference must specify the edition on which it relies.

Collating the original manuscripts and surviving eighteenth-century impressions remains a task for specialist research. The 1766 text used here has been checked against an identified digital critical edition, rather than inferred from the numbering of a transcription with an uncertain editorial history.

The right to punish: from contract to necessity

In chapter II, Diritto di punire (‘The right to punish’), Beccaria invokes Montesquieu and extends the principle beyond criminal law. An exercise of authority that exceeds necessity is tyrannical. This proposition compels us to ask how much of their freedom citizens have actually placed in common so that they may live more securely with one another.

His answer draws on the hypothesis of the social contract. Each person surrenders only the smallest portion of liberty needed to induce others to defend them. The right to punish arises from the combination of these small, necessary portions. Public power thus acquires its origin and its limit in the same act: it exists to preserve security, yet it cannot claim what there was no necessity to entrust to it.

The principle encounters a difficulty that outlives the treatise. Someone must recognise and delimit what is necessary. A measure declared indispensable by those who impose it may appear excessive to those who suffer it. During a crisis, the word security admits almost unlimited expansion. Beccaria therefore looks for standards that can withstand a sovereign's mere assertion: general and knowable laws, differentiated functions, proportionality to social harm, public punishment and a policy of prevention. None by itself settles the question of who is to judge what necessity requires.

Here justice assumes a civil and earthly meaning: the bond indispensable to preserving common interests. It is distinct from religious justice and its rewards or punishments in the life to come. Separating sin from crime allows civil coercion to be examined against a standard citizens can debate without first agreeing on a theology.

We should not read a fully developed liberal constitution back into this text. Beccaria uses the language of sovereign and subjects. Yet the sovereign himself is made to answer a test of legitimacy. Beccaria begins from something more demanding than a ruler's good intentions: punishment requires a warrant, and that warrant expires when necessity ends.

Written law and the judge

In chapter IV, Interpretazione delle leggi (‘Interpretation of the laws’), the problem moves from the origin of power to its daily application. A citizen may know the rule and still be exposed to the unpredictable interpretation of the person who judges. If guilt changes with a magistrate's temperament, connections or interests, the trial becomes another theatre of caprice.

Beccaria consequently asks the judge to reason in the form of a syllogism: general law, particular act, legal consequence. Making law belongs to the legislator; determining what happened and applying the rule belongs to the judge. His hostility to judicial interpretation must be understood against a penal system marked by uncertain sources, inherited authorities, inequalities of status and broad discretionary powers.

The syllogism promises to remove the accused person's fate from the temperament of another human being. Yet the proposed remedy has a limit of its own. A general rule cannot anticipate every circumstance, and deciding that an event falls under a rule already calls for interpretation. Modern legal orders demand reasons open to examination, an opportunity for the parties to be heard and respect for procedural safeguards. The eighteenth-century ideal of rigid deduction belongs to a different legal world. What remains valuable is the suspicion of a passage from law to individual case which no one can scrutinise.

Chapter V, Oscurità delle leggi (‘The obscurity of the laws’), completes the argument. An incomprehensible law cannot guide conduct and leaves legal professionals with a power that can shut citizens out. Making the law public and accessible is part of preventing arbitrary rule, even though clarity of language alone cannot guarantee fairness or sound judgement.

Evidence: before calling someone guilty

A crime almost always reaches the court as an incomplete account. One witness remembers a voice; another saw no more than a figure; one piece of circumstantial evidence seems to support both accounts. The act is in the past, while the decision still lies ahead. Between the two stands the problem of evidence. Whoever judges must reconstruct events, and may be mistaken.

In chapters XIII and XIV, on witnesses and circumstantial evidence, Beccaria confronts this distance between an event and its reconstruction. Testimony does not become true merely because it is given before a judge. Its credibility depends on the circumstances of the account and the relationships between the witness and the accused. Items of circumstantial evidence must be examined for their connections and their independence from one another. In contemporary terms, this is an inquiry into the conditions of evidential inference, not a formal method of proof suitable for mechanical application.

In chapter XV he attacks secret accusations, which can turn suspicion into a resource of government. In the chapters on judicial procedure, leading questions and trials, he distrusts methods capable of manufacturing a judicial truth instead of establishing what someone has done. Suspicion is one matter; sufficient grounds for punishment are another. The institutional passage from one to the other must remain open to scrutiny.

The Voto of 1792 brings an additional consideration alongside the treatise: the problem does not disappear when a commission is satisfied that it has followed proper procedures. Human evidence remains fallible. Where the punishment cannot be undone, the institution's margin of error ceases to be a mere technical detail. It becomes an argument against the punishment itself.

Torture: when pain takes the place of truth

Judicial torture does more than inflict suffering on someone already found guilty. Under the ancien régime it could be used while a case was still being formed, before the facts had been conclusively established, to secure a confession, resolve contradictions or identify accomplices. This is the mechanism Beccaria dismantles in chapter XVI.

If the crime has already been proved, a confession extracted by torture is unnecessary. If the facts remain uncertain, a citizen must not be subjected to punishment for something that has yet to be demonstrated. The question of the right to punish thus enters the time before judgement. Inflicting pain in advance means treating someone as guilty while the institution itself admits it does not yet know whether they are.

Beccaria turns to differences in physical endurance. A guilty person with a stronger body may remain silent under torment, while an innocent person less able to withstand it may say whatever the interrogators wish to hear. Torture introduces a variable unrelated to the fact it is meant to establish: how much pain a body can bear. A confession secured in this way obscures the distinction between the person who committed the offence and the person willing to claim it merely to end the suffering.

He also contests the notion that torment can cleanse a person of infamy, an idea that confuses a moral or social condition with a physical event. The judge comes to treat the body as a surface on which to read what the evidence has failed to disclose. Violence acquires an epistemic justification: it is said to produce knowledge at the very moment it distorts it.

Rational and moral objections meet at this point. A procedure that tortures a possibly innocent person exceeds the limits of legitimate power. One that treats pain as a guarantee of truth misunderstands how knowledge is obtained. For Beccaria, the promise of civil protection is broken in both instances.

Capital punishment and its exceptions

In chapter XXVIII of the fifth edition of 1766, Della pena di morte (‘On the death penalty’), Beccaria asks whether society ever received from a citizen the right to take that citizen's life. The agreement by which each person surrenders the smallest necessary part of freedom does not appear to include the surrender of life's greatest good. Capital punishment therefore resembles a war waged by the nation against one of its members when the state declares that person's elimination necessary.

A second argument concerns effectiveness. Execution is spectacular but brief; ordinary memory is more powerfully shaped by the duration and repetition of impressions. Against death Beccaria sets a long deprivation of liberty, which he considers a more effective deterrent and better suited to the preventive purpose of punishment. The argument also has an anthropological dimension: a community accustomed to spectacles of brutality may learn cruelty from the very authority that professes to combat it.

The chapter considers two circumstances in which death might seem necessary. The first concerns a man who, although deprived of liberty, retains connections and influence capable of overturning the nation's security. The setting is an extreme collapse of political order, extending to anarchy. The second is the supposition that, even under the ordinary rule of law, execution alone could prevent further crimes. Beccaria attempts to rebut the second circumstance by arguing that death has less preventive force than a continuing penalty. The first circumstance remains a matter of historical interpretation: does it describe a genuine penal exception, or circumstances in which civil society is no longer functioning normally? Neither reading permits us to turn Beccaria into an unqualified abolitionist in the modern sense.

In the Voto of 1792 the three signatories recognise a more precisely defined case: a person already imprisoned and under guard who, through connections inside or outside the prison, continues to plot the overthrow of the state and retains the power to bring it about. They explicitly distinguish this from killing insurgents who actively resist in open disturbances, which they describe as an effect of a situation akin to war, not capital punishment imposed by a court. The social-contract argument, central to the 1764 treatise, is not repeated here. The sovereign's right to prescribe death, if considered necessary, is not challenged in principle. What remains is the argument that death is ordinarily less necessary and less effective, now accompanied by the irreparability of a conviction based on fallible evidence. The continuity between the texts lies in their opposition to execution as a normal sanction; their difference reveals how vulnerable the principle of necessity remains to interpretation by those in power.

Another difficulty remains. Beccaria presents lifelong deprivation of liberty as a more effective alternative, at times drawing on images of forced labour and servitude that cannot be accepted without objection today. The intellectual force of his criticism of capital punishment must be distinguished from the full defensibility of the punishments he imagines in its place.

The time of punishment: proportionality, certainty and promptness

Chapter VI develops the relationship between crimes and punishments; the following chapter VII, Errori nella misura delle pene (‘Errors in the measure of punishments’), identifies harm to the nation as the standard by which offences should be assessed. A system of penalties requires distinctions that make the distance between acts of different gravity intelligible. Beccaria uses the image of a scale and calls for gradation, but he supplies no arithmetical schedule assigning crimes a fixed number of years in prison. His is a demand for rational order addressed to legislators, leaving open the disputed question of how social harm is to be judged.

Proportionality prevents radically different acts from being met with the same penalty. If the difference between a lesser wrong and a greater one is erased, a person loses a reason to stop short of the more serious act. Moderation is therefore more than an act of mercy towards the offender. It seeks to shape incentives and prevent further harm.

In chapter XXVII Beccaria sets the certainty of punishment against its cruelty. A moderate penalty applied predictably can have greater effect than a savage torment accompanied by the hope of escaping it. Certainty does not mean convicting everyone who is accused. It presupposes a sound determination of the facts; without that condition, it would amount to the certainty of abuse.

Promptness, in chapter XIX, concerns the closeness in time between an act and its consequence. It matters both because an accused person should not be left to suffer needlessly and because the relation between conduct and response should remain intelligible. To confuse promptness with a rushed trial would be to squander the quality of evidence on which legitimate punishment depends. Time is a resource of the legal order: it is needed for careful judgement, but unjustified delay can itself become an undeclared punishment.

In chapter XLVII, the conclusion gathers six requirements. Punishment should be public, prompt, necessary, the least possible under the circumstances, proportionate to the offence and prescribed by law. The formula concentrates a civic understanding of penal power. No one requirement is sufficient on its own; together they make it harder to substitute the anger of the moment for a public rule.

Prevention: legislation's harder task

Towards the end of the treatise, the direction of the inquiry changes. In chapter XLI, Come si prevengano i delitti (‘How crimes are prevented’), Beccaria turns from the art of punishing after harm has been done to the conditions that might make harm less frequent in the first place. Intelligible laws, trustworthy institutions, knowledge, education and fewer opportunities for corruption become part of a wider conception of civil life.

The following chapters discuss the sciences, magistrates, rewards, education and pardons. Prevention is not synonymous with unlimited surveillance. It includes the quality of law and the incentives through which people learn to live alongside one another. Yet the relationship between liberty and prevention remains delicate. A legal order might claim to eliminate every risk by multiplying forms of observation. Necessity, transparency and the limits imposed on the means used are what distinguish a defensible policy from that ambition.

The strength of Beccaria's proposal lies in his reminder that a justice system evaluated solely by the number or severity of its convictions always arrives too late. A state that claims to provide security must also be able to explain how it makes crime less likely without turning ordinary life into a perpetual anticipation of punishment.

Cerchi framework — The path by which punishment is authorised

Punishment appears at the end of an institutional path that begins long before conviction. To read Beccaria, it is useful to trace the transitions that make that outcome possible, asking at each point where power may lose its justification. This is an interpretive framework developed by Cerchi d’inchiostro. Beccaria did not draw a diagram of this sequence in his treatise.

This path is not a model of present-day criminal procedure. It lacks legal elements developed in different ways by modern constitutions. Its purpose is to locate Beccaria's questions. A conviction may comply with a written law and remain unjust because the evidence is false. Evidence may be convincing and lead to a grossly disproportionate penalty. A formally regular punishment may defeat its own purpose by making society more brutal.

The most revealing point comes when the path turns back on itself. An irreparable penalty would demand a certainty that human evidence cannot absolutely promise. The Voto of 1792 adds no merely technical box to the sequence. Alongside necessity and effectiveness, it introduces the argument of irreparability. The capacity to correct a mistake becomes one measure of a civilised legal order.

Cerchi framework — Two ways of losing the measure

The discussions of torture and capital punishment may look alike because both concern suffering. Their underlying structures of judgement are different.

Before the sentence: force claims the power to know. In judicial torture, authority uses pain to obtain a confession that is then supposed to determine whether punishment should follow. The order is inverted: suffering comes before the finding of fact and attempts to replace it. Someone physically weaker may falsely confess; someone stronger may withstand the pain. Violence damages the evidence just when it claims to be searching for it.

After the sentence: force claims finality. In capital punishment, a trial may have reached a conclusion the legal order regards as sufficient. The possibility of error nevertheless remains. A decision to kill removes the time in which justice might discover and remedy that mistake. In 1792 Beccaria and his colleagues insist on the irreparability of a decision resting on fallible knowledge.

The two cases reveal an asymmetry. Before judgement, pain is called upon to manufacture missing evidence. After judgement, death destroys the possibility of repairing an error in evidence already accepted. The first concerns the means by which authority claims knowledge; the second, the price others pay when that authority may have been mistaken.

Imprisonment, too, can be unjust and can sometimes leave irreparable consequences. The comparison between torture and death does not absolve it. It identifies two points at which imperfect knowledge, joined to coercive power, demands an especially strict limit.

From theory to administration

A book can be translated quickly. Administrative practice changes at another pace. Beccaria begins teaching political economy in 1769 and enters the councils of Austrian Lombardy in 1771. He works on food provisioning, trade, money, crafts and manufacturing, regulations and matters of government. This part of his life is sometimes treated as a prolonged, quiet epilogue to early fame. The administrative records tell a different story: years of sustained judgement on concrete interests.

Economic policy provides a way into his penal thought. Bad rules produce undesirable behaviour; the answer cannot always be to add another prohibition. A rule that interferes unnecessarily with exchange and activity may create opportunities for transgression instead of reducing them. Government must then decide when to intervene and when to leave room for citizens to act.

In 1786 the penal reform sponsored by the Grand Duke of Tuscany, Pietro Leopoldo (Peter Leopold), abolishes both torture and capital punishment. It is not simply a decree by Beccaria implemented in another state. It emerges from an independent political and administrative process involving advisers, sovereign choices and practices that had begun to change before the legislation itself. The treatise was one recognisable intellectual antecedent. The actual decision belonged to the Grand Duchy and its government.

The Lombard commission of the 1790s tests the passage from theory into legislation once more. The minority opinion Beccaria signs in 1792 argues for abolition in almost all cases but preserves an exception for a prisoner still able to endanger the security of the state. In the same document, the sovereign legislator retains ultimate authority over the choice of capital punishment. An argument can change considerably when it moves from philosophical criticism to a proposed code. Necessity retains its name, but the institution empowered to judge it has changed.

Ricerche sullo stile and Elementi di economia pubblica

The Ricerche intorno alla natura dello stile, published in 1770, may seem remote from the reform of justice. In it Beccaria studies how ideas call up other ideas, awaken sensations, and hold or scatter attention. His interest in communication goes beyond the beauty of expression. Language can sharpen knowledge or obscure it beneath verbal associations and habits. For an author who had fought the obscurity of law, the continuity of method is significant, although his aesthetics cannot be reduced to an appendix of his penal arguments.

For Beccaria, style is no ornament added to a demonstration already complete. Words help establish the order of ideas and the power those ideas exert upon human beings. The treatise of 1764 contains passages in which a legal question becomes almost intolerable to the civic conscience, alongside pages built through careful distinctions and consequences. The theory of 1770 invites us to read the treatise's form as one source of its effectiveness.

The Elementi di economia pubblica, published posthumously in 1804 from lectures at the Scuole palatine, broaden the field. Beccaria examines agriculture, manufacturing, trade, the division of activities, and private and public interest. Economic science should observe regularities in human conduct, free itself from pointless prohibitions and select interventions proportionate to their effects. Necessity reappears, though it would be wrong to overlay a finished economic system onto the earlier penal insights.

The Tentativo analitico sui contrabbandi, published in Il Caffè in 1764, gives this problem a concrete form. Given a commodity, a duty and the risk of discovery and confiscation, what advantage remains to a trader who evades the tax? Beccaria uses calculation to examine the incentive created by a fiscal rule. The example shows how a rule may encourage the very conduct it intends to deter. It also shows why his equations should not be imported into a calculation of the proportionality between human suffering and crime.

Key ideas

Security comes at a cost in liberty, and that cost must remain justifiable. The contract of chapter II gives rise to the right to punish because people wish to escape insecurity. Every extension of coercion renews the question of how much freedom they are being asked to give up.

Law must speak before the judge. A general, intelligible and public rule reduces the risk that a magistrate will substitute personal opinion for the law. Beccaria's answer belongs to his century; the problem of arbitrary interpretation does not.

A trial can manufacture what it claims to discover. Torture damages the quality of judicial knowledge; secret accusations remove the alleged facts from public challenge. Violence can begin before sentencing.

The effect of punishment depends in part on how it is perceived. Promptness, predictability, duration and moderation work together. An atrocious threat that seems remote may deter less effectively than a limited consequence that is likely to follow.

Severity is not the only language of protection. If the law creates opportunities for offending, the legal order must examine and correct the law itself. Prevention makes it necessary to look beyond the courtroom.

Reform does not retain an unchanged vocabulary when it enters institutions. Between 1764 and 1792 Beccaria continues to oppose execution as an ordinary penalty, but does not repeat the contractual objection in the Voto and leaves an exceptional decision to the sovereign. Institutional practice changes the terms of the problem.

Judicial fallibility has unequal consequences. The argument of 1792 shows why imperfect evidence matters especially when punishment removes every chance of an effective review.

Key concepts

Civil necessity

Under the social contract described in chapter II, necessity both founds the right to punish and confines its extent. It is a demanding principle, but its force depends on the contested question of who may define what is indispensable.

The right to punish

A power derived from the minimum portions of liberty each person places in common to obtain security. The central issue is the legitimacy of that authority, prior to the choice of any specific penalty.

Social harm

In chapter VII, Errori nella misura delle pene, harm to society provides a criterion for distinguishing offences by their effects upon life together. It shifts the measure away from religious offence and personal rank towards the civil consequences of an act.

Proportionality

A reasoned relationship between the gravity of different acts and the penalties attached to them, needed to prevent the legal order from treating unequal harms as though they were equal. Beccaria supplies no arithmetic formula for assigning a penalty to each crime.

Certainty and promptness

The former concerns the predictable application of the law once the facts have been established; the latter, the closeness in time between an offence and its consequence. For Beccaria both serve prevention. Neither authorises automatic convictions or trials unable to scrutinise the evidence.

The moderation of punishments

Beccaria's historical expression dolcezza delle pene conveys the idea that effective severity requires no gratuitous torment. This criticism of cruelty must be distinguished from later theories of prisoners' rights, developed on additional grounds.

Publicity of the law

A rule should be knowable by those to whom it applies. Obscurity and arbitrary interpretation weaken the security the state promises to provide.

Prevention

The creation of legislative, educational and social conditions that make crimes less likely. It transfers part of the responsibility for justice from the execution of punishment to the quality of government.

Arbitrary power

Coercion that exceeds stated rules and purposes, or invokes a necessity no one is required to demonstrate. The problem runs through legislation, evidence, punishment and administration.

Public utility

A standard for judging the effects of institutions on life together and the common good. It can restrain cruelty, yet leaves open which individual interests must never be sacrificed in the name of collective benefit.

Constellation

Before: three accounts of political authority

Montesquieu gives Beccaria a decisive intellectual resource, including the principle invoked in chapter II that ties the exercise of authority to necessity. For both writers, penal law must be assessed in relation to political order and civil liberty, though their positions on capital punishment are not identical. Rousseau offers a particularly sharp contrast. In The Social Contract, the political bond may lead to the loss of the life of someone who radically breaks that bond; Beccaria asks how the surrender of a minimum of liberty could possibly include the right to kill. The comparison rests on their works and must not turn either position into a caricature.

Hobbes belongs to this project's constellation through a different tension. In Leviathan, the fear of violence prompts the establishment of a common authority capable of judging and punishing. Beccaria shares the seriousness of the problem of security but directs attention to how far penal force has been authorised to extend. The relationship concerns shared questions, not a documented line of textual influence.

Alongside: a book written in the midst of controversy

Pietro Verri reshapes Beccaria's manuscript, taking it from four autograph parts into a text organised in sections, and helps defend the work. Alessandro Verri brings knowledge of trials and prisoners to the discussion. Without them, we lose the material circumstances in which the book was made; without Beccaria, we lose the voice that gave it its extraordinary European force.

Ferdinando Facchinei attacks the book in 1765, prompting a response from the Verri brothers. Voltaire supports its circulation and joins the debate on punishment. André Morellet, in rearranging the French translation, places before readers a different order of reasons. Reception thus reveals something essential: the form that carries an idea into the world can also change the way it is read.

After: from the printed page to penal institutions

The Leopoldina of 1786, introduced by Pietro Leopoldo, abolishes torture and the death penalty in Tuscany. This was no simple application of a book. A sovereign decision brought a reforming process to a legislative conclusion, choosing a more unequivocally abolitionist solution than the Milanese Voto of 1792.

Alessandro Manzoni, in Storia della colonna infame, examines the responsibility of judges when a city believes itself threatened. His attention falls on how evidence is made and on the possibility of resisting collective convictions. The kinship with Beccaria is a biographical fact; the intellectual comparison rests on the works themselves and the particular problem of torture.

Bentham rethinks punishment in terms of utility and institutional organisation. His Panopticon also leads towards questions that Foucault will examine under the heading of discipline. Foucault's work asks what happens after the sentence, when deprivation of liberty becomes classification, exercise and continuous observation. These later effects are not projected backwards into Beccaria's intentions.

In the present: what still needs to be examined

The constellation opens two separate inquiries. Faced with a sanction, we may ask whether the law and evidence justify the penalty. Entering the institution that carries it out, we must also observe the powers that organise daily life there. A civilised penal order depends on both forms of scrutiny, which do not always lead to the same answer.

Beccaria, Sophocles and Foucault

Triad 14 proposes a sequence of reading, Sophocles → Foucault → Beccaria, not a historical genealogy. The tragedian belongs to classical Athens; Beccaria writes in the eighteenth century; Foucault investigates modernity from the twentieth. Each brings to light a difficulty the others do not resolve.

In Antigone, Creon's decree forbids the burial of Polyneices; Antigone performs the rite and admits that she has disobeyed. The act, from the ruler's point of view, is established. What remains disputed is the authority of the command that would punish it. In Oedipus the King, the search for a culprit leads the ruler to discover what he had not known about his own actions. The plays expose the dependence of judgement on law, knowledge and the limits of understanding, without being treated as anticipations of the criminal code.

In the final part of Discipline and Punish, Foucault pauses at the penal colony of Mettray, inaugurated on 22 January 1840. The boys are placed in groups called families; their daily lives combine labour, schooling, supervision, roll calls and inspections. The institution aims to correct conduct and produces observations about each young person, day after day. This is a precise example of the difference between a sentence established in law and the means by which an organisation forms, classifies and governs those entrusted to it.

A Beccarian inquiry might ask about the legal basis of detention, the evidence of the offence and the proportionality of the sentence. Foucault asks what happens inside that regime: who observes the boys, which gestures are judged proper, how behaviour becomes a personal characteristic, and which disciplinary practices continue after judgement. Mettray provides a meeting point between distinct forms of analysis. It does not imply that Beccaria's legislative proposals historically caused the colony to exist.

Together the three works resist the confusion of different questions. A command may be publicly issued and remain tragically contestable. A punishment authorised by law may coexist with practices of normalisation that the sentence never describes. A criticism of discipline may make power visible without determining which sentence is legally legitimate. Beccaria closes the sequence by demanding that authority account for the evidence it accepts and the punishment it imposes. His reasoning is essential to this comparison, but cannot settle every question the three authors raise.

Legacy and reform

Beccaria's work enters European debate with extraordinary speed. Reprints, fresh editions, translations, praise from the philosophes and hostile responses make it a point of reference. The French version, reorganised by Morellet, encourages its reception while changing the way the argument is encountered. To speak of influence without acknowledging these textual mediations risks attributing the wording and arrangement of one edition to another.

The Tuscan reform of 30 November 1786, known as the Leopoldina, marks a decisive moment. It abolishes capital punishment and torture, restricts arbitrary practices and alters aspects of criminal procedure. Abolition resulted from political determination and a process of reform to which Beccaria's ideas contributed, without being its sole cause. It did not follow automatically from reading the treatise, nor did it ensure that change everywhere would be irreversible.

Over the longer term, Beccaria's name accompanies the development of liberal criminal law, abolitionist movements and reflection on procedural safeguards. That legacy must be distinguished from particular legal systems, which change through subsequent conflicts, legislation and interpretation. Modern criminal law is not wholly Beccarian, and not every use of the words security or proportionality preserves the substance of his thought.

Its most durable inheritance may be a discipline of questioning. Faced with a penalty, we must ask more than whether it is prescribed. From what authority does it derive? What evidence supports it? What necessity requires this particular degree of suffering? Later history has enriched that inquiry with rights and procedures that an eighteenth-century treatise could not develop in full.

The contemporary wound — When security demands a victim

In 2025, Amnesty International recorded at least 2,707 executions worldwide, in 17 countries. This is a documented minimum. Reliable information is unavailable for some states, and the total does not include all the executions Amnesty believes took place where the authorities keep such information secret. In the same year, at least 2,334 new death sentences were recorded in 48 countries. The report, published on 18 May 2026, distinguishes the long-term reduction in the number of executing states from the increase in known executions during the latest year surveyed.

The same report records at least 1,257 executions for drug-related offences in five countries, a partial figure that does not include every execution concealed from observation. It also records one exoneration of a person previously sentenced to death in the United States. The first figure invites questions of necessity and proportionality; the latter returns us to the possibility of correcting a judicial finding. These are contemporary records with their own definitions and limitations. They cannot serve as experimental proof of Beccaria's claims. They do, however, give his questions a concrete weight. What kind of harm could justify the most extreme punishment? What does irreparability mean when a review may come too late?

The problem crosses different political systems. The regimes that use the death penalty differ, and Beccaria could not have known the conventions of our own age. We can follow his objection without treating these regimes as identical: the authority that punishes must be able to demonstrate that the suffering it imposes is necessary for a civil purpose, and that no less destructive measure would achieve that purpose. Evidence and the possibility of correcting an error become decisive here.

The same grammar, with important differences, returns when public opinion demands harsher punishment after a violent event. Outrage can be understandable and can express a legitimate desire for protection. Yet law cannot be a direct transcription of outrage. Protecting people, establishing what happened, determining the necessary penalty and staging a punishment that promises reassurance are separate tasks.

This is Beccaria's contemporary wound. The greater the fear, the more urgently we need to know who judges a measure necessary, on what information, and with what possibility of correction. A punishment may be authorised by law and still lose its proper measure when the authorities consider themselves exempt from having to justify it.

Limits and contradictions

Necessity is an unstable boundary. Beccaria derives the right to punish from what is indispensable, but he has no method capable of preventing every ruler from enlarging the indispensable minimum in the name of collective security. This tension lies within his reasoning and is more revealing than a retrospective accusation of inconsistency.

Utility may come into conflict with limits. Beccaria evaluates punishment partly by what it does for collective security. A ruler might nevertheless argue that terror inflicted on a few benefits the many. The theory offers arguments against cruelty and needless suffering, but the criterion of utility is not identical with later formulations of inviolable individual rights. Dialogue with modern constitutionalism must preserve that critical distance.

A syllogism cannot abolish interpretation. Beccaria's wish to restrain judges is a reaction to the procedures of the ancien régime. Within a contemporary legal order, applying a rule involves interpretation, the giving of reasons and engagement with complex rights and facts. His warning can be retained without imagining the judge as a machine for deduction.

Opposition to execution coexists with an exceptionally harsh alternative. The perpetual servitude Beccaria regards as more effective than death subjects the convicted person to an extreme loss of liberty. It would be mistaken to turn this proposal into a modern doctrine of rehabilitation or a complete affirmation of dignity in imprisonment.

The political exception survives. Chapter XXVIII of the treatise distinguishes the crisis of civil order from the ordinary operation of the laws. In the Voto, a prisoner still capable of overthrowing the state becomes the exceptional case put before the sovereign. The survival of this exception marks a limit both of the theory and of its administrative translation. Whoever decides that it is necessary retains a power particularly difficult to contain.

The reformer is also a public official. The Lombard Enlightenment works alongside institutions pursuing efficiency, prosperity and greater administrative capacity. Humanitarian reform may coexist with a stronger grip of the state upon social life. Foucault offers a different historical perspective from which to examine this possibility.

Illuminating details

The book first appears without an author's name. The anonymity of 1764 and the fictitious imprints of subsequent printings remind us that civic argument did not circulate under neutral conditions. The physical form of a book was part of its exposure to power.

The Verri brothers shaped the making of the book. Alessandro works close to prisoners and criminal proceedings; Pietro encourages and revises the writing. Their collaboration opens a route into the material history of ideas, with its revisions, exchanges and disputes over attribution.

The French translator changes the reading experience. Morellet does more than move words between languages. His rearrangement contributes to the book's success and alters the sequence of reasons the reader encounters.

Beccaria was Alessandro Manzoni's maternal grandfather. His daughter Giulia was the novelist's mother. The family relationship invites a comparison between works about justice and torture; it does not by itself prove intellectual influence.

Much of the important work comes after fame. The many years he spent in Lombard administration left memoranda and official papers. Less familiar than the treatise, they are indispensable to understanding the relationship between ideas, competence and compromise.

Common misreadings

‘Beccaria invented abolitionism.’ Capital punishment had been criticised before him. His achievement was to make its criticism central to European reform, with far-reaching public effect.

‘He opposed the death penalty in every possible circumstance.’ The treatise considers exceptional situations and the opinion of 1792 preserves one. The significance of those exceptions is disputed and must not be erased.

‘Certainty of punishment means convicting everyone.’ Certainty presupposes that facts have been properly established. Otherwise the result is merely predictable arbitrary punishment.

‘Beccaria supplied a formula for calculating proportional penalties.’ He argues through differences in gravity and preventive usefulness, without producing a numerical scale that can be applied automatically.

‘A written law is enough to ensure justice.’ The book is equally concerned with evidence, judges, the measure of punishment and the limits of authority. The existence of a written statute cannot settle every question.

‘The Verri brothers wrote the book instead of him.’ Pietro Verri's copy profoundly altered its organisation and, in some places, its wording. This is a documented editorial process, not an anecdote. Beccaria's authorship is recognised, while attribution of particular additions remains partly contested.

‘Foucault disproves Beccaria.’ Foucault examines penal practices, forms of knowledge and institutions genealogically; Beccaria develops standards of penal legitimacy. Their relationship is a productive tension, not a historical debate in which one replies to the other.

Glossary

Secret accusation. A denunciation or charge withheld from effective scrutiny by the accused; for Beccaria it fosters suspicion and weakens the possibility of defence.

Moral certainty. The degree of conviction that, in the evidentiary language of the period, could support a verdict without logically excluding every contrary possibility. The Voto of 1792 invokes its limitations when considering irreversible punishment.

Social contract. A philosophical hypothesis about the origin of common political authority. In the 1764 treatise Beccaria grounds the right to punish upon it; the Voto of 1792 does not repeat that argument.

Public happiness. An eighteenth-century expression encompassing prosperity and security in common life, central to Lombard reform. It is not a modern economic index.

Criminal justice under the ancien régime. The diverse legal sources, courts and procedures preceding modern codifications. Torture took different institutional forms and was subject to different limits within that varied legal world.

Infallibility of punishment. In eighteenth-century deterrence language, the expression refers to the predictability, rather than the literal infallibility, of a sanction. It must be distinguished from infallibility in establishing the facts, which Beccaria and his colleagues reject in the Voto.

Leopoldina. The Tuscan penal reform promulgated by Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo (Peter Leopold) on 30 November 1786, abolishing torture and capital punishment.

Perpetual punishment. A deprivation of liberty theoretically without end, often associated in Beccaria's historical account with forced labour and servitude. It differs from present-day legal forms of life imprisonment.

Judicial evidence. Information and reasoning used to establish a fact relevant to a court’s judgment. In the treatise, the credibility of witnesses and indications must be distinguished from the intensity of a confession extracted under torment.

Reading pathways

If you have fifteen minutes

Read chapters II (Diritto di punire), XVI (Della tortura), XXVIII (Della pena di morte) and XLVII (Conclusione) of Dei delitti e delle pene. These four passages reveal the origin of penal authority, the failure of violent methods of proof, the criticism of execution and the conditions of legitimate punishment. The chapter numbers refer to the Italian version divided into forty-seven sections.

If you have an hour

Add IV–V on law and interpretation, VI–VII on proportionality, XIII–XV on witnesses and indications, XIX on promptness, XXVII on moderation and XLI–XLV on prevention. Then try to distinguish, on a single page, problems of the rule from problems of evidence and sanction.

If you have a week

Read Dei delitti e delle pene in full in a critical or reliably annotated edition. Follow its revisions and Morellet's translation. Set beside it the Voto of 1792 on capital punishment, selected pages of Ricerche intorno alla natura dello stile, the introductory lecture on economics and extracts from Elementi di economia pubblica. Complete the reading with a history of the Lombard Enlightenment and the Tuscan reform of 1786.

Within Cerchi d’inchiostro

Read Beccaria alongside Hobbes for security and political authority, and Manzoni for evidence, error and the responsibility of judges. Sophocles and Foucault form the other two perspectives of Triad 14, Law, Guilt, Punishment.

Questions to think with

Where does the state's power come from? If the answer is a contract necessary for security, which powers cannot be derived from that contract?

What may judges do when the law is silent or obscure? How does Beccaria's distrust of interpretation change when placed alongside modern procedural safeguards?

What makes an accusation convincing? Which pieces of evidence are independent, and which might have been produced by fear or pressure from authority?

How much suffering is necessary? Who must demonstrate it, against what purposes and in comparison with which alternatives?

What can be corrected? Why does the reversibility of a decision matter when those deciding know they can make mistakes?

When does reform become an institution? What part of its promise survives, and what may change as principles enter practice?

Points to remember

  1. Necessity alone leaves the central political question unanswered: who decides when coercion is truly indispensable?
  2. The penal path has distinct points of failure. An obscure law, false evidence and an excessive punishment can each produce injustice independently of the others.
  3. Torture and capital punishment endanger judicial knowledge in opposite ways. Torture intervenes before the facts are established; execution makes the consequences of a fallible finding final.
  4. The form of the book matters. From autograph to Verri's copy, through authorial editions and Morellet's translation, the arrangement and, at points, the substance of the argument change.
  5. Between 1764 and 1792, Beccaria's opposition to execution as an ordinary penalty survives, while the argument about legitimacy changes and a political exception remains.
  6. Dialogue with Foucault reminds us that punishment continues in the daily life of institutions, beyond what any sentence can fully describe.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Cesare Beccaria?

A Milanese jurist, philosopher and economist (1738–1794), author of Dei delitti e delle pene (1764) and a leading figure in the Lombard Enlightenment. He also taught economics and worked as an official in Austrian Lombardy.

What does Dei delitti e delle pene argue?

That punishment is legitimate only within the limits of civil necessity. It must rest on knowable law, be proportionate to the offence, follow a sound determination of the facts and aim to prevent further harm without needless suffering.

Why did Beccaria oppose torture?

Because it inflicts suffering before guilt has been conclusively established and turns physical endurance into a false test of truth. An innocent person unable to bear pain may confess, while a stronger guilty person may resist.

Did Beccaria call for the abolition of capital punishment?

In nearly all ordinary cases, yes. He challenged its legitimacy and usefulness under the ordinary rule of law. The treatise discusses exceptional conditions; in the Voto of 1792, written with two other commissioners, he retains only the case of a prisoner who, despite detention, remains capable of endangering state security.

What did he mean by the right to punish?

The power arising from the smallest part of their liberty that citizens surrender to secure life together. Coercion beyond what is necessary for that purpose becomes an abuse.

Does certainty of punishment mean harsher penalties?

No. A moderate sanction prescribed by law and predictably applied after the facts have been properly established may deter more effectively than the threat of an extreme punishment that offenders expect to escape.

Did Beccaria allow judges to interpret the law?

He sought to restrict their discretion radically, fearing that variable interpretations would turn judgement into arbitrary rule. His solution belongs to eighteenth-century law and differs from the reasoned interpretive work of judges within modern legal systems.

How does Beccaria distinguish punishment from revenge?

Public punishment should depend on law, necessity and proportionality and seek to prevent further harm. Revenge takes its measure instead from an injury already suffered and the passion of the person who wishes to return the pain.

What was Beccaria's relationship with Pietro and Alessandro Verri?

Pietro encouraged, discussed and revised the work that became the treatise. Alessandro contributed knowledge of judicial practice. Their collaboration is documented, although the exact boundaries of individual authorship remain a subject of philological research.

Why is the Leopoldina of 1786 important?

It was the Tuscan criminal-law reform introduced by Pietro Leopoldo that abolished torture and capital punishment. Beccaria's ideas were an important antecedent, but they do not exhaust the political and administrative causes of the reform.

Why read Beccaria's economic writings as well?

Because they show how his method attends to the effects of rules, incentives, material conditions and the necessity of public intervention. Dei delitti e delle pene belongs to this broader culture of reform.

What connects Beccaria with Alessandro Manzoni?

Beccaria was Manzoni's maternal grandfather. At the level of their works, Storia della colonna infame invites a substantial comparison concerning torture, evidence, collective fear and the responsibility of judges. Their family relationship alone proves no direct transmission of ideas.

Do Beccaria and Foucault contradict each other?

They investigate different problems. Beccaria seeks the legal conditions that justify and limit punishment; Foucault reconstructs the disciplinary mechanisms that can also operate inside formally reformed institutions. Their comparison reveals a tension, not a direct genealogy.

Why does Beccaria still matter?

Because he requires those who punish to justify the origin, evidence, measure and purpose of coercion, especially when public fear makes it easier to demand severity than to demonstrate necessity.

Closing reflection — The time to put things right

The Voto of 1792 does not give us the image of an ageing philosopher faithfully repeating his younger self. The man seated on the commission is a public official, asked to propose a rule to a sovereign who will have the final word. His reservation for the prisoner who can still threaten to overthrow the state exposes the difficulty running through this entire guide. Who decides when defending political order is truly necessary, and who may challenge that judgement?

Yet acknowledging the tension does nothing to weaken the argument from fallibility. A deposition may be mistaken. Two items of circumstantial evidence may depend on the same source. A confession may be obtained under pressure. The judge must decide, but cannot turn judgement into absolute knowledge merely by pronouncing a sentence.

In the treatise of 1764, torment could falsify the evidence before conviction. In the document of 1792, death denies justice the time it might need to recognise and remedy an error. Between those moments, Beccaria spent many years writing and debating the instruments of government. The language of the social contract recedes in the Voto, while human fallibility gains a new force. His reasoning remains accompanied by a political reservation that marks its own limit.

That is the question we must continue to put to any authority invoking security: what law permits it to act, what facts has it truly established, what punishment is necessary, and what will still be possible to correct once that punishment has been carried out?
