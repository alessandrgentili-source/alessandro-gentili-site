The Separation of Cognitive Powers

Judgment, authority and responsibility in the age of generative AI

Alessandro Gentili

An answer can acquire authority before anyone has consciously granted it. It arrives in a form we have learned to trust: fluent language, an orderly explanation, perhaps a table of figures with references to documents. It passes from a screen into a meeting, from a meeting into a report, and eventually into an instruction. No one need declare that a machine has acquired the right to decide. Yet by the time that instruction reaches the person affected by it, the original proposal may be difficult to question.

There is something familiar about this passage. Organizations have always converted judgments into procedures and procedures into actions; they could scarcely function if every choice had to begin from first principles. What has changed is the ease with which an artificial system can supply the language, reasoning and apparent completeness that ordinarily accompany a decision before the people responsible have determined what should count as a sufficient reason.

We tend to look for danger in an incorrect answer. Generative systems may invent facts, misconstrue a source or construct an argument that does not survive scrutiny. Such failures matter, sometimes gravely. Yet a system may also execute its assigned task accurately and contribute to an unjustified decision. Its figures may be sound, its reasoning internally consistent and its recommendation faithful to the criteria supplied. The question then returns to the assignment itself: who defined the task, and why did those criteria deserve authority?

Consider a company distributing customer-service requests among a limited number of employees. Its management asks an AI system to recommend an order of priority. The system ranks requests by estimated time to resolution, with the aim of reducing the average time a request remains in the queue. The system also drafts a concise explanation of its ranking, presenting the shorter estimated resolutions as an efficient and consistent response to the company’s needs. A review confirms that the records are genuine, the calculations sound and the ranking consistent with its instructions. The explanation is fluent, and it makes the chosen objective appear almost self-evident. Over time, however, requests that require patience, unusual expertise or extended contact can be pushed to the margins. Some of the customers with the most urgent or complex problems may become the least convenient to serve.

The system need not malfunction for this to happen. The difficulty lies in the distance between a measurable improvement and the purpose for which an organization exists. Reducing the average time in a queue may be useful; it is an incomplete account of good service. Verifying a recommendation cannot, by itself, establish whether the objective that produced it deserves to govern the work.

This is where the question of intelligence crosses into the question of power.

The authority hidden in a procedure

The problem of power is older than its modern division into branches of government. Aristotle, in the Politics, distinguished deliberation, public offices and courts as elements of constitutional life, while asking what purposes a political order ought to serve. Polybius examined how consuls, Senate and people could restrain one another within the Roman Republic; Cicero, in De re publica, returned to the mixed constitution and the stability of a commonwealth. In the seventeenth century, John Locke distinguished legislative, executive and federative powers, the last concerned with relations beyond the commonwealth. Montesquieu later gave the division of legislative, executive and judicial powers its familiar modern articulation. These thinkers did not advance an identical theory, nor can the institutions they described be transferred intact into contemporary workplaces. Their different inquiries nevertheless expose a persistent difficulty: power is not adequately limited merely because its work has been described or assigned a name. It must encounter limits it cannot cancel at will.

That insight has already entered debates about artificial intelligence. In his account of Trias Algorithmica, Hamilton Mann distinguishes rulemaking, execution and judgment in algorithmic governance. His argument and the present inquiry converge on more than an analogy with constitutional powers. Mann distinguishes the trustworthiness of an output from the legitimacy of the authority exercised through it; he also insists that choosing a system’s objectives and exercising meaningful human review cannot be reduced to technical operations or a signature without power. This essay does not claim priority over those distinctions. Its attention falls closer to the movement of ordinary cognitive work: how a fluent answer, accompanied by an apparently complete explanation, can acquire the force of a decision before the assumptions that framed it have been properly questioned.

I use separation of cognitive powers to name three functions within that passage: generating, verifying and deciding. This is an organizational and interpretive distinction, not a new constitutional doctrine. To generate is to produce a possible answer, interpretation or course of action. To verify is to examine its claims and empirical assumptions against evidence or standards that do not depend merely on its persuasiveness. To decide is to exercise authority over what may be done, with an obligation to explain, revise or remedy its consequences.

These are distinctions of function, not necessarily distinctions between three people or three machines. The same professional may perform more than one of them, and automated procedures can support each stage. Where separate reviewers are impracticable, recorded reasons, a different checking method or access to outside challenge can provide some resistance to an unchecked conclusion, though none guarantees independence by itself. Separation becomes meaningful when the checks can expose a failure, when an objection can reach someone empowered to act on it, and when the rule itself remains open to challenge.

Decision enters the process twice. Someone first establishes the objective and the criteria against which a result will be judged; someone later authorizes action under those criteria. Those responsibilities may belong to different people, and a person approving an individual case may have no authority to rewrite the governing rule. The familiar sequence generate, verify, decide is useful only if it does not conceal the earlier decision that frames everything the system is asked to do.

The distinction matters because each function rests on a different kind of justification. Deciding what counts as sufficient evidence belongs to verification, but even a satisfactory evidential test cannot establish the legitimacy of the objective being served. A proposal may be persuasive without being accurate; an accurate account of the facts may still support a poor course of action; and an authorized decision can rest on criteria that deserve to be challenged. Where these differences disappear, a seamless process may conceal a series of judgments for which no one has been asked to account.

Increasingly capable machines can also shift the burden of proof. A recommendation once had to earn its way into a decision; a polished generated answer may become the default position, leaving anyone who objects to justify the interruption. This reversal requires no deliberate surrender of authority. Convenience, pace and the appearance of completeness may be enough.

A human signature does not automatically reverse this shift. If the person expected to approve an output lacks time to examine it, access to the evidence, competence to understand its limits or authority to reject it, approval records a passage through the organization rather than an exercise of judgment. The signature is visible, while the power that would make it meaningful remains uncertain. Madeleine Clare Elish has described a related displacement of responsibility through the idea of a moral crumple zone: a person with limited control may become the point at which blame settles after an automated system fails.

What verification cannot settle

The demand for independent verification is easily misunderstood. Independence is not achieved simply by asking a second chatbot to agree with the first. Two systems may share sources, assumptions or characteristic errors, even when their outputs are produced separately. The important question is whether the check is capable of exposing mistakes that the generating process is likely to miss. Sometimes that calls for a different model; sometimes for a different method, direct access to evidence or a reviewer empowered to dispute the premise. Independence is always a matter of degree and design; when it cannot be obtained at a reasonable cost, the responsible choice may be to limit what the system is permitted to do.

A calculation can be checked against an independently implemented formula; a quotation against the document from which it was supposedly taken; a proposed action against an authorized rule. Much of this work can be automated, and in appropriate circumstances it should be. Reliable mechanical checks can preserve scarce human attention for judgments that require it. Compulsory manual approval of every minor output would burden work without necessarily improving it, and might eventually encourage people to bypass the ritual.

The limit appears when the criteria themselves are at stake. Return to the service queue. A reviewer may confirm that the waiting-time estimates are sound and that the ranking follows the chosen objective. Another test might reveal that complex or urgent cases remain unresolved longer than simpler ones. These findings are valuable, but they do not determine how an organization ought to weigh speed against urgency, fairness and the obligations it has accepted towards its customers. The figures can disclose the consequences of a choice; they cannot alone confer authority on that choice.

Questions of purpose are not beyond rigorous reasoning. Their consequences can be investigated, competing principles argued for, assumptions challenged and trade-offs made explicit. Artificial systems may contribute valuable analysis. Herbert Simon’s distinction between factual and value premises in administrative decision-making reminds us that this problem did not begin with generative AI. Yet the ability to calculate under a chosen criterion cannot justify, by itself, why that criterion should prevail. The new pressure comes from the ease with which the system can make the calculation and its supporting argument appear to be one completed judgment.

In Thinking and Moral Considerations, Hannah Arendt considers how thinking can loosen the hold of habitual rules and make room for the judgment of particular situations. Her inquiry belonged to a historical and moral horizon far removed from artificial intelligence; it offers no ready-made theory of organizational design. Its relevance here lies in the possibility of interruption. If a person is assigned the final act of approval but given no occasion to question the rule, the presence of that person cannot guarantee the exercise of judgment.

Professional expertise includes more than applying established knowledge. It includes noticing when a familiar category no longer fits. An experienced colleague may see that an apparently routine request conceals a particular vulnerability, or that a target which improved last quarter has begun to damage the service it was meant to measure. Such perceptions cannot be guaranteed by placing a human at the end of a workflow. They require opportunities to observe, institutional permission to disagree and habits of attention kept alive through practice.

This is a boundary of verification, rather than a reason to diminish its importance. We need the ability to check claims against evidence and, separately, the capacity to reconsider the purposes that make those claims relevant. The second undertaking is less easily formalized. It is also where responsibility begins to extend beyond correctness.

The power to reopen a decision

Modern organizations cannot function without delegation, and delegating to machines is not inherently a retreat from human freedom. Automated systems can carry out some well-defined tasks with speed and consistency beyond what people can reasonably sustain. It would be perverse to require fresh human permission for every routine action. What matters is how the authority to automate has been established, where its limits lie and under what conditions it can be withdrawn.

Long before generative AI, research on human interaction with automation distinguished the functions that can be automated from the degrees of human involvement. It also exposed the difficulty of calibrating trust: a generally dependable system may invite excessive reliance, while opacity makes appropriate intervention harder. Calling a workflow human in the loop therefore tells us surprisingly little. It identifies a position in a process without establishing whether the person occupying it can exercise informed judgment.

An organization might allow a well-tested system to handle routine requests automatically, within defined limits and with records, exception routes and periodic evaluation. Decisions with more serious consequences may need stronger verification or explicit authorization. Applicable laws can impose additional requirements in particular contexts. The appropriate design will vary with risk; a control that detects nothing, permits no objection and cannot stop an action merely transfers delay and anxiety to the person asked to approve it.

The deeper requirement is that authority remain traceable and contestable. Filippo Santoni de Sio and Jeroen van den Hoven have described meaningful human control in terms of a system’s responsiveness to relevant human reasons and the possibility of tracing its outcomes to responsible human agents. For an organization, that inquiry must also reach the criteria adopted before the system begins work. It should be possible to discover who chose the objective, which evidence was admitted, what was tested, who accepted the remaining uncertainty and how an affected person can seek correction. An audit log alone does not answer these questions. A system may record every operation while concealing who can still change the rule that governs it.

In the company handling customer-service requests, meaningful oversight would include the power to revise the definition of success. Waiting time might need to be weighed against urgency and the harm caused by leaving a difficult case unresolved. The balance cannot be inferred from processing speed alone. Those choosing the criterion must be able to explain its purpose, answer objections from people bearing its costs and revise it when its consequences become evident. These conditions do not guarantee that the choice is just; they make its justification open to challenge. Customers need a practicable route to challenge the outcome, rather than an assurance that the ranking was computed correctly.

Even a private service queue distributes attention, resources and burdens; the priority assigned to one person affects the time another must wait. When a generated explanation presents that ranking as a technical necessity, it can hide the earlier choice of what the organization considers valuable. The process may operate smoothly while the question of its purposes becomes increasingly difficult to raise.

The formation of competence is at stake as well. In earlier generations of automation, researchers observed a paradox: people assigned to monitor reliable systems could lose practice in the skills they would need when intervention became essential. Generative AI may extend a related risk from operating machinery to forming interpretations and arguments. This is a possibility to investigate, not a universal fate of automated work.

The less often a professional has to construct an argument, the harder it may become to detect an assumption buried inside a polished conclusion. When the available alternatives are supplied in advance, possibilities left outside the list may gradually escape attention. Yet intelligent tools can also enlarge learning by introducing contrary evidence, unfamiliar perspectives and difficult objections. Whether they narrow or expand judgment will depend partly on how people are trained and how work is organized.

A separation of cognitive powers that protects only the final signature would therefore be incomplete. It must also preserve the conditions under which people become capable of signing responsibly: access to evidence, opportunity to gain experience, time to challenge a proposal and genuine authority to change a procedure. These conditions require resources. Their absence can impose costs that remain invisible until a difficult case arrives.

This is no universal blueprint, and naming three functions does not demonstrate that an organization has improved. The distribution of tasks will differ between drafting an internal note, managing a production process and making a decision that affects someone's livelihood. Its value must be examined against results: errors actually caught, consequences avoided, the cost of controls, skills retained and genuine opportunities to correct a harmful decision. Where separation merely adds steps, it should be revised or abandoned. The possibility of legitimate full automation remains: it depends on the scope of the delegated authority, the consequences of error and the arrangements for review and reversal.

The distinction nevertheless survives the limitations of any single design. A system can generate a plausible option and the organization can verify its accuracy without yet having justified the decision to act on it. The authority to choose an objective, to reconsider it and to answer for its effects has to be located somewhere that a convincing output cannot simply bypass.

Generative AI makes the confusion unusually tempting because it can give the entire movement—from question to explanation to proposed action—the appearance of a single, continuous act of intelligence. The system may be useful throughout that movement, including in the search for contrary evidence. But continuity of performance must not silently become continuity of authority.

Return, finally, to the service queue. The request left waiting at the bottom may have been placed there by a perfectly executed instruction. Its position can still reveal that the organization has mistaken what it can easily measure for what it ought to value. A responsible organization leaves someone able to make that discovery count: to question the measure, change the priority and answer to those who have borne its consequences.
