# Antonio Gramsci

*Hegemony, common sense, and the long education of a political world*

**Circles of Ink · International · Guide 11**  
**Within the triad:** Unconscious, hegemony, sovereignty — Freud · Gramsci · Hobbes

> **Guiding question:** How does a historical arrangement become so familiar that people experience it as the natural order of things—and how can that familiarity be challenged?

## A reader's route

The main essay moves through nine encounters: the factory councils of Turin; the making of a political newspaper; the fracture between southern Italy and its northern centres of power; the notebooks written under fascist imprisonment; the contradictions inside common sense; the institutions that make authority durable; the intellectual who organizes a collective voice; a transformation that preserves the power it appears to disturb; and the present-day struggle over what counts as realistic. The **Atlas desk** is a separate reference work, with biography and context, disciplinary circles, works, an original reading instrument, ideas and concepts, the constellation of thinkers, limitations, a glossary, study routes and FAQs. The essay makes a historical and philosophical argument. The Atlas lets the reader return to particular distinctions without losing that argument.

## The main essay

### 1. Inside the factory, a question of government

Turin in 1919 was a city of workshops, industrial discipline, newspapers and political argument. The war had ended, leaving inflation, social unrest and a revolution elsewhere in Europe whose meaning remained fiercely contested. Workers in the large factories faced questions extending beyond wages and hours. Who should organize production? What could those who performed the work know and decide about it? Could the forms of cooperation already present in the factory become the beginnings of a different political order?

Antonio Gramsci approached these questions through the movement of factory councils. With fellow socialists he launched *L'Ordine Nuovo* in May 1919; the journal became closely associated with the Turin council movement. A council was more than a protest meeting. It suggested that workers might learn political and administrative capacities through participation in the organization of their common activity. The factory, already a place of technical discipline and unequal authority, could become a school of collective self-government.

Gramsci's hopes were those of a revolutionary Marxist, not a neutral observer of industrial relations. The council movement belonged to a struggle over social ownership and political power. Its history cannot be detached from the strikes and occupations of the *Biennio Rosso*, the violent postwar reaction, and the defeat of the political possibilities that Gramsci and others saw in it. The hopes matter precisely because the outcome was not what their advocates intended. An institution can teach people to act together without being able, by itself, to transform the wider society that contains it.

This experience left a problem running through his later writing. A group may occupy a strategic position in economic life and still lack the alliances, cultural confidence, institutions and language necessary to lead a society. Production does not automatically create political direction. A class is made capable of collective action through education, organization, argument and relationships with people whose circumstances differ from its own.

It is tempting to begin Gramsci with the famous term **hegemony** and treat the factory councils as an example. The historical direction runs the other way. His theory matured through political engagement and the investigation of its failures. The question that stayed with him was how those who are governed might acquire the capacities required to govern, and why an existing order remains credible long after its contradictions have become visible.

### 2. What a newspaper teaches its readers

A political newspaper places events beside one another. A strike, an argument about education, a parliamentary decision, a book, a speech and a question about language occupy the same space. Readers learn to recognize some events as connected and others as peripheral. Each issue proposes a hierarchy of attention; over time, those choices help form the habits through which people interpret a public world.

Gramsci worked for the socialist press before founding *L'Ordine Nuovo*, and his journalism cannot be separated from his intellectual life. He wrote on culture and literature as well as industrial conflict. He was interested in the work a periodical could perform for readers who had received little of the education ordinarily reserved for the ruling classes. The newspaper was a site of instruction, disagreement and organization; it could give a dispersed experience the means to become a collective argument.

For that reason, the famous idea of **organic intellectuals** begins to become intelligible before we reach its later theoretical formulation. Intellectual work is performed in many social roles. Someone who organizes a journal, teaches a skill, makes an argument understandable or links scattered experiences can help a group develop a coherent account of itself. The function is historical and social; possessing a university title is neither necessary nor sufficient.

The newspaper also reveals a danger. The effort to educate a public can shade into the effort to determine in advance what that public is allowed to think. A movement that claims to give voice to a class may end by speaking in its place. Gramsci's commitment to a revolutionary party and collective discipline makes this a real problem for his work, not a hypothetical accusation imposed from outside. How can an organization provide direction while enlarging the capacity of its members to judge? At what point does education become obedience?

These questions extend to his conception of hegemony. An established social order enjoys durable influence when its view of the world comes to circulate through institutions, practices, beliefs and language. It can present particular interests as if they expressed a wider good. Equally, those who challenge an order need more than the ability to denounce it. They need forms of association in which a different understanding of social life becomes thinkable and practicable.

A page of news is a modest object. Yet it reveals what may be at stake in cultural leadership: the repeated, often unremarked labor of teaching people which questions belong together.

### 3. The country that could not speak with one voice

Gramsci was born in Ales, Sardinia, in 1891. Poverty, the instability of his family's circumstances and long-standing health difficulties marked his childhood. In 1911 he arrived in Turin on a scholarship to study at the university. The change of setting brought him into the industrial and political heart of northern Italy. It also sharpened his awareness of the distance between that world and the lives of rural and southern populations.

The history matters without becoming a psychological key that explains every later thought. Gramsci's political understanding of the *Southern Question* took shape through the analysis of land, class, intellectual leadership and the incomplete formation of a national people. The unification of Italy had created a state, but common citizenship did not eliminate regional inequalities or establish equal participation in cultural life. Industrial workers and southern peasants could have interests in common while remaining separated by institutions, language, mistrust and contrasting political experience.

In his unfinished essay on aspects of the Southern Question, written shortly before his arrest in 1926, Gramsci examines the relation among the northern working class, the southern peasantry and the intellectual groups through which social relations were organized. A potential alliance cannot be willed into existence by a declaration of solidarity. It depends on understanding different positions in the social order and on creating a political language in which those differences can be negotiated.

Here the national question joins the problem of **subalternity**. To be subordinate is partly to lack control over resources and institutions. It may also mean that the record of one's own experience is supplied by others: landlords, officials, journalists, schoolbooks, experts, or political movements that claim to know what a community needs. Subaltern histories can survive as fragments in records made for purposes alien to their subjects. Recovering those fragments requires more than announcing that the oppressed are finally speaking.

Gramsci's later notebooks return to the Risorgimento, folklore, literature, education and language because the formation of a nation is a cultural problem as well as a constitutional one. The gulf between learned Italian and vernacular speech has political significance. An official language may connect citizens while marking some of them as unable to speak with recognized authority. A school can open a route into public life or reproduce the social distance it claims to overcome.

The lesson is demanding. A political community cannot be inferred from a map, a flag or an economic statistic. It has to be made through institutions and reciprocal recognition, and those means can also become instruments of exclusion. The nation that appears unified from its capital may remain divided in the daily experience of its inhabitants.

### 4. A notebook written under custody

On 8 November 1926 Gramsci was arrested by the fascist authorities, despite his status as a member of parliament. In June 1928 the Special Tribunal sentenced him to more than twenty years' imprisonment. He spent much of his detention at Turi, near Bari, and later in medical facilities as his health deteriorated. He died in Rome in April 1937. These facts demand attention in their own right: a political opponent of Mussolini was deprived of his freedom, and the imprisonment severely damaged an already vulnerable life.

The *Prison Notebooks* were written chiefly between 1929 and 1935, under restrictions of health, surveillance, available books and working materials. They were neither one continuous manuscript nor a completed book waiting to be published. Gramsci drafted notes, made translations, returned to earlier problems and produced revised treatments of some subjects. Different kinds of notebooks and different stages of writing survived. Their present organization is inseparable from the history of editing them.

The material history of the text matters. After the war, the notebooks were issued in thematic volumes between 1948 and 1951. Valentino Gerratana's critical edition of 1975 restored their sequence with a different scholarly ambition. The ongoing National Edition distinguishes translation, miscellaneous and special notebooks. A paragraph encountered under a thematic heading in one English collection may belong to a different position in the handwritten sequence; some formulations were subsequently reconsidered by Gramsci himself. Reading him well requires attention to those layers, especially when a sentence has become a political slogan.

The prison did not grant the thinker a privileged view of history. It deprived him of information and direct participation in political life. At the same time, his notebooks gave the experience of defeat a new intellectual scale. He investigated why revolutionary advances in Russia could not simply be repeated in the developed societies of Western Europe; how institutions surrounding the state sustained existing relations; how people acquired convictions; how the relation between economic forces and political organization had to be understood without a mechanically predetermined outcome.

The notebooks use an exploratory form suited to a thinker whose questions cannot easily be settled. An idea introduced in one note may be qualified elsewhere. A concept like hegemony unfolds through historical investigations instead of appearing as a dictionary definition from which every consequence follows. The text's incompletion is a condition of its interpretation. A guide should make that work more navigable while preserving the places where the thinker is still at work.

The image of a prisoner writing about freedom has an obvious emotional force. Gramsci's more difficult legacy lies in what the pages demand intellectually: patient attention to the organizations, beliefs and compromises through which political orders endure.

### 5. What everyone already knows

Imagine hearing someone say that a particular profession is unsuitable for people from a certain background. The statement may arrive as ordinary realism, supported by anecdotes and repeated experience. Elsewhere, the same speaker may insist that ability should decide opportunity. Both claims may be sincerely held. The contradiction is familiar because the language we inherit is assembled from different times, institutions and social positions. It carries practical experience alongside judgments no one has examined carefully.

Gramsci calls this unstable mixture **common sense**—*senso comune*. The phrase has a different reach from its usual English sense of reasonable practicality. He treats common sense as a historically layered and often contradictory conception of the world. Fragments of religious belief, popular philosophy, inherited prejudice, scientific claims and everyday experience can coexist within it without forming a consistent doctrine. The contradiction matters because people live and act through such mixtures. Formal philosophical arguments enter a field that already contains explanations, expectations and loyalties.

Within common sense, Gramsci identifies the possibility of **good sense**—*buon senso*: a more coherent and critical element that can be developed from practical experience. That possibility distinguishes his educational ambition from contempt for everyday thought. People do not enter politics as empty vessels to be filled with a superior doctrine. Their judgments contain knowledge as well as error, and political education has to work through that experience.

This is related to his claim that every person participates, in some way, in a conception of the world. Such participation does not make everyone a professional philosopher. It means that language, action and judgment already carry assumptions about causation, justice, possibility and obligation. To examine those assumptions is to begin transforming one's relationship to them.

The resulting conception of freedom is slow and demanding. A person can reject a particular opinion while preserving the larger framework that made it seem plausible. An institution can change its official vocabulary while leaving its incentives and hierarchies intact. Conversely, a lived experience may expose the limits of an accepted account and provide material from which a more coherent understanding can grow. The struggle takes place inside ordinary speech, where inherited authority meets circumstances that no longer fit it.

Common sense is therefore a political terrain. The contest is partly about which experiences are given names, which contradictions can be publicly discussed and what counts as an argument worth hearing. Whoever dominates that terrain helps determine the terms on which more explicit political conflicts will be understood.

### 6. The fortifications around the state

In the *Prison Notebooks*, Gramsci repeatedly returns to the relation between **political society** and **civil society**. The first term points toward the apparatuses of government and coercion; the second toward the organizations and relationships through which forms of consent and cultural direction develop. Schools, churches, newspapers, associations, and other institutions can give a political order a social depth that no police force alone could provide.

His conception of the **integral state** brings these dimensions together. The state in an expanded sense involves both the exercise of coercion and the active organization of consent. This is an analytic proposition about modern power, not the assertion that every school or voluntary association is simply a hidden branch of government. Institutions can conflict with ruling groups, and struggles over civil society are part of what makes hegemony historically unstable.

Gramsci describes the developed societies of Western Europe as having dense institutional defenses beyond the immediate state apparatus. The image of trenches and fortifications belongs to a comparison between different strategic circumstances. A direct seizure of political offices—a **war of movement**—might not produce lasting transformation where a whole field of organized consent remains intact. A **war of position** involves the difficult formation of alliances, capacities and cultural leadership across that field. These terms have histories and uses within Marxist political strategy; they are much more than labels for short- and long-term advertising campaigns.

Hegemony is best understood through this setting. It concerns leadership and direction, exercised in a relation with domination and sustained through social organization. A ruling group can make a particular historical arrangement credible as a wider horizon of life. Its authority is reproduced in habits and institutions, in the kinds of expertise deemed trustworthy, and in the social alliances that make some projects appear possible while others seem unthinkable.

The relationship is never perfectly smooth. Consent may be partial, contested, negotiated or withdrawn. A society can be governed through a mixture of agreement, fear, dependence and resignation. Gramsci's distinction between direction and domination should not be turned into a fiction that successful hegemony means everyone happily agrees. Organized coercion remains part of the integral state, sometimes becoming especially visible during crises of leadership.

The theory changes what we ask about political stability. A government can win an election while failing to establish durable leadership. A new law can alter formal rights while institutions continue to reproduce older hierarchies. A movement can capture public attention without developing schools, associations, alliances or trustworthy practices. Durable political change is a problem of the whole social formation.

### 7. Who gives a group its language?

Gramsci's **organic intellectuals** have often been mistaken for loyal publicists attached to a party or social group. His account is more historically ambitious. Every emerging group engaged in social production tends to create people who organize its practical life and give it greater coherence: technicians, administrators, educators, specialists and those who connect economic activity to wider forms of thought and conduct. Intellectual function describes a role in the making of a collective world.

Alongside them are **traditional intellectuals**, whose positions appear to persist across changes in the social order. Clergy, teachers and other professional groups may understand themselves as independent bearers of knowledge, even where their institutions have long histories of connection with social power. The distinction identifies social relationships and functions. It is not a moral ranking by which one group is automatically virtuous and the other corrupt.

A movement's task, in Gramsci's account, is to build the ability to organize experience, to deliberate and to speak beyond its immediate membership. This helps explain his interest in education and the formation of intellectuals. A school can transmit existing distinctions of prestige and competence; it can also make unfamiliar forms of thought accessible to those whose lives have not prepared them for the institution's language. The problem of intellectual leadership is inseparable from the conditions of learning.

Machiavelli becomes a crucial interlocutor. Reading *The Prince* through the conditions of modern mass politics, Gramsci develops the idea of the **modern Prince**: an organized political body capable of forming a collective will. In his historical setting the revolutionary party is central to that project. Its role combines organization and education, connecting dispersed grievances to a larger political direction.

The proposal has a profound danger. The organization that claims to form a collective will may start to identify that will with its own leadership. The distinction between enabling people to think and authorizing oneself to think for them can become thin. Gramsci did not give a complete institutional solution to this problem; his commitment to communist party organization and political discipline must remain visible. The theory of organic intellectuals can illuminate emancipation and the possible concentration of interpretive authority within the same movement.

His work is often borrowed to discuss media strategists, academics, cultural professionals or influential public figures. These analogies require specificity. An algorithm that ranks messages has effects on visibility and attention; it does not thereby become an organic intellectual in Gramsci's historical and social sense. People and institutions make decisions about the system's purposes, its design and the kinds of authority it helps to establish. The location of that human responsibility is part of the question.

### 8. Change that leaves command intact

The history of Italian unification occupied Gramsci throughout the notebooks. He examined why the Risorgimento produced a national state without the broader popular mobilization and transformation that other historical possibilities might have entailed. The interests of established groups could be rearranged; reforms could occur; political authority could be consolidated without the subaltern becoming an effective author of the change.

The term **passive revolution** helps him investigate transformations that absorb certain demands for change while preserving decisive relations of leadership. A ruling group may concede or reorganize enough to prevent an alternative force from forming a durable independent project. Such outcomes are historically complex. A reform can improve people's lives and still leave the structure of power largely intact. Neither its benefits nor its limits should be denied for the sake of a clean ideological verdict.

Gramsci also investigates **Americanism and Fordism**. Industrial organization transforms labor processes, working time, bodily routines and expectations far beyond the factory gate. Productive arrangements demand ways of living; managerial techniques have implications for discipline, private life, consumption and the training of capacities. The factory becomes part of the making of a particular human type. This argument relates to the early council movement, but it is informed by a much darker knowledge of how modern production can organize consent and constrain conduct.

Taken together, passive revolution and Fordism prevent the word *culture* from floating free of material history. The structures through which people live are built from work, machines, law, wages, households, education and authority. A new way of naming an institution does not necessarily alter the relations it organizes. Equally, changes in productive life can reshape expectations and values without any single institution announcing a cultural programme.

There are limits to the theory. It can become so capacious that every reform is denounced as a disguised victory for the old order, rendering historical change impossible to evaluate on its actual effects. Gramsci's concepts ask for careful inquiry into who acts, what changes and what remains; they should be tested against evidence rather than used as labels that settle the case in advance.

The person living through a social transformation may gain real opportunities while remaining excluded from the decisions that define those opportunities. It is in that gap between material change and political authorship that Gramsci's thought remains especially searching.

### 9. The struggle over what sounds reasonable

A town proposes to reorganize its public transport. Officials cite a budget deficit; some residents speak of routes that have become too costly. Others point to workers who depend on the first bus of the morning, older people who cannot drive and students whose journeys are about to become longer. The argument is quickly described as a choice between efficiency and sentiment. Once that description takes hold, some forms of evidence begin to sound serious and others are treated as complaints.

This is a contemporary illustrative case created for the guide, not an event described by Gramsci. It shows how much can be decided before a formal vote. The words *efficient*, *realistic*, *essential* and *affordable* do necessary work in public debate, but they can also conceal judgments about whose time, mobility and opportunities count as costs. The issue cannot be resolved by discovering that every participant has an ideology. It requires opening the frame itself to criticism and gathering the forms of evidence the original frame has neglected.

Gramsci's concern with **common sense** gives us a way to study that preliminary struggle. A vocabulary has a history. Public institutions, schools, newspapers, professional associations and political movements help determine what is readily believed. Contemporary platforms add new conditions of repetition and visibility. Recommendation systems, commercial incentives and the authority of particular voices can make some descriptions more familiar than others. Gramsci did not witness these technologies; describing an algorithm as a new intellectual class would confuse an infrastructure with the people, organizations and relations of power that create and operate it.

The contemporary wound identified by Cerchi is **the struggle over what appears self-evident**. A society may present its inherited arrangements as natural limits of choice, even while describing itself as open and free. The possibility of criticism depends partly on the availability of words that make alternatives recognizable. A group denied the means to describe its own experience may be heard only when it speaks in the categories supplied by those already in authority.

This does not mean every prevailing opinion is false, nor that all social agreement is manufactured. Public institutions need habits, trust and stable expectations. An education can transmit knowledge worth preserving; a shared norm can protect the vulnerable. The question concerns how these arrangements were formed, whose experience they contain, and whether they can be revised when their costs become visible. Gramsci's strongest insight demands historical investigation rather than an indiscriminate suspicion of everything familiar.

The link to Freud and Hobbes in this triad is a tension between different levels of explanation. Freud's inquiry unsettles the subject's account of their own motives. Gramsci studies the historical formation of the language and institutions through which subjects understand their world. Hobbes asks how political order can be authorized amid fear and conflict. These problems may intersect, but none can be collapsed into the others. A person's inward attachments, an organized culture and a sovereign command have different histories and different demands for evidence.

The transport meeting finally has to make a decision. No inquiry can exempt public life from disagreement and consequences. But the deliberation will be different if those taking part can dispute the categories that seemed to settle the matter before anyone spoke. Gramsci helps us recognize this earlier field of struggle: the long formation of what a community has learned to call obvious.

## The Atlas desk

### Gramsci at a glance

**Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937).** Italian journalist, communist political leader and philosopher, born in Ales in Sardinia. A leading participant in the Turin workers' movement, a co-founder of the Communist Party of Italy and later its secretary, he was imprisoned by the fascist regime in 1926. During detention he wrote the *Prison Notebooks* and an extensive correspondence.

**The historical problem.** Why can a ruling order endure beyond any particular law or government, and how can people who occupy subordinate positions become capable of acting collectively?

**The main intellectual fields.** Political history; Marxist philosophy and the theory of the state; language and culture; education; the formation of intellectuals; social classes and subaltern groups; economic organization; political strategy.

**Entry points.** The Turin factory-council writings; *Some Aspects of the Southern Question*; carefully contextualized selections from the *Prison Notebooks* on intellectuals, common sense, hegemony, state and civil society; the *Prison Letters*.

**An essential distinction.** Gramsci's hegemony concerns direction within a historically organized society. It involves education, alliances, institutions and consent in relation to the possibility of coercion. It cannot be reduced to propaganda, popularity or message control.

### Why Gramsci matters

Gramsci gives political thought an expanded field of evidence. A constitution or election tells us something about power; a school system, newspaper, workplace and inherited vocabulary tell us how political possibilities become familiar or remote. His investigation of hegemony joins material relationships to cultural formation, revealing why an economic grievance does not automatically become a collective political project.

He is also a thinker of the unequal capacity to speak in public. Groups may be materially subordinated while the authoritative language for describing their lives is held elsewhere. In his work, emancipation involves the ability to organize, understand and criticize one's own conditions, along with the resources and alliances necessary to make that understanding consequential. The project cannot be achieved by an eloquent message alone.

His intellectual importance survives the considerable limits of his revolutionary politics. We can study how durable consent is made while refusing to assume that any party or leader has a right to manufacture it without challenge.

### The human problem

No one begins with an untouched mind. We inherit words and distinctions, habits of respect and contempt, judgments about what appears possible. Even opposition to an established order may rely on the categories through which that order taught us to speak. Gramsci asks how people can make their inherited understanding an object of collective criticism and develop a more coherent account of their experience.

The problem reaches from everyday language to the organization of political will. To identify an injustice may be a beginning. To turn dispersed recognition into a durable capacity to act requires institutions, alliances, intellectual work and the willingness to revise one's own assumptions.

**Lives connected to the work.** Tatiana Schucht, his sister-in-law, preserved and supported much of the prison correspondence and was vital in the fate of the manuscripts. Giulia Schucht was Gramsci's wife and the mother of their two sons. Piero Sraffa, an economist and friend, played an important role in the network that made books and other forms of support available. Their roles should be recognized without treating correspondence as an unfiltered autobiography or reducing Gramsci's thought to his suffering.

**Historical setting.** The crisis of Italian liberal institutions, the First World War and its aftermath, the Russian Revolution, workers' occupations, the rise of Mussolini and the transformation of political conflict by mass parties all belong to Gramsci's intellectual world. He was a committed Marxist revolutionary. A guide that treats his ideas as neutral techniques for cultural influence would remove their historical object and their political stakes.

### The disciplinary circles

**Political philosophy and the state.** Leadership, coercion, legitimacy, civil and political society, the formation of collective will and the difference between obtaining office and becoming capable of durable social direction.

**Marxism, political economy and historical change.** Economic relationships, class struggle, the philosophy of praxis, historical blocs, passive revolution and the material conditions of cultural power.

**Sociology of culture and everyday belief.** Common sense, good sense, folklore, religion, institutions and the historically inherited categories through which ordinary people understand the world.

**Language, education and philology.** The relation between national and regional languages, the role of linguistic competence in participation, educational institutions and the transformation of practical experience into articulate judgment.

**Journalism, media and public life.** Periodicals as instruments of argument, organization and political education. The study of modern digital environments belongs to later readers and requires new evidence.

**Organization and intellectual labor.** Traditional and organic intellectuals, the formation of social groups, political parties, alliances and the conflicts between educating a public and claiming to speak on its behalf.

**Territory, nation and subaltern history.** Sardinia, the Southern Question, the Risorgimento, the north–south divide, agrarian society and the ways subordinate groups enter historical records.

**Industrial organization and modernity.** Factory councils, Americanism and Fordism, discipline of labor and the social effects of systems of production.

**Literature and cultural formation.** Dante, popular fiction, the theatre of Pirandello, intellectual life and the public circulation of literary forms; a crucial corrective to the notion of Gramsci as a theorist only of governmental power.

### The works: a map for reading

**The Turin journalism and the early socialist writings (1913–1918).** Short articles about political life, culture, education and social conflict. These are indispensable for seeing the writer who moves between daily events and broader questions of collective understanding. Collections and datings differ by edition.

***L'Ordine Nuovo* (1919–1920 and later activity).** A journal founded with collaborators including Palmiro Togliatti, Angelo Tasca and Umberto Terracini. Its discussions of factory councils and workers' democracy show the political and institutional concerns from which later questions developed.

***The Revolution against Capital* (1917).** An article on the Bolshevik Revolution and mechanical interpretations of Marxian historical development. Read as a polemical intervention under particular revolutionary circumstances, rather than a finished theory of the relation between economy and politics.

***Some Aspects of the Southern Question* (*Alcuni temi della quistione meridionale*, 1926, unfinished).** A crucial essay on northern workers, southern peasants, intellectuals and the construction of political alliances. It links territorial inequality to the problem of who can direct a common project.

***The Prison Notebooks* (*Quaderni del carcere*, written 1929–1935).** Gramsci's central mature intellectual workshop. Its notes investigate hegemony, civil society, the state, Machiavelli, the Risorgimento, intellectuals, common sense, language, history, subaltern groups, Americanism and Fordism, and Croce, among many other subjects. The notebooks are layered writings, sometimes revised. Their editorial history must accompany interpretation.

***Prison Letters* (*Lettere dal carcere*).** Correspondence with relatives and friends, indispensable for the experience of captivity, intellectual work, family life and the limits under which he wrote. The letters' literary quality should not erase the unequal conditions of communication in prison.

**Posthumous thematic volumes (1948–1951).** The initial arrangement grouped notes by subject, including *The Intellectuals and the Organization of Culture*, *Notes on Machiavelli, Politics and the Modern State*, *The Risorgimento*, *Literature and National Life* and *Past and Present*. These titles are editorial arrangements of notebook material, not necessarily finished books planned by the author.

**Valentino Gerratana's critical edition (1975).** A pivotal reconstruction of the notebooks' sequence, with critical apparatus. The later National Edition continues textual and chronological study, distinguishing translation notebooks, miscellaneous notebooks and special notebooks. The history of publication affects how a single passage may be cited and read.

**A note on English editions.** Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith's *Selections from the Prison Notebooks* (1971) was central to international reception. Selections cannot stand for the entire corpus, and some of the critical vocabulary differs among translations. The multivolume translations associated with Joseph A. Buttigieg have also expanded access to the notebooks. Use the notebook number, paragraph and reliable edition when precision matters.

### A Cerchi reading instrument: the frame of the obvious

This instrument is an **original contemporary reading developed for Cerchi d'inchiostro**. It is informed by Gramsci's treatment of hegemony and common sense; it is not one of his authored analytical tables. Its purpose is to examine a public dispute without treating disagreement as evidence that one side has simply been manipulated.

**Begin with a sentence accepted without argument.** Examples might include 'There is no alternative to closing this route', 'This kind of work deserves less pay', or 'People from this district do not attend university'. The task is to establish where the claim is repeated, who accepts it and what experience it appears to summarize. The claim may contain a real constraint, an inherited prejudice, or a mixture of both.

**Trace the institutions that make the sentence familiar.** Which policies, school practices, employers, local organizations, news outlets or administrative categories give it durability? Distinguish active persuasion from the quiet continuity of a rule or habit. Include the material circumstances that make the statement plausible for some people.

**Ask whose experience it translates and whose it erases.** A budget can reveal a shortage while overlooking the different costs borne by those who lose a service. A professional category can help allocate resources while obscuring skills it was never designed to recognize. Listen for the experience that lacks authorized words.

**Look for the critical element already present in everyday thought.** Gramsci's *good sense* is a reason to examine what people know about their own lives. A counterclaim becomes stronger when it can connect lived experience, evidence and a coherent account of the wider situation. It is weaker when it simply substitutes a new slogan.

**Identify the capacities required to change the arrangement.** Who must be able to participate? What alliances, organizations, expertise and sustained practices would allow the claim to be tested and revised? A protest may reveal a problem; transforming the public decision requires ways to act upon it.

**Example: a school merger.** Local officials say that combining two schools will be efficient. The claim can be measured against expenditure, staffing and educational provision. Parents and teachers may raise the costs of longer journeys, the needs of vulnerable students and the place of a school in the life of a community. The question becomes whether the institution's definition of efficiency is adequate to the good it has been authorized to provide. A more honest deliberation may still favor the merger, but the reasons will have been tested against what the first formulation left unseen.

**The instrument's limit.** The diagnosis of hegemony must not become the idea that anyone who disagrees with the analyst is merely the victim of false consciousness. Consent can be reasoned, contested and partial. An institution may earn genuine trust. The test belongs to public argument and evidence; its findings must remain corrigible.

### Key ideas

1. **Leadership is historically made.** Durable political direction depends on relations of production, institutions, alliances, cultural life and the organization of consent.
2. **Coercion remains part of modern rule.** The integral state joins political and civil society; cultural leadership should not be imagined as a power free of force.
3. **Common sense is contradictory.** Everyday thinking contains historical layers, practical insight and inherited error. It is the terrain on which critical education works.
4. **Intellectual function is social.** Organizing knowledge and collective understanding is not confined to university scholars or recognized writers.
5. **A group does not become a political subject automatically.** Its economic position does not by itself provide the institutions, language or alliances required to act collectively.
6. **Subaltern history is fragmentary.** The experience of subordinate people is frequently preserved through accounts produced by others.
7. **Political strategy has a cultural duration.** A war of position attends to the institutional terrain of consent and direction beyond the immediate state apparatus.
8. **Change may preserve domination.** Passive revolution names forms of transformation whose benefits and limits must be investigated together.
9. **Production forms ways of life.** Fordism helps show the relation among industrial organization, discipline, social expectations and culture.
10. **A different future requires different practices of judgment.** This is a Cerchi contemporary application of Gramsci's attention to the making of what appears possible.

### Key concepts

**Hegemony (*egemonia*).** Political and cultural leadership through which a social group seeks to organize a wider order and build consent, in a relation with domination and coercion. Its meaning changes with Gramsci's inquiries; it is never a synonym for public relations.

**Common sense (*senso comune*).** The heterogeneous, historically formed beliefs and assumptions through which people experience the world, often containing contradictions they have not examined.

**Good sense (*buon senso*).** Elements of practical, critical understanding that can be developed within common sense toward greater coherence.

**Organic intellectuals.** People whose social function organizes and gives coherence to the activity and outlook of a developing social group; their work may be technical, cultural, administrative, educational or political.

**Traditional intellectuals.** Groups who appear to enjoy continuity or autonomy across historical changes, while still occupying historically formed social positions.

**Political society.** The institutions and relations more directly associated with formal government and organized coercion.

**Civil society.** Institutions and associations through which social groups organize their relations, cultural leadership and consent. It is not identical with a morally pure sphere free of politics.

**Integral state.** Gramsci's expanded conception of state power joining political society and civil society, including the interaction between consent and coercion.

**Historical bloc (*blocco storico*).** The concrete relation among material structures, institutions, social forces and forms of consciousness within a historical order. It is not merely a coalition formed for one election.

**Philosophy of praxis (*filosofia della prassi*).** Gramsci's way of developing a historically grounded Marxist philosophy attentive to the reciprocal relations of thought, activity, institutions and material life. Censorship is part of the context of his terminology; its intellectual significance exceeds circumlocution alone.

**Modern Prince (*moderno Principe*).** A political organization capable of forming collective will and direction, developed in Gramsci's reading of Machiavelli and the problems of modern mass politics.

**War of position (*guerra di posizione*).** Strategic struggle within the dense institutions and social relationships through which political direction is sustained, contrasted with a war of movement.

**War of movement (*guerra di movimento*).** A strategic image for direct political confrontation or assault on positions of power, whose effectiveness varies with historical conditions.

**Passive revolution (*rivoluzione passiva*).** A historically specific pattern of transformation in which ruling groups incorporate elements of change while restricting the autonomous political role of subaltern forces.

**Subaltern groups (*gruppi sociali subalterni*).** Groups whose histories and powers of organization are constrained by relations of domination, often rendering their independent political action discontinuous or fragmentary.

**National-popular (*nazionale-popolare*).** A concern with the relation among cultural life, the formation of a national people, and the ability of intellectual work to engage widely shared historical experience.

**Transformism (*trasformismo*).** A process in which political leadership absorbs or incorporates figures and demands from other social forces, limiting the development of independent alternatives in the historical contexts Gramsci investigates.

**Organic crisis.** A crisis of authority and political direction extending beyond a momentary government failure, in which established arrangements lose the ability to organize consent effectively.

**Americanism and Fordism.** Gramsci's investigation of modern productive organization and its relations to bodies, discipline, social habits and the making of new forms of life.

**Collective will (*volontà collettiva*).** The capacity of social forces to form an organized political project rather than remain a collection of disconnected grievances.

### The constellation

**Before Gramsci: political realism and material history.** **Machiavelli** is indispensable to the thought of the modern Prince: how political power is founded, organized and made durable in a real historical world. **Marx** supplies the analysis of class, capitalist production and practical social transformation; **Engels** and the subsequent Marxist traditions shaped the vocabulary Gramsci inherited and contested. **Lenin** is important to the strategic history of hegemony and to Gramsci's revolutionary political commitments. **Vico** and **Croce** belong to the philosophical and historiographical setting in which history, culture and human activity were debated. These inheritances should be differentiated rather than blended into one line of predecessors.

**Contemporaries and opponents.** **Benedetto Croce** is a sustained philosophical interlocutor, not simply a name standing for bourgeois culture. **Giovanni Gentile** and the institutionalization of fascist culture form another difficult background. **Mussolini** and the regime provide the immediate history of political repression. **Togliatti, Terracini and Tasca** belong to Gramsci's organizational and journalistic world; debates with **Amadeo Bordiga** matter for party strategy. **Piero Sraffa**, **Tatiana Schucht** and **Giulia Schucht** belong to the human and intellectual history of his detention. The struggles among workers, peasants and political movements are as important as disputes among prominent authors.

**After Gramsci: transformations of the concepts.** **Stuart Hall** and cultural studies developed influential analyses of ideology, popular culture, race and political articulation using Gramscian resources. **Raymond Williams** worked with a related notion of lived, cultural hegemony; the exact relationships of influence and interpretation should be specified in specialist studies. **Ranajit Guha** and the Subaltern Studies collective drew from Gramsci to investigate colonial histories and the difficulty of recovering subordinated voices, while transforming his concepts in different settings. **Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe** made hegemony central to a distinctive theory of political articulation. **Perry Anderson**, **Peter D. Thomas**, **Guido Liguori**, **Fabio Frosini** and others have contested the interpretation and history of Gramsci's concepts. The afterlife contains disagreements, not a single 'Gramscian school'.

**Contemporary fields where the distinctions help.** Public education, regional inequalities, media institutions, professional expertise, civic associations, the formation of political coalitions, labor organization, and the capacity of marginalized communities to determine the language in which their experience is heard. Digital platforms enter as infrastructures within these social relations. A claim of influence or empirical explanation would require evidence beyond conceptual resemblance.

### Legacy: what passes into the present

Gramsci's work has crossed political theory, cultural studies, historiography, education, the sociology of intellectuals and studies of postcolonial subalternity. The range reflects the strength of the questions about consent, historical formation and political direction. It also makes him vulnerable to superficial appropriation: **hegemony** may become a label applied to any successful narrative, and **organic intellectual** a compliment awarded to any public commentator.

The continuing scholarly editions of the notebooks have changed the field by showing how some apparent doctrines developed through sequences of drafts and revisions. His legacy therefore includes a lesson about reading: we inherit a text through the labor and choices of others, and a compelling quotation may conceal the history of the argument from which it came.

### The contemporary wound: who defines the obvious?

The public contest over what seems realistic can shape a decision long before its formal announcement. Terms such as efficiency, safety, merit, competence or freedom may help clarify a question; they can also exclude relevant experiences by determining which costs and reasons may be counted. The wound examined here is the **difficulty of contesting the framework within which a problem is allowed to appear**.

Gramsci helps identify the historical work through which a framework becomes familiar. His categories should not be used to deny the possibility of reasoned consent or to interpret every shared norm as manipulation. Their value lies in asking how a social understanding was formed, what supports it materially and institutionally, and how it can be changed when experiences it has excluded become visible.

### Limits and contradictions

**Party leadership and political pluralism.** Gramsci's account of the modern Prince and the formation of collective will is inseparable from revolutionary communist politics. It raises unresolved questions about the right of a party to represent a class, the independence of intellectuals and the freedom of dissent inside a collective project.

**The danger of strategic instrumentalization.** A theory of cultural direction can be used as if the aim were merely to secure consent for a favored programme. This use threatens the educational and critical possibilities of common sense and good sense. The concepts cannot supply a moral justification for any project that employs them effectively.

**Violence and authoritarian historical settings.** Gramsci was a victim of fascist political imprisonment, but this fact does not exempt his own revolutionary commitments from scrutiny. The relation between coercion, transformation and democratic safeguards remains difficult.

**The incompletion of the notebooks.** Notes with different dates, purposes and degrees of revision do not constitute a closed philosophical system. A guide can organize themes without treating them as chapters Gramsci authoritatively assembled.

**The limits of class categories.** Relations of class and production are central to his analysis. The historical situations of gender, race, empire, colonialism and other forms of inequality require additional concepts and evidence, including from later traditions that revised Gramsci's framework.

**The risk of interpreting everything as hegemony.** A cultural explanation can become unfalsifiable if every agreement proves hegemony and every disagreement proves its depth. Attention to material circumstances, alternative causes and the possibility of legitimate public trust is essential.

**Historical analogy and digital technology.** The relation between a newspaper and a contemporary feed is comparative, never identity. Platforms have architectures and economic incentives that Gramsci could not have studied. Algorithms should not be attributed will or the social role of intellectuals simply because they organize visibility.

**Translation and editorial authority.** International reception has relied on selected and reorganized texts. English keywords such as *civil society*, *common sense* and *state* can conceal differences in Italian usage or context. Edition-sensitive reading remains necessary.

### Curiosities worth following

**Why does he write so much about grammar and language?** His early linguistic studies and his attention to the relation between national and popular culture helped make language a question of social participation and authority.

**Why was a weekly newspaper so important?** *L'Ordine Nuovo* linked intellectual work to practical organization during the Turin factory-council period; a journal could form a political readership and a collective vocabulary.

**Why is the Southern Question central?** It forced Gramsci to examine inequalities and alliances between workers and peasants across regional and cultural divisions within Italy.

**Were the *Prison Notebooks* one book?** They were multiple notebooks of notes, translations, revisions and special studies, transmitted through different editorial arrangements.

**Why does Machiavelli appear in a Marxist's notebooks?** The modern Prince concerns the formation of collective political will and the organization of leadership in a mass society.

**Why is common sense internally contradictory?** It preserves ideas and experiences acquired in different historical situations without making them one fully coherent doctrine.

**Why is Gramsci read outside Marxist politics?** His analysis of culture, institutions, education and public meaning raises questions that later thinkers have reinterpreted in other political and disciplinary contexts.

**What is the risk in calling a platform hegemonic?** The phrase may conceal the actual human and institutional relationships that design, govern and use the platform, and may substitute metaphor for evidence.

### Common misreadings to avoid

- **Hegemony is propaganda.** The construction of durable leadership reaches across institutions, alliances, habits and material relationships.
- **Consent means everyone agrees.** Consent can be uneven, pressured, contested or tied to reliance on the existing order.
- **Civil society is automatically independent of power.** It contains organizations and struggles implicated in the formation of political authority.
- **Good sense is ordinary pragmatism.** It is the potential for critical coherence within the contradictory world of common sense.
- **Organic intellectuals are simply famous influencers.** The concept concerns an organizing function in relation to social groups and production.
- **The modern Prince is an exceptional individual leader.** Gramsci's image concerns political organization and collective will.
- **War of position means improving communications patiently.** It concerns political strategy within a differentiated institutional terrain.
- **A passive revolution changes nothing.** It can produce real reforms while preserving decisive structures of leadership.
- **Gramsci wrote a finished treatise on hegemony.** The notebooks contain layered and sometimes revised inquiries.
- **His prison sentence makes every argument morally unassailable.** The injustice he suffered and the merits of his political theory are separate questions.
- **Digital recommendation systems are organic intellectuals.** Technical systems influence attention through social decisions; they are not historical social groups or intellectual actors in Gramsci's sense.
- **Gramsci predicts the internet.** Applying his categories today is a later interpretive choice requiring fresh evidence.

### Reading paths

**If you have fifteen minutes.** Read sections 1, 5 and 9 of the main essay and the *Frame of the Obvious* instrument. Ask how an economic position becomes a political capability and how a public claim becomes familiar.

**If you have an hour.** Read selections from *L'Ordine Nuovo* about factory councils, *Some Aspects of the Southern Question*, and notebook passages on the formation of intellectuals. Compare organization in production with organization in cultural life. Use a reliable edition that indicates the provenance of the selections.

**If you have a week.** Day 1: Turin journalism and the council movement. Day 2: the Southern Question. Day 3: notebook passages on common sense and the philosophy of praxis. Day 4: intellectuals, education and language. Day 5: state, civil society and war of position. Day 6: passive revolution and Americanism/Fordism. Day 7: selected prison letters and a critical account of the notebooks' editorial history. The aim is to trace how a concept changes when it moves among settings.

**For university study.** Work with Gerratana's 1975 edition or the National Edition where available and examine at least one note in relation to its revision. Compare an English selection with its textual provenance. A concordance is often more valuable than another stand-alone quotation.

**For political and media analysis.** Examine one institutional dispute, identifying its material interests, cultural language, relevant organizations, limits of public participation and alternative explanations. The aim is disciplined historical inquiry, not the assignment of the label *hegemonic* to an opponent.

### Questions to orient a reading

1. Why did Gramsci treat the factory council as a place of political education rather than merely economic bargaining?
2. What work does a newspaper do in the formation of a collective political language?
3. How do regional inequalities and linguistic differences affect political participation?
4. Why do the publication and revision histories of the *Prison Notebooks* matter to interpreting a concept?
5. What makes common sense historically contradictory, and what can good sense achieve within it?
6. How do consent and coercion interact in the integral state?
7. When does the formation of intellectuals enlarge collective capacity, and when can it concentrate authority?
8. What would count as evidence of passive revolution in a particular historical reform?
9. Who can criticize a public decision if the vocabulary of the problem is determined in advance?
10. How does a specifically Gramscian explanation differ from a psychological account of obedience or a theory of sovereign law?

### Nodes to remember

- Gramsci was a revolutionary political organizer and a historically minded thinker of culture; neither dimension is dispensable.
- Hegemony concerns organized direction, institutions and alliances in relation to coercion, not the cleverness of a persuasive message.
- Common sense is an unstable historical mixture containing possible elements of critical good sense.
- A class must acquire capacities and alliances; its position in production cannot create political leadership by itself.
- The intellectual is defined partly by social function, with no automatic moral prestige attached to the role.
- The *Prison Notebooks* are an unfinished, layered and edition-dependent body of work.
- Passive revolution, historical bloc and war of position belong to specific analyses of change and political strategy.
- Gramsci's categories can illuminate how the familiar is made, but each contemporary application requires its own evidence.

### Frequently asked questions

**1. Who was Antonio Gramsci?**  
Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937) was an Italian journalist, Marxist political leader and philosopher. Born in Sardinia, he became active in the Turin workers' movement and helped found the Communist Party of Italy. Imprisoned by the fascist regime in 1926, he wrote the *Prison Notebooks* and an extensive correspondence during detention.

**2. Why is Gramsci important?**  
He showed how political leadership is sustained through economic relations, institutions, culture, education and consent as well as coercion. His analysis offers tools for understanding why people accept an established order and how subordinate groups can organize a different one.

**3. What does hegemony mean for Gramsci?**  
Hegemony refers to a social group's ability to exercise leadership and direction within a wider historical order, building consent through institutions, alliances and cultural practices. It remains related to the possibility of coercion and cannot be reduced to popularity or messaging.

**4. Is hegemony the same thing as propaganda?**  
No. Propaganda is a form of persuasive communication. Hegemony concerns a wider organization of life that gives some ideas, interests and institutions durable authority and makes particular arrangements appear reasonable or normal.

**5. What is common sense in Gramsci's thought?**  
*Senso comune* is the historically layered mixture of beliefs, practical experiences, assumptions and inherited judgments through which people understand ordinary life. It can contain contradictions and should not be equated with a single, coherent popular wisdom.

**6. What is the difference between common sense and good sense?**  
Gramsci identifies within common sense the possibility of *buon senso*: a more critical, coherent understanding that can develop from lived experience. Good sense is a resource for intellectual and political education rather than a guarantee that popular judgment is always correct.

**7. Who are organic intellectuals?**  
They are people who perform intellectual and organizational functions in the formation and direction of social groups. Their roles can include education, technical coordination, administration and political organization. The concept is about social function, not simply formal education or public fame.

**8. What are traditional intellectuals?**  
They are intellectual groups who understand their professional or cultural positions as continuous or autonomous across historical transformations. Gramsci examines how these positions nevertheless have histories and relationships with social power.

**9. What is the integral state?**  
It is an expanded conception of political power joining the apparatuses of government and coercion with institutions of civil society that help organize consent and cultural leadership. Civil and political society interact; neither is a sealed domain.

**10. What is the war of position?**  
Gramsci's term describes political struggle across the dense institutions and social relationships that sustain a modern order. It contrasts with a war of movement, a more direct confrontation with political power. These are historically situated strategic concepts, not general marketing formulas.

**11. What is a historical bloc?**  
A *blocco storico* is the concrete configuration of material relationships, institutions, social groups and prevailing ideas that together sustain a particular historical order. It involves more than a temporary parliamentary coalition.

**12. What is passive revolution?**  
It describes forms of historical change in which ruling groups incorporate or direct reform while limiting the independent political role of subaltern forces. A passive revolution may produce significant change, so the concept must be tested against what actually happens.

**13. What is the philosophy of praxis?**  
Gramsci's term names a historically grounded form of Marxist philosophy that treats thought and activity as connected to material life and social relationships. Its prison context matters, but the expression is more than a code word used to evade censorship.

**14. What does subaltern mean in Gramsci?**  
Subaltern groups occupy subordinate positions whose ability to organize politically and narrate their own histories is constrained. Their experiences can survive in fragmentary forms within records made by ruling institutions. The term has a later, broader life in postcolonial scholarship.

**15. What did Gramsci mean by the modern Prince?**  
Drawing on Machiavelli, he used the image for an organized political body able to form collective will and direction. In his own political setting the revolutionary party was central. The concept also raises questions about internal pluralism and the concentration of leadership.

**16. Why are the *Prison Notebooks* important?**  
Written largely between 1929 and 1935, they contain Gramsci's wide-ranging inquiries into hegemony, language, education, philosophy, state, politics and history. Their multiple notebooks, revisions and later editorial arrangements mean they must be read as a developing intellectual workshop rather than one finished treatise.

**17. What was the Southern Question?**  
It concerned Italy's regional and social inequalities, especially the relations among northern industrial workers, southern peasants, landholding interests and intellectual groups. For Gramsci it was central to the problem of building a broad political alliance and a genuine national-popular life.

**18. Did Gramsci believe culture mattered more than economics?**  
He rejected a simple separation of culture from material relationships. Work, institutions, law, language and political organization shape one another in historically specific ways. An account of hegemony that ignores production and class would misrepresent his thought.

**19. Can Gramsci help us understand digital media and algorithms?**  
His categories can help frame questions about visibility, public language, cultural authority and institutional interests. Digital platforms and algorithms have structures Gramsci did not study. Applying his theory today is a comparative research task, not a claim that he predicted these technologies.

**20. How does Gramsci relate to Freud and Hobbes in the Cerchi triad?**  
Freud investigates the conflicts that complicate a person's account of their own motives. Gramsci examines how shared language and organized culture form consent and political possibilities. Hobbes addresses the authorization of sovereignty amid fear and conflict. Their problems intersect without becoming one doctrine or one historical explanation of obedience.

These are scholarly directions and named reference points; their inclusion is not a claim that every study was read in full when writing this guide.

They are not predictions or claims of direct historical observation by Gramsci. No unattributed English sentence from a modern translation is presented as a primary quotation.

### An editorial closing

Gramsci began among disputes that could be heard in a factory, a newspaper office and a fractured national life. Imprisonment changed the conditions of his work and forced his inquiry through pages that remained unfinished. He kept returning to the problem of how people learn a social order: through their work, their language, their institutions, their memories and the arrangements that make some forms of action easier to imagine than others.

A society can alter its rulers while leaving much of that education untouched. It can also learn to examine what it once took for granted. Gramsci's work belongs to the difficult interval between those possibilities. When a community says that an arrangement is simply the way things are, the question he leaves us is how it came to appear that way—and who has been able to speak while it was becoming so.
