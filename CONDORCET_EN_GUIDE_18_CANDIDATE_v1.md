# Nicolas de Condorcet

*Education, uncertainty, and the fragile reason of collective decisions*

**Circles of Ink · International · Guide 18**  
**Within the triad:** Republic, opinion, decision — Cicero · Alexis de Tocqueville · Nicolas de Condorcet

> **Guiding question:** What must a society give its citizens before it can trust the decisions it asks them to make together?

## A reader's route

The nine movements of the main essay begin in the French Legislative Assembly in April 1792, when Condorcet laid before its deputies an ambitious plan for public instruction. From that scene the argument moves backwards to the mathematics of fallible judgment, then through a disturbing cycle of majority preferences and onwards to those excluded from the rights being proclaimed in the Revolution's name. It follows the troubled passage from proposals for education and constitutional revision to persecution, concealment and an unfinished promise of progress. The last movement asks what becomes of democratic judgment in a world exceptionally capable of collecting votes, preferences and data.

The **Atlas desk** that follows is a reference structure for a different purpose: biography, context, disciplinary circles, works, an original Cerchi instrument, ideas, concepts, constellation, legacy, limits, curiosities, common errors, glossary, reading paths, questions, FAQ, sources and copyright. The Italian guide defines the editorial field; this English work follows its own line of inquiry and is not an abridged translation. The analytical examples are presented openly, with their assumptions, so they can be checked rather than admired at a distance.

## The main essay

### 1. A school for the citizen who must ask permission to understand

On 20 and 21 April 1792, Nicolas de Condorcet presented a plan for the organization of public instruction to France's Legislative Assembly. The Revolution had made the equality of citizens a founding claim of political life. It had removed privileges, declared rights and called into question authorities that had once appeared unassailable. But in a country where access to reading, calculation and sustained schooling remained uneven, the declaration of a right left a practical difficulty unresolved. What could a citizen do with a law he was unable to read, a calculation he could not check or an official explanation whose terms he had never been taught?

Condorcet described the predicament with unusual precision. A person might be told that the law recognized his equality while circumstances deprived him of the knowledge necessary to exercise it. He would remain dependent on those able to interpret the rules, transactions and institutions that ordered his life. The injustice did not end when a legal barrier was removed. It persisted in a relation of unequal understanding, which allowed one person to possess the language of a right while another possessed only its promise.

The report was not an appeal to the vague virtues of enlightenment. Condorcet proposed an institutional structure: primary schools, secondary schools, institutes, lycées and a national society of sciences and arts. Their functions differed. The elementary levels would give citizens knowledge necessary for the conduct of life and the exercise of basic civic duties; advanced study would foster specialist knowledge and the teaching of future generations. He wanted opportunities to continue learning in adult life, because a person forced to work after leaving school could otherwise lose the abilities that made independence possible. Public instruction required locations, teachers, resources and institutional continuity. A general right to education had to become a public capacity.

A further condition was no less demanding. Teaching should be protected, as far as possible, from the partisan interests of the authority that financed it. In the Revolution's name, a government might be tempted to replace inherited religious dogma with an official political creed. Condorcet feared that possibility. The public power could support the acquisition of knowledge without claiming a monopoly over truth or teaching its own opinions as if they were certain. His programme was simultaneously a proposal for wider provision and a limit on the power of those who provided it.

The moment makes Condorcet difficult to classify. A mathematician is drawing the architecture of a school system; a revolutionary legislator is trying to prevent legislation from becoming intellectual command. The citizens to be educated are not merely future workers, or subjects to be made loyal to a new order. They are potential critics of the order itself. The state undertakes the duty of giving them the means to understand it, including the means to disagree.

This is the threshold of Condorcet's politics. Decisions need to be made, and a community cannot wait for everyone to acquire perfect knowledge. Yet the act of voting gains democratic substance from what happens long before a ballot is cast: the ability to learn, compare evidence, resist dependence and form a judgment. Condorcet had spent much of his life asking how uncertain judgments might be combined. In the Assembly he confronted the problem that makes the mathematics political: who has been given a fair chance to judge at all?

### 2. The jury that could become wiser

Condorcet's *Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions* appeared in 1785. Its title sounds forbidding, but one of its central questions is recognizably human. Suppose several people must decide whether a claim is true. Each has considered the available evidence; each may still be mistaken. Can the judgment of the group become more reliable than the judgment of any member?

Imagine three jurors deciding a binary question with a right and wrong answer. Give each, for the sake of a transparent example, a 60 per cent probability of reaching the correct conclusion. Assume their judgments are independent: the fact that one is wrong does not make the others more likely to be wrong. Assume also that their competence is the same, and that the majority decides. The group is correct when all three are correct or when exactly two are correct. The calculation is simple enough to expose its own premises:

\[
P(\text{correct majority})=p^3+3p^2(1-p).
\]

For \(p=0.6\), the result is \(0.648\), or 64.8 per cent. The majority has a better chance of being right than an individual, even though no juror is particularly reliable. With larger odd-numbered groups, the familiar version of the theorem says that this probability increases and, as group size grows, approaches certainty—*provided the assumptions continue to hold*. There is no mathematical warrant for omitting those words.

The attraction is evident. A community need not await an infallible judge. It can assemble fallible persons and, under suitable conditions, make their independent judgments contribute to a more dependable result. Authority is distributed rather than entrusted to a solitary knower. The theorem expresses an Enlightenment hope that the ordinary limits of individual knowledge need not doom common inquiry.

Yet the very assumptions that make the result powerful also mark its limits. If most jurors have less than a fifty-fifty chance of being correct under the relevant model, adding more of them makes the majority less reliable. If all learn the same falsehood from one source, their errors are correlated, and headcount cannot be treated as independent evidence. If a decision concerns rival values or a distribution of burdens rather than a proposition with a discoverable true answer, the model's notion of 'correctness' requires scrutiny before its probabilities can tell us anything useful. The statistical theorem cannot choose the political end to which it should be applied.

Condorcet's original analysis was more intricate than the compact version now taught as the **Condorcet jury theorem**, and later scholars have refined and challenged its premises. The simplified example is worthwhile because it refuses two comforting convictions at once. The public can sometimes reason better together than any one participant; the larger number can also multiply a common error. A hundred repeated judgments drawn from one mistake may count as a hundred votes, while adding little independent information.

A society committed to this lesson must care about what citizens know and how they come to know it. An argument for majority judgment leads unexpectedly to schools, freedom of inquiry and the protection of dissent. Independence is more than an algebraic symbol. It is a political achievement, difficult to sustain where fear, deference, shared misinformation or the monopolization of information govern the way people think.

### 3. Three reasonable voters, an unreasonable circle

A second result from the 1785 *Essay* unsettles a different assumption. Here the question is no longer whether a jury can identify a correct fact. It concerns a group choosing among three alternatives. Each person may have a coherent order of preference, and the group's majority comparisons may nonetheless fail to produce a coherent collective order.

Consider three citizens and three proposals, A, B and C. The first citizen prefers A to B and B to C. The second prefers B to C and C to A. The third prefers C to A and A to B. Every person knows what comes first, second and third. No one has contradicted herself. Compare the proposals in pairs, however, and something unexpected occurs.

| Voter | First choice | Second choice | Third choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | B | C |
| 2 | B | C | A |
| 3 | C | A | B |

A defeats B by two votes to one. B defeats C by the same margin. But C also defeats A by two votes to one. The collective preference forms a circle: A above B, B above C, C above A. The group has no stable ordering that satisfies all three pairwise majority verdicts. The voters are individually consistent; the inconsistency has emerged through aggregation.

This is the **Condorcet paradox**. It reveals a political fact concealed by the apparent simplicity of majority rule. A sequence of votes can help determine which result appears to express the majority. If an assembly chooses between A and B, then asks the winner to face C, it may obtain a different final outcome than if it starts with B against C. The person who sets the agenda can influence the result without altering a single citizen's preferences. Procedure enters the making of the collective will it claims to record.

The paradox should not be exaggerated. Most groups are not trapped in the cycle just described, and there are electoral methods designed to handle pairwise comparisons in different ways. A **Condorcet winner**, where one exists, is an alternative that defeats every other alternative in a head-to-head majority contest. There can be no such winner in a strict three-way cycle. Methods of the Condorcet family and later social choice theory address such difficulties; they differ in how they resolve cycles and how they handle ties. The original insight is a question about the logic of collective preference, not a complete set of rules for every modern election.

Condorcet worked in an intellectual world that included Jean-Charles de Borda, whose voting methods approached the organization of preferences through a different rule. Their contrast illustrates a lasting difficulty. One procedure can privilege positional scores while another privileges pairwise majority victories. Both require arguments about what a fair or informative result should mean. No formula escapes the need to choose the values embodied in the formula.

A political decision requires an end to deliberation, but the existence of an agreed ending does not erase the assumptions that brought the group there. The paradox gives democratic citizens a reason to examine who defines the alternatives, what information is omitted, how the ballot is framed and what changes if an excluded option is introduced. The dignity of counting equal votes remains. What has disappeared is the illusion that counting, by itself, gives a collective choice the coherence of an individual mind.

### 4. The woman asked to accept a right she cannot exercise

In 1790 Condorcet published *On the Admission of Women to the Rights of Citizenship*. The French Revolution had declared rights in universal language while political participation remained, in decisive respects, the privilege of men. Condorcet approached the exclusion by asking whether the principle that justified equal rights could survive its own restrictions. If persons possess rights because they can think, experience moral concerns and reason, what property of women makes them an exception?

His argument exposed a particular circularity. Women had been denied access to many forms of education, public activity and responsibility. Their lack of experience in those fields was then offered as evidence that they should remain excluded. The deprivation supplied the proof for its own continuation. To understand an unequal society by taking its observed inequalities as immutable facts of nature was, in Condorcet's judgment, to mistake the outcome of institutions for a legitimate ground of rights.

The implications ran through citizenship. Women were affected by laws, taxation, property relations and public decisions. They could be required to obey political rules in whose making they were refused a formal voice. The inconsistency was not a marginal imperfection to be corrected after the successful establishment of men's freedom. It struck the claim that the new political order rested on rights belonging to human beings as such.

Condorcet was not alone in this developing struggle. Olympe de Gouges would issue the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen* in 1791. Mary Wollstonecraft argued for women's rights and education in 1792. Those writers deserve independent histories; neither should be treated as a decorative confirmation of Condorcet's foresight. The philosophical and political field was being contested from more than one position, and the principles at stake acquired force through disagreement.

Sophie de Grouchy, whom Condorcet married in 1786, belonged to this intellectual world as a philosopher and translator in her own right. Her reflections on sympathy and moral relations cannot be reduced to her husband's programme. Their collaboration and shared commitments formed part of Condorcet's environment, while her later writings and editorial work require recognition under her own name. The history of political ideas becomes poorer whenever a woman's intellectual labor is recorded only as the influence she exercised upon a better-known man.

The women's-rights argument reconnects with the 1792 educational project. Equal access to knowledge is a way of making political rights usable; access itself must not be made conditional on the achievement of a competence unavailable to those historically denied instruction. Equal standing establishes the claim to a right. Education can then give people greater independence in exercising it. Confusing those two functions would turn an emancipatory programme into a new examination administered by a privileged minority.

The distinction also exposes a limit of Condorcet's own mathematical model. The competence of a voter can be represented by a parameter in an abstract theorem. The conditions through which competence is acquired are historical. A polity that neglects half its members, then praises the wisdom of those permitted to vote, has shaped its evidence before it begins to count. In 1790, Condorcet asked the Revolution to recognize this fact within its most elementary claim to equality.

### 5. The freedom that the enslaved were told to await

A decade before the great debates over revolutionary citizenship, Condorcet had turned his attention to slavery. In 1781 he published *Reflections on Negro Slavery* under the pseudonym Joachim Schwartz, presented as a foreign religious voice. The work condemned the reduction of human beings to property as a violation of natural right. Commerce, plantation profit and imperial convenience could not make the ownership of persons just. The argument demanded that Europe subject its own sources of wealth to the principles it was beginning to proclaim.

The programme contains a compromise that should disturb a reader of the rights argument. Condorcet acknowledged immediate emancipation as the strict demand of justice, yet proposed a graduated path as a matter of political practice. His reasoning included the paternalistic assumption that people injured by enslavement might need preparation before the full exercise of freedom. Recent scholarship has disputed how to weigh the force of his moral condemnation against the delay and hierarchy of the measures he recommended. Both belong to the historical record. The wrong he identified was absolute; the remedy he proposed was made conditional on judgments held by the society that had committed it.

The gap matters because it cannot be dismissed merely as an eighteenth-century preference for caution. A person's liberty becomes something managed by those who possess the power to defer it. The former slave is acknowledged as the bearer of a right yet treated as the object of an administrative timetable. The universal principle survives in language while its application waits on other people's assessment of readiness. This contradiction is inseparable from the history of European abolitionism and from the colonial assumptions that survived within some of its most articulate critics.

Condorcet participated in the Société des Amis des Noirs, founded in 1788, which fought the slave trade and campaigned for changes in French colonial policy. The organization did not speak for all enslaved or colonized people; the struggle also depended on resistance by people in the colonies, who were agents of their own freedom. Treating an enlightened European campaign as the sole origin of emancipation would repeat the hierarchy that a serious history ought to question.

A further limitation appears in Condorcet's philosophy of historical progress. His hopes for improved knowledge, law and conditions of life could be accompanied by an assumption that European forms of civilization represented the measure by which other societies should be judged. The desire to emancipate becomes suspect when those claiming to know the future believe themselves entitled to direct the present of others.

The same thinker who exposed the error of excluding women because of an inequality produced by institutions did not consistently escape the temptation to make another group's actual liberty depend upon a judgment of preparedness. The contradiction does not erase his abolitionism. It forces the reader to ask how a universal right can be put into effect without reproducing the authority of those who once denied it. Mathematical equality, political equality and freedom from domination require more than the removal of an item from the law. They require the transfer of standing and power to the people concerned.

### 6. An institution designed to be contradicted

Condorcet's essays on instruction from 1791 and the legislative report of April 1792 give the project of enlightenment its public architecture. Children of different social origins should have access to basic teaching; higher learning should remain open through further opportunities; adults should be able to renew knowledge after the school years. The point was to equip a country with a means of learning that could outlive any minister, party or particular doctrine.

He distinguished **instruction** from the comprehensive shaping of belief or character by political authority. The state had duties towards knowledge, because ignorance could deprive citizens of the effective exercise of their rights. But official power must not claim the right to impose a contested opinion as demonstrated truth. Schools ought to be protected against immediate governmental interests, while remaining publicly accountable for their use of resources. The balance was hard to design: the institutions require public support, yet their intellectual credibility depends on a freedom that their sponsor may find inconvenient.

A child learning arithmetic receives more than a technique for calculating a wage or an account. She gains a means of noticing when figures offered by another are false. A citizen who can read a law and follow an argument is better able to judge the authority asking for obedience. Those abilities do not create an expert in everything. They diminish the distance between those who possess the language of institutions and those who must live under their decisions.

This is why adult learning deserves more than a parenthetical mention. Laws change; professions change; new knowledge renders an old explanation unreliable. Someone whose opportunity to learn ends when paid work begins can lose practical independence while retaining formal rights. Condorcet envisaged public ways of acquiring and refreshing knowledge throughout life. In an age of rapidly changing institutions this seems ordinary; in the political economy of late eighteenth-century France, it belonged to a radical argument about the distribution of intellectual power.

There was a further institutional tension. Condorcet entrusted considerable importance to scientific bodies, teaching expertise and the public authority of demonstrable knowledge. Their independence from partisan command was an emancipatory ambition. But the institutional guardians of knowledge can acquire their own forms of hierarchy and self-protection. Who decides which subjects deserve instruction? Whose experience counts as relevant knowledge? How can a citizen challenge a school or academy without replacing evidence with mere suspicion of expertise? The proposal raises these questions even where its author does not supply final answers.

In Cerchi's contemporary reading, the most fertile part of Condorcet's programme is that a public institution should be able to educate someone who may later refute it. A government that teaches the laws ought to equip citizens to challenge those laws. A university that teaches established knowledge ought to supply methods for discovering that something it has taught is mistaken. That principle extends Condorcet's argument into a present-day language of institutional corrigibility; it should not be passed off as a quotation from his texts.

A society may invest heavily in the machinery of decision and scarcely invest in the capacity to examine what is decided. Condorcet's school reverses the order of importance. It prepares the person before appealing to the number; it cultivates judgment while acknowledging that no citizen or institution will become infallible. The educational task is unfinished by design, because every generation will inherit both knowledge and errors from the one before it.

### 7. The constitution that could not protect its drafter

The Revolution transformed Condorcet's intellectual questions into immediate political choices. He had been a mathematician and a public intellectual associated with reform, economic debate and the institutions of the Republic of Letters. By 1791 he was a deputy in the Legislative Assembly; in 1792 he entered the National Convention. In the midst of war, internal unrest and fierce factional competition, the forms of political decision became matters of survival.

Condorcet participated in preparing a constitutional plan presented in February 1793. It gave a large place to popular participation, representation, deliberation and mechanisms through which public decisions could be revised. His mathematical interest in voting procedures had never existed in a separate universe from this practical question: how might a sovereign people act through institutions without allowing a particular assembly, committee or faction to become identical with the people itself?

The pressure was extraordinary. The fall of the monarchy and the trial of Louis XVI had revealed an irreconcilable conflict over the future of the state. Condorcet opposed capital punishment and took a position against the execution of the former king, while accepting that monarchical power had lost its claim to rule. War and fear of counter-revolution intensified rivalries within the Convention. The broad division between Girondin and Montagnard positions captures part of this struggle, though Condorcet's intellectual and political position should not be compressed into an uncomplicated party identity.

After the defeat of the Girondin leadership and the development of a rival constitution, Condorcet criticized the new constitutional settlement. His opposition became dangerous in a political environment where dissent could be represented as hostility to the Revolution itself. A warrant for his arrest forced him into hiding. The man who had examined the conditions of a reasoned collective decision now faced a public authority increasingly unwilling to tolerate the independent judgment of one of its own legislators.

There is a terrible irony here, but the irony should not be allowed to substitute for history. Condorcet was not simply the innocent representative of pure reason destroyed by an irrational crowd. He had taken part in a revolution, advocated institutional changes and participated in disputes whose consequences affected many others. His proposals were contested; he had political allegiances and sometimes insufficient answers to the social emergencies around him. Still, an institutional system in which disagreement over constitutional rules is treated as an offence against the people has crossed a decisive boundary. A majority's legal power to choose cannot justify the elimination of all legitimate opposition to its choice.

Cicero, who defended the Roman republic while participating in extraordinary measures against alleged enemies, provides a distant and revealing tension. Condorcet, more explicitly committed to expanding political equality, experienced another form of emergency: the revolutionary authority that claimed to represent the people could no longer accommodate a dissenter within the people. The cases are separated by almost two millennia and by different conceptions of citizenship. Their comparison is useful only where the historical differences remain intact.

For Condorcet, the lesson is inseparable from the mathematical one. If even a legitimate majority can err, constitutional liberty must preserve a place for the person who challenges an error. A procedure that allows the decision but not its criticism has mistaken the settlement of a question for the extinguishing of thought.

### 8. A history of progress written in concealment

While in hiding during 1793–94, Condorcet composed much of the *Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind*, published posthumously in 1795. It follows the history of human knowledge and social development through successive epochs, including the growth of writing, science, public communication and ideas of rights. Its final horizon looks beyond the present towards the reduction of inequalities among nations and within societies, and towards further improvements in human capacities and conditions of life.

The circumstances of its composition resist a complacent reading. Condorcet was writing about human improvement while the revolution to which he had devoted his political intelligence was hunting him. The work did not emerge from the security of a victorious reformer. Its confidence was composed against immediate defeat. He wanted to show how knowledge could accumulate across generations and how the institutions that preserved and circulated it might allow people to recognize and correct injustices that had once been accepted as necessary.

**Perfectibility** is the operative notion. Human beings can learn; knowledge and techniques can be transmitted; institutions can improve. Condorcet imagined that advances in science and medicine, education and social organization might reduce avoidable suffering. The possibilities were expansive, but the route was never purely mechanical. Science could be suppressed, books censored, education denied, prejudice protected, freedom postponed. His own fate supplied a counterexample to any belief that intellectual advance automatically produces political restraint.

The *Sketch* stakes its hope on the conditions of progress: preserving knowledge, allowing criticism and changing institutions so that learning can alter lives. Its confidence also deserves criticism. Its organization of history can suggest a single ascending scale of civilizations, with European achievements treated as a privileged measure. Differences among societies risk being interpreted as stages on one predetermined road. This danger is connected to the paternalism visible in parts of his abolitionist programme. Hope for universal emancipation does not guarantee that the people being described are being heard as equal authors of their futures.

Condorcet left his place of refuge, was arrested in March 1794 and died in custody in Bourg-la-Reine, then called Bourg-Égalité. Accounts disagree over the precise mechanism of his death, and the surviving evidence does not establish a secure cause. The persistent story of poison concealed in a ring should not be turned into a definitive final scene. A sober account must leave that uncertainty visible.

Sophie de Grouchy played a major role in the subsequent preservation and circulation of Condorcet's writings, and she continued her own intellectual work. The *Sketch* entered the history of political philosophy as a testament to improvement written amid repression. Its later influence did not make its author the prophet of an inevitable enlightened future. It preserved a more fragile proposition: the accumulated work of human minds can survive a political disaster and make new criticism possible, provided later generations have both access to it and the freedom to question it.

### 9. The count that arrives before the question

Imagine a public consultation on a city's transport plan. A digital platform presents three proposals. Residents register their preferences, the system produces a ranking and a dashboard displays the winner. The process is transparent about how many votes were counted and how quickly results were processed. The organizers can produce impressive figures for participation. Yet citizens may never have been able to ask whether three proposals were enough, who chose them, how the costs were distributed or what information the ranking left outside its columns.

This example belongs entirely to our present. Condorcet neither predicted digital platforms nor supplied a ready-made theory of contemporary opinion systems. His investigations help us identify the questions that the dashboard can conceal. Is the decision about a fact that may be judged correct or incorrect, or about incompatible public ends? What independent evidence have participants received? Are preferences being gathered after adequate time for discussion? Does the aggregation rule produce a stable choice, and can the agenda change its result? Were people affected by the decision permitted to take part?

A highly accurate count may coexist with a poor deliberative process. Its mathematics can be impeccable while the initial conditions are unjust. The problem is not resolved by assigning all decisions to experts: experts, too, work within institutions, assumptions and interests. Their evidence must be accessible enough for criticism; their specialist methods must be explained when they become grounds for public authority. Condorcet's project was directed toward a society in which more people could judge, not one in which the most learned few would be spared the inconvenience of accountability.

The contemporary wound Cerchi calls **the decision without judgment** arises when a community equips itself to record a choice more thoroughly than it equips its members to form, understand and contest one. Some technologies can help remedy the wound. Open records, accessible instruction and more transparent methods of aggregation may distribute knowledge and reveal procedural bias. Other arrangements can concentrate information, reward imitation and transform a single source's mistake into the apparent agreement of thousands. Which effect prevails is an empirical and institutional question.

The triad now comes into focus without collapsing its authors into one voice. Cicero examined the laws, duties and civic speech through which something might remain common in a threatened republic. Tocqueville studied the habits of association and local responsibility that give citizens an experience of governing. Condorcet asks how a decision can be made through fallible judgments and what education, inclusion and institutional revision are required to improve its conditions. Each speaks from a different political order; their tension is more illuminating than any claim that they jointly invented contemporary democracy.

The April 1792 report and the 1785 probability treatise belong to the same intellectual life. The mathematician who discovered how majorities can fail asks the legislator to provide schools for those who will have to live under majority decisions. He understands that no rule will eliminate uncertainty. His response is to make the resources of judgment more widely available and the grounds of collective authority more open to correction.

When the votes have been counted, the task has not ended. One should still be able to ask whether the question was fairly posed, whether every person was admitted as an equal, whether the evidence could withstand criticism and whether the outcome may be revised without treating dissent as treason. Condorcet's legacy resides in that demanding interval between a valid count and a decision worthy of a free community.

## The Atlas desk

### Nicolas de Condorcet at a glance

**Name and dates.** Marie-Jean-Antoine-Nicolas de Caritat, marquis de Condorcet, born 17 September 1743 in Ribemont, Picardy; died in March 1794 in custody at Bourg-la-Reine, then Bourg-Égalité. The exact mechanism of death remains uncertain.

**Intellectual position.** Mathematician, philosopher, historian of science, public intellectual, institutional reformer and revolutionary politician. An important figure in early social mathematics, the study of voting and probability, arguments for public instruction, women's political rights and abolition, and the philosophy of human perfectibility.

**Historical world.** The late French Enlightenment and the crisis of the Bourbon monarchy; the Republic of Letters and scientific academies; reformist political economy; the French Revolution, its constitutional disputes and the Terror.

**The central problem.** How can citizens make collective decisions responsibly when their judgments are fallible, their resources of knowledge unequal and their procedures capable of shaping the result they supposedly merely record?

**Primary works to meet first.** The 1785 *Essai* on probability and voting; the 1791 *Cinq mémoires* on instruction; the April 1792 legislative report on education; the 1790 essay on women's citizenship; the 1781 abolitionist reflections; and the posthumously published *Esquisse* of human progress.

**A caution for readers.** The same thinker who enlarged the moral boundaries of equal citizenship accepted paternalistic delay in his plan for ending slavery. His voting analysis depends on specified assumptions. His philosophy of progress is a historical commitment, not a demonstrated law of inevitable improvement.

### Why Condorcet matters

Condorcet links problems usually taught in separate departments. A mathematician investigating the aggregation of judgments must consider what competence and independence mean. A legislator committed to equal rights asks who has access to the knowledge needed to exercise them. An advocate of public education examines the conditions under which political authority can be questioned. An abolitionist who proclaims universal right must confront the contradiction between emancipation and control over its timetable.

His importance to political philosophy begins with a challenge to any easy identification of majority rule with collective wisdom. Votes are essential instruments of political equality; they are not magic operations that transform all forms of uncertainty into truth. His importance to educational thought lies in giving public instruction an explicitly emancipatory function: citizens need capacities that let them judge laws, claims and evidence for themselves. His importance to the history of rights appears where he insists that women and enslaved persons cannot be excluded from a principle claimed to be universal.

His errors matter too. Where he assumed an unequal society could decide when the enslaved were ready for freedom, the difficulty of substituting paternal judgment for equality became visible inside his own work. The guide therefore reads intellectual power and intellectual limitation together.

### The human and political problem

Every person needs to decide without certainty. Societies compound the difficulty: they gather people whose evidence, interests, education and opportunities differ, then need a result capable of binding them. A majority can decide what to do while remaining mistaken about the facts. Well-formed individual preferences can generate inconsistent collective comparisons. Decisions must nonetheless take place, and public authority cannot be suspended until agreement becomes perfect.

Condorcet's answer is to improve the **conditions of judgment**: open access to knowledge, independence of inquiry, rights that include those previously excluded, methods that reveal their assumptions and institutions able to revise their own work. His life shows the fragility of that programme. A government born in the name of equal rights could persecute someone who criticized its constitutional decisions. The problem is therefore not only epistemic. It concerns the political protection that allows a person to say an authorized decision may be wrong.

### Life, context and the turning points of the work

**1743–1765: A talent formed within the old order.** Born in a noble family at Ribemont, Condorcet was educated in institutions of the ancien régime and developed an early aptitude for mathematics. His work on integral calculus brought him to the attention of d'Alembert and the French scientific world. The coexistence of inherited privilege and intellectual ambition is important: a critic of unequal civic status emerged from the social order he sought to reform.

**1769–1770s: The institutions of science.** Election to the Académie royale des sciences and later service as its permanent secretary put him among researchers, correspondents, patrons and administrators of learning. Scientific knowledge appeared as a collective enterprise maintained through institutions and public argument. His work in this world helps explain why a reformed education system would later occupy so central a place in his politics.

**Turgot and reform.** Condorcet's friendship with Anne Robert Jacques Turgot linked mathematical and philosophical inquiry to economic and governmental reform. Condorcet saw how proposals grounded in argument encounter interests and established privileges. His biographical work on Turgot records both admiration and a view of reform as something exposed to political resistance. Turgot's own ideas and limits are not identical with Condorcet's.

**1781–1788: Rights under pressure.** In 1781 the abolitionist reflections appeared under the name Joachim Schwartz (also described in scholarship as 'Monsieur Schwartz'). The 1785 *Essay* applied probability to collective decisions. In 1786 Condorcet married Sophie de Grouchy, his intellectual partner and a philosopher with her own later work. The Société des Amis des Noirs, founded in 1788, gave abolitionist argument an organizational form.

**1789–1792: Principles become institutional questions.** With the Revolution, declarations and constitutional debate raised the stakes of earlier philosophical work. Condorcet defended the political inclusion of women in 1790, wrote on public instruction in 1791 and presented a systematic plan to the Legislative Assembly in April 1792. His mathematical inquiries and rights arguments converged on a concrete demand: an equal citizen must have means of independent judgment.

**1792–1793: Revolution and constitutional conflict.** Condorcet became a member of the National Convention, opposed the death penalty and contributed to a constitutional project presented in February 1793. The intensification of revolutionary conflict made constitutional disagreement dangerous. His criticisms of the succeeding constitutional direction helped bring persecution upon him.

**1793–1794: Concealment and a future history.** In hiding, Condorcet composed the *Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind*. He was arrested in March 1794 and died in custody in Bourg-la-Reine. The often repeated poison-ring story cannot be treated as established fact. The *Sketch* appeared in 1795; subsequent editorial work, including that of Sophie de Grouchy, shaped its transmission.

**The age and its contradictions.** Mathematical and scientific inquiry expanded rapidly, while literacy and political participation remained uneven. European powers presented themselves as bearers of civilization while benefiting from colonial slavery. France moved from monarchy to experiments in constitutional and republican government amid financial crisis, war, factional violence and new forms of public mobilisation. Condorcet's beliefs about progress were formed in the presence of these conflicts, though he did not resolve them.

### The disciplinary circles

**Mathematics.** Analysis, calculation and demonstration taught Condorcet to identify premises, draw consequences and expose errors for inspection. Mathematical form matters because results are conditional on definitions and assumptions.

**Probability.** Judgments about uncertain propositions may be modeled through chances of correctness. In the jury theorem, independence and competence are substantive prerequisites, not dispensable technical details.

**Political philosophy.** Legitimate collective authority must be reconciled with human fallibility, legal equality, rights, dissent and the capacity to reconsider institutions. The relation between decision and justification is central.

**Social choice and voting theory.** Majority aggregation can produce cycles even when individual preferences are coherent. Different electoral procedures embody different understandings of fair collective choice.

**Education and lifelong learning.** Public instruction should enable individuals to understand laws, assess claims and develop capacities. Learning must extend beyond childhood, since changing knowledge and difficult working lives expose citizens to dependence.

**Constitutional design.** Representation, participation, institutional checks and revision mechanisms shape how the people can exercise authority without being reduced to the will of one assembly or faction.

**History and the philosophy of progress.** The transmission of knowledge and rights across generations creates the possibility of improvement. The account remains open to criticism where it places cultures on a single ascending scale.

**Economics and administration.** Condorcet's work intersects with eighteenth-century political economy, Turgot's reforms, calculation, taxation, public welfare and the relation between policy expertise and political responsibility.

**Women's rights and the history of citizenship.** His argument for equal political rights attacks the circular use of educational exclusion as evidence for further exclusion. It must be read alongside other voices in the struggle for women's autonomy.

**Abolition and colonial history.** His condemnation of slavery is part of the history of universal rights; his gradualist programme exposes the persistence of European paternal authority within an emancipatory cause.

**The contemporary study of institutions.** A Cerchi application: information infrastructures, public consultations, algorithmic aggregation, access to evidence and the procedures by which authorized choices can be contested. These topics require modern evidence and were outside Condorcet's historical experience.

### The works: a map for reading

**Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions (*Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix*, 1785).** The principal mathematical and political investigation of the reliability of collective judgments and the aggregation of preferences. The familiar simplified jury theorem and the majority-cycle paradox are two legacies of a broader and technically demanding work. A reader should keep binary truth judgments and multi-option preference rankings distinct.

**Reflections on Negro Slavery (*Réflexions sur l'esclavage des nègres*, 1781; revised reissue in 1788).** Published under a pseudonym, an explicit attack on slavery as a violation of natural rights. It also proposes gradualist measures that require scrutiny for their paternalism and assumptions about emancipation.

**On the Admission of Women to the Rights of Citizenship (*Sur l'admission des femmes au droit de cité*, 1790).** A concise argument that the universality of rights cannot coexist coherently with women's formal political exclusion. Particularly important for understanding the link between institutions that produce inequality and claims about allegedly natural incapacity.

**Five Memoirs on Public Instruction (*Cinq mémoires sur l'instruction publique*, 1791).** A programme connecting access to knowledge, freedom of conscience, intellectual independence, civic ability and continued education. It distinguishes public provision from the state's claim to impose one set of beliefs.

**Report and Draft Decree on the General Organization of Public Instruction (*Rapport et projet de décret sur l'organisation générale de l'instruction publique*, 1792).** Condorcet's concrete institutional proposal presented to the Legislative Assembly on 20–21 April 1792. The plan comprises five levels or types of institution, from primary education to the organization of scientific and artistic knowledge. Proposal must not be confused with a fully enacted system.

**Constitutional writings and the draft constitution of 1793.** Documents concerning representation, civic sovereignty, decision procedures, political rights and revision. Condorcet's participation in the constitutional commission shows that the mathematics of decision was connected to the design of public institutions.

**Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind (*Esquisse d'un tableau historique des progrès de l'esprit humain*, written 1793–94; published 1795).** A compressed history of knowledge, social development and the possibility of human improvement, including a future-oriented final section. The circumstances of composition in concealment make its confidence more difficult and more moving than a routine celebration of Enlightenment progress.

**Life of Turgot (*Vie de Monsieur Turgot*) and Life of Voltaire (*Vie de Voltaire*).** Biographical and intellectual studies that reveal Condorcet's understanding of reform, public reason, intolerance and the transmission of ideas through persons and institutions.

**Academic eulogies, mathematical memoirs, political writings and correspondence.** A large and varied body of work, often tied to specific scientific problems and political events. The corpus is not a single completed philosophical treatise; it requires attention to genres, occasions and editorial history.

### A Cerchi reading instrument: before the count

A public authority wants to know what a community thinks, chooses or knows. It arranges a vote, survey, consultation, citizens' panel or automated classification. The resulting number looks conclusive. Cerchi proposes a prior examination of the conditions that allow that number to mean what the authority says it means. This is an **original contemporary instrument**, informed by Condorcet but not presented as an historical Condorcet procedure.

**The question.** What exactly is being decided? Is there a factual proposition with a discoverable correct answer, or a political choice among competing values and burdens? The answer changes what competence and correctness can mean. An environmental measurement, a choice about spending and a vote on moral priorities are different problems, even if each produces an integer at the end.

**The participants.** Who is affected, who is admitted, and who is absent? Are rights being restricted by criteria that the institution itself has helped make unequal? The inquiry begins before competence is measured: a right cannot be made conditional on standards designed around an excluded class.

**The knowledge.** What evidence is available to each person, in what language and at what cost? Can the sources and methods be questioned? A count of decisions formed from inaccessible or systematically distorted information cannot claim to embody well-informed participation simply because every eligible person received a ballot.

**The independence.** Are judgments formed through sufficiently diverse evidence and deliberative routes, or does one uncorrected source shape them all? The jury theorem's independence condition suggests a question; it does not supply an empirical answer for a modern information environment.

**The procedure.** Who chose the alternatives, the order of voting, the thresholds and the treatment of ties? Would a change of agenda produce a different result? Record the rule before announcing that the outcome is the expression of a unified public will.

**The possibility of correction.** Can the result, its factual premises and the governing rules be challenged through a known route, without treating dissent as disloyalty? A binding decision needs closure; a free institution also needs legitimate means of revision.

**A worked case: a municipal school closure.** The council presents two sites for consolidation and asks residents to choose. Before the vote, the instrument asks why closure is assumed, whether transport and disability impacts are known, whether families in remote areas can participate, who verified the enrollment forecast and whether a third proposal was excluded. A majority choice might still be justified. Its legitimacy would rest on a fuller process than the mere fact that the winner received the larger number.

**The limit.** This is a framework for criticism, not a claim that every public decision can be made through perfect knowledge or universal agreement. Institutions face time, expense and conflicting claims. Better procedures must remain feasible; the responsibility for selecting among reasonable trade-offs cannot disappear into a form. A specialist's knowledge deserves attention without giving specialists an exclusive right to decide the values of everyone else.

### Key ideas

1. **The right to judge depends on more than legal permission.** Unequal access to knowledge can make nominally equal citizens dependent on the explanations of others.
2. **Judgment is fallible and sometimes improvable collectively.** Under specified conditions, independent, better-than-random votes on a binary fact can produce a majority more reliable than an individual.
3. **The assumptions of aggregation are political questions.** Sources of knowledge, independence, agenda and competence cannot simply be assumed away.
4. **The majority may lack a coherent ranking.** Consistent individual preferences can produce pairwise collective cycles.
5. **Different decision rules can produce different legitimate-looking outcomes.** The choice of procedure is itself consequential and must be justified.
6. **Education distributes practical independence.** Reading, mathematics, knowledge of institutions and opportunities for adult learning change the relation between a citizen and authority.
7. **Public knowledge must remain capable of criticism.** Education financed by the state can become indoctrination if public power dictates contested opinions as truths.
8. **Universal rights have to reach those whom institutions exclude.** Condorcet defended women's citizenship and opposed slavery, even while his own abolitionist gradualism compromised that universal principle.
9. **Progress is an institutional possibility.** It depends on the preservation, criticism and transmission of knowledge; historical catastrophe can interrupt it.
10. **A revolution can persecute the critic of its procedures.** Protecting dissent is part of the means by which a collective decision remains answerable to reason.
11. **A present-day count is not a verdict on its own conditions.** This is Cerchi's application of Condorcet to contemporary public decisions, not a prediction made by Condorcet himself.

### Key concepts

**Probability.** A mathematical treatment of uncertainty, requiring a specified model of what may occur and what information is available.

**Collective judgment.** A decision formed by combining the individual judgments of a group, possibly about a proposition with an objectively correct answer.

**Competence.** In the simplest jury-theorem model, the probability that an individual voter gives the correct answer to a binary question. It is not a measure of the person's equal civic worth.

**Independence.** In the probability model, a condition under which one person's correctness is probabilistically independent of the others'. Real group judgments often have correlated informational sources.

**Majority rule.** A procedure that gives effect to an alternative supported by more than half of the relevant votes under specified conditions.

**Plurality.** An electoral procedure in which the option with the most votes prevails, even when it does not command an absolute majority; the word may carry different historical usages.

**Condorcet jury theorem.** The now-standard name for a result about the reliability of majority judgment when a binary question has a correct answer and voters meet sufficiently strong competence and independence assumptions.

**Condorcet paradox.** The possibility of a cycle in pairwise majority preferences even when every individual voter's ranking is transitive.

**Transitivity.** The coherence condition that if A is preferred to B and B to C, A is preferred to C.

**Pairwise comparison.** The examination of alternatives two at a time, often under majority rule.

**Condorcet winner.** An alternative that defeats every other alternative in pairwise majority contests, under the usual strict-winner definition; such a winner need not exist.

**Condorcet method.** A family of election methods using pairwise comparisons; different later methods resolve cycles in different ways, so the label alone does not identify a unique complete voting rule.

**Borda count.** An alternative voting rule assigning points to positions in ranked preferences; associated with Jean-Charles de Borda, not Condorcet.

**Agenda dependence.** The possibility that the order or framing of collective choices changes which outcome prevails.

**Social choice.** The study of how individual judgments or preferences can be aggregated into collective choices, including the limits of proposed aggregation rules.

**Epistemic value of a vote.** Its relation to the correctness or quality of judgment about a question, distinct from the vote's expression of equal political standing.

**Public instruction.** Institutions that develop and distribute capacities for learning and civic independence, extending beyond elementary literacy and beyond childhood.

**Freedom of inquiry.** The ability to investigate and criticize claims without political authority dictating what must be accepted as true.

**Perfectibility.** The capacity of human beings and institutions to improve through knowledge, reflection, social reform and the transmission of acquired abilities.

**Political equality.** Equal civic standing and rights, which can remain practically weakened by unequal access to information and opportunities to exercise them.

**Universal rights.** Claims grounded in the standing of human beings as such; their consistency is tested by whether women, colonized people and enslaved persons are treated as genuine holders.

**Abolitionism.** Opposition to slavery and, in Condorcet's historical case, proposals for its elimination; the timeline and terms of emancipation are morally and politically significant.

**Gradualism.** A staged approach to change, which in the abolitionist context may delay recognition of a right already affirmed as unconditional.

**Constitutional revision.** The institutional possibility of modifying political rules rather than treating an existing majority's victory as permanently sufficient.

### The constellation

**Before Condorcet — the mathematics and institutions of the Enlightenment.** Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat opened important lines of reasoning about uncertainty; Jacob Bernoulli and other early probability thinkers developed tools for reasoning under repeated trials and incomplete knowledge. Montesquieu investigated the structure and limitation of political power, while Rousseau's reflections on sovereignty and the general will posed questions that Condorcet would approach differently through representation and decision procedures. Voltaire's struggles against intolerance, d'Alembert's scientific and philosophical work and the encyclopedic project shaped a milieu in which learning could be treated as a public force. Turgot linked economic reform with the practical resistance of entrenched interests.

**Interlocutors and opponents — reason inside an unequal society.** Jean-Charles de Borda offered voting procedures that raised a competing way of aggregating preferences. Sophie de Grouchy, Condorcet's intellectual partner and an independent philosopher, wrote on sympathy and moral relations. Olympe de Gouges pursued a distinct and radical statement of women's rights; the developing revolutionary assemblies tested claims of equality through their omissions as much as their declarations. Advocates of colonial property and the slave trade resisted abolition. The Jacobin and Montagnard leadership became part of the political world in which disagreement over a constitution could lead to persecution. These conflicts were not a single debate among intellectual friends; they touched people who had been denied equal entry to the debate itself.

**After Condorcet — election theory, political equality and educational reform.** Later mathematicians and political scientists investigated probability, representation and the aggregation of preferences. Duncan Black's work on committees and elections, Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem and Amartya Sen's investigations of collective choice belong to a later, conceptually transformed history. Arrow did not simply restate Condorcet, and the different results should not be folded into a single pessimistic theorem. Historians and theorists of education have returned to Condorcet's argument for public instruction as civic emancipation. Women's political rights and abolition acquired force through wider struggles, for which Condorcet was one contributor among many. His legacy reaches beyond direct influence into problems that subsequent thinkers have made more exact.

**Contemporary phenomena — tests rather than predictions.** Participatory budgeting, citizens' assemblies, electoral design, public schooling, adult education, statistical literacy, algorithmic recommendation, digital consultation and contested public expertise can all be examined using questions Condorcet helped formulate. The analogies vary in strength. A binary panel assessing a factual claim belongs closer to the jury theorem than an election among conflicting ethical programmes. A platform ranking preferences may exhibit agenda effects, but only analysis of its actual procedure can establish them. Condorcet is useful where he makes hidden conditions visible, not where his name is used as a decorative historical predecessor for every decision technology.

### Legacy: the institutions of corrigible judgment

Condorcet left two related lines of inheritance. One belongs to the mathematics of collective decisions: a procedure may increase reliability under some conditions and generate inconsistency under others. The problem survives in jury research, electoral design and modern social choice theory. The second belongs to the politics of knowledge: rights require institutions that make them exercisable, and intellectual authority should remain open to examination. These lines intersect wherever citizens are asked to trust a collective decision.

His concern with public instruction continues to matter because it refuses to separate the formation of a judgment from the social circumstances of the person who makes it. A nominal right to vote is compatible with serious inequalities in time, schooling, language and access to evidence. Modern political systems have improved on Condorcet's own account in many ways, but his fundamental question remains demanding: who supplies the means by which equal citizens can understand an exercise of power?

The legacy also has boundaries. Condorcet's mathematical models cannot settle disagreements about values; his universalism was compromised by paternalism and by assumptions about civilizational hierarchy. His historical confidence in progress must be subjected to the evidence of political reversals, colonial violence and continuing inequalities. What passes usefully into the present is a method of exposing conditions and revising errors, together with a history that shows why those capacities need political protection.

### The contemporary wound: the decision without judgment

A public consultation can record preferences with extreme precision while the participants' capacity to understand the alternatives remains unequal. The count may be correct and still arrive at the end of a process whose questions, evidence and access have been shaped by powers that the participants cannot examine. Condorcet's work makes this mismatch legible by putting the quality of judgment and the design of voting rules into the same investigation.

The wound is visible whenever participation is treated as a sufficient substitute for public knowledge, when statistical expertise is used to close a dispute over values, or when a correct procedure is mistaken for a guarantee of a wise decision. It can also appear when an excluded group is told it will acquire equal rights only after institutions have judged it ready. All these failures involve the separation of political equality from the means of exercising it.

The remedy is institutional and revisable: wider access to trustworthy instruction; explanations that citizens can question; genuinely open alternatives where possible; procedures that disclose how outcomes are aggregated; and lawful means to correct a decision without converting dissent into disloyalty. This contemporary formulation is a Cerchi reading, not a claim that Condorcet described our information systems in advance.

### Limits and contradictions

**The theorem's demanding assumptions.** A simplified jury result relies on a binary truth question, sufficiently competent judgments and independence. Shared information sources, correlated errors and strategic behaviour can make its conclusions unreliable as descriptions of actual political participation. Newer theorems explore weaker conditions; none gives an unconditional warrant for majority wisdom.

**Facts and values are unlike objects.** A jury deciding whether an event occurred faces a different logical task from a community distributing a scarce budget among rival goods. The language of correct answers may conceal political disagreement over the standards by which an outcome is judged. Condorcet recognized differences among decision problems, but later uses of his theorem often collapse them.

**An educational programme can centralize authority.** Public institutions need knowledge, teachers and standards. They can also entrench the power of specialists to decide what others ought to learn. Independence from the government of the day does not make every educational authority infallible. The proposed protection is continued criticism, not a monopoly of expertise.

**Universal rights and abolitionist paternalism.** Condorcet condemned slavery while proposing a staged route to emancipation that could withhold immediate exercise of rights on the ground that formerly enslaved persons needed preparation. The contradiction has been interpreted differently by historians; it cannot be concealed beneath a general portrait of enlightened universalism.

**A Eurocentric horizon of progress.** The *Sketch* can imply that the forms of knowledge and government developed in Europe set the norm for world history. A theory meant to reduce inequality risks describing other societies as behind on a single civilizational scale. A modern reading must allow multiple histories and the voices of people subjected to colonial domination.

**A revolution with no safe place for dissent.** Condorcet's life shows the insufficiency of announcing rights while political institutions allow differences over public arrangements to be treated as treason. This history does not make every revolutionary actor interchangeable or prove that any one constitution would have prevented the Terror. It demonstrates the political vulnerability of reasoned opposition.

**A philosophy of progress that can look too assured.** The possibility of transmitting knowledge does not establish that each generation will use it well. Destruction, censorship, war and dispossession can interrupt or reverse improvements. Perfectibility is better understood as a capacity requiring conditions than as a prophecy that those conditions will always prevail.

**The danger of mathematical prestige.** A mathematically correct argument can carry unwarranted authority when its assumptions, choice of model or relation to the political problem are hidden. Condorcet's intellectual ambition is best honoured by examining his demonstrations as demonstrations, not by using their elegance to command agreement.

### Curiosities worth following

**Why 'Condorcet'?** He is commonly known by a territorial title, marquis de Condorcet, rather than by the full family name de Caritat. The title belongs to the old social order whose privileges the Revolution challenged.

**Where did his mathematical career begin?** His early work on integral calculus attracted the attention of the scientific community; d'Alembert became an important mentor and interlocutor.

**What did a permanent secretary of a scientific academy do?** The role included the continuing public and intellectual work through which a scientific institution preserves and communicates research. It helps explain his practical understanding of knowledge as organized cooperation.

**Did he write biographies?** Yes, including lives of Turgot and Voltaire. They are valuable for understanding the reformers and intellectual models he admired, while retaining the selectivity of such tributes.

**Who was Sophie de Grouchy?** A philosopher, translator, political interlocutor and editor with an independent body of thought. Her intellectual achievement must not be reduced to her work in preserving her husband's writings.

**What name appeared on his abolitionist book?** The 1781 *Reflections* appeared under a form of the name Schwartz, commonly identified as Joachim or Monsieur Schwartz. The choice of a persona is part of its rhetorical and publishing history.

**Was public instruction intended only for children?** No. Condorcet's writings attend to the need to maintain and extend knowledge in adult life, especially where work and unequal opportunities restrict continued education.

**What was his relation to the death penalty?** He opposed capital punishment, including in the revolutionary debates around the fate of Louis XVI. The precise record of votes and positions should be approached through the revolutionary proceedings, not reduced to a single heroic anecdote.

**Was the *Sketch* a finished book written in peace?** It was composed substantially while he was in concealment during 1793–94 and published after his death. Its structure and manuscript history require editorial care.

**How did Condorcet die?** He was arrested in March 1794 and died in custody in Bourg-la-Reine. His precise cause of death is uncertain. The poison-ring narrative remains a story, not established proof.

### Common misreadings to avoid

- **'Condorcet proved that the majority is always right.'** The jury theorem is conditional; its independence and competence assumptions must be justified.
- **'The jury theorem and the Condorcet paradox contradict each other.'** They treat different decision structures: the reliability of a binary truth judgment and the consistency of rankings across multiple alternatives.
- **'Three voters who create a cycle must be irrational.'** Every individual may have a coherent ranking while pairwise group preferences are cyclical.
- **'There is always a Condorcet winner.'** Some preference profiles produce no alternative that defeats every rival in pairwise comparisons.
- **'Condorcet and Borda proposed the same procedure.'** Their approaches to preference aggregation differ in significant ways.
- **'Arrow's theorem was already proved by Condorcet.'** Modern social choice theory developed distinct results centuries later.
- **'Condorcet was an eighteenth-century data scientist.'** This is a modern analogy and risks obscuring the political and mathematical setting of his work.
- **'He wanted educated experts to rule everyone else.'** His educational programme aimed to reduce dependence, though it also created questions about the power of institutions of knowledge.
- **'Schooling and political rights are interchangeable.'** Rights belong to persons as a matter of equal standing; education improves the ability to exercise them.
- **'He only defended women's education.'** He explicitly argued for women's political citizenship.
- **'His abolitionism was identical to immediate emancipation.'** His proposed gradualist measures require independent ethical and historical scrutiny.
- **'His optimism guaranteed progress.'** The *Sketch* expresses a powerful account of perfectibility amid persecution; history supplies no automatic guarantee.
- **'His death has a known dramatic explanation.'** The exact cause remains unresolved.
- **'A digital consultation is a Condorcet election.'** Actual rules and forms of decision must be examined before the analogy is useful.

### Working glossary

**Agenda.** The set and order of choices put before voters; its design can affect outcomes under some voting procedures.

**Binary question.** A decision with two possible answers, such as whether a particular factual claim is true or false.

**Transitive preference.** A ranking satisfying the condition that A above B and B above C imply A above C.

**Majority cycle.** A sequence of pairwise majority victories that returns to an earlier alternative, preventing a consistent overall ranking.

**Correlated error.** A mistake shared by several people because their sources, influences or decision processes are statistically dependent.

**Strategic voting.** A voter reporting a preference or choosing an action to influence the outcome rather than simply stating a sincere ranking; its significance varies by electoral system.

**Competence threshold.** In the simplest jury theorem, the better-than-one-half probability of correct individual judgment that helps make larger groups more reliable.

**Condorcet winner.** The alternative with pairwise majority support against every other alternative, if one exists.

**Borda count.** A point-based procedure that aggregates the positions of alternatives in voters' rankings.

**Publicity.** The public accessibility of reasons, evidence and decision procedures; a political commitment that cannot be secured by publication of a final number alone.

**Corrigibility.** The ability to identify and revise an error. In this guide it is used in Cerchi's contemporary institutional sense, rather than as a claim that Condorcet invented the term's modern technical usage.

**Lifelong learning.** Opportunities to develop knowledge beyond initial schooling, important to civic independence as institutions and sciences change.

**Political citizenship.** A form of equal participation in the making and revision of public laws and decisions.

**Gradualist abolition.** Ending slavery through staged measures; in Condorcet's case the relation between unconditional rights and delayed freedom is a critical issue.

**Perfectibility.** The capacity for improvement of human understanding, institutions and conditions of life, without a logically necessary guarantee of success.

**Social mathematics.** A historical ambition to apply mathematical methods to problems of collective human and political life; it must be read with attention to the limits of models.

### Reading paths

**If you have fifteen minutes.** Read movements 1, 2 and 3 of the main essay, then the opening of the **Before the count** module. This establishes the connection between learning, collective reliability and voting procedures.

**If you have one hour.** Read the 1792 parliamentary report on public instruction in a reliable edition, with the question of intellectual dependence in mind. Then study the three-voter preference cycle and ask how changing an agenda changes what appears to be the majority's choice.

**If you have a week.** Begin with the 1790 essay on women's citizenship. Proceed to the first of the 1791 memoirs on instruction, the April 1792 report and selected sections of the 1785 *Essay*. Read the 1781 slavery text beside contemporary scholarship on gradualist abolition. Finish with the *Sketch* and a scholarly reconstruction of the 1793 constitutional disputes.

**If your interest is mathematics.** Derive the three-person jury calculation, vary \(p\) above and below 0.5, and then ask what changes if error is correlated. Reconstruct the preference cycle. Compare the theoretical questions with a modern specialist study of jury theorems and social choice; do not confuse proofs with empirical observations about electorates.

**If your interest is education.** Read the five memoirs and the legislative report together. Track which institutions provide access, what freedoms the authority funding them must respect and how adult instruction changes the notion of citizenship. Compare the historical proposal with a contemporary example using documented facts.

**If your interest is rights.** Place the women's citizenship argument alongside the abolitionist reflections. Ask whether the same principle is extended consistently, where paternalism enters, and whose agency is absent from the European author's account.

**If you are reading the triad.** Read Cicero on *res publica*, Tocqueville on associations and the force of opinion, and Condorcet on the conditions of collective judgment. Hold the tension between law, learned civic freedom and revisable decisions without converting the three writers into a single historical movement.

### Questions to orient a reading

1. When does a political decision concern a fact that can be correct or incorrect, and when does it concern conflicting legitimate ends?
2. How can a mathematically valid inference become a politically misleading claim when its premises are hidden?
3. Why does independence of judgment matter as much as the number of people voting?
4. What changes in a collective preference when alternatives are introduced or compared in a different sequence?
5. Can a right be fully exercised if the person holding it has no practical access to the knowledge required?
6. How should education be protected from the authority responsible for funding it?
7. What can we learn from Condorcet's defence of women's citizenship about rights and institutionally produced inequalities?
8. Where does his abolitionism fall short of its own unconditional principle?
9. Does the possibility of human improvement imply that history necessarily improves?
10. Which forms of dissent must a constitutional democracy protect even after an authorized majority has decided?
11. What evidence would be needed to show that a contemporary information environment produces correlated judgments?
12. Whose questions are absent from a consultation that presents only a limited set of alternatives?

### Nodes to remember

- Condorcet connects mathematics, political rights, education and institutional revision within a single Enlightenment project.
- The jury theorem makes conditional claims about the reliability of a majority, not a universal defence of its correctness.
- Majority voting across three or more ranked alternatives can generate a cycle even when individual preferences are coherent.
- The agenda and aggregation rule are part of the decision, not merely a neutral container for preferences.
- Equal civic standing requires capacities to understand public rules, though rights cannot depend on passing an education test.
- The education programme combines universal access with an ambition to protect inquiry from political doctrine.
- Condorcet defended women's political rights and opposed slavery, yet his gradualist approach to emancipation retained a troubling paternalism.
- The *Sketch* presents human improvement as a historical possibility while its author was being persecuted.
- The contemporary wound, in Cerchi's reading, is the decision without adequate judgment: efficient counting detached from open, inclusive and revisable formation of the choice.

### Frequently asked questions

**1. Who was Nicolas de Condorcet?**  
Nicolas de Condorcet (1743–1794) was a French mathematician, philosopher and political reformer who worked on probability, voting, public instruction, rights and historical progress. He served in revolutionary assemblies and died in custody after being persecuted during the Terror.

**2. What are Condorcet's most important works?**  
The 1785 *Essay* on probability and majority decisions; the 1791 memoirs and 1792 report on public instruction; the 1790 essay on women's citizenship; the 1781 abolitionist reflections; the constitutional writings of 1793; and the posthumous *Sketch* of human progress.

**3. What is the Condorcet paradox?**  
It is the possibility that pairwise majority preferences form a cycle. A group can prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A even though each voter has a consistent ranking. The procedure and agenda can therefore affect the collective result.

**4. What is the Condorcet jury theorem?**  
In its familiar simplified form, it says that under suitable conditions—such as a binary question with a correct answer, independent votes and individual probabilities of being correct above one-half—the majority can be more reliable than an individual, with reliability increasing as group size grows. Its conditions matter.

**5. Does the jury theorem prove that democracy is always right?**  
No. It models a particular kind of truth-oriented decision under strong assumptions. It cannot guarantee reliable majority decisions when information is correlated, voters are systematically mistaken or the choice involves competing political values without a single objectively correct answer.

**6. What is a Condorcet winner?**  
An alternative that defeats each other alternative in head-to-head majority comparisons. Depending on the preferences, no such winner may exist.

**7. How does Condorcet differ from Borda?**  
Condorcet's approach emphasizes pairwise majority comparisons; Borda's count assigns scores according to positions in a ranking. These methods can generate different outcomes, and each reflects a choice about how preferences ought to be aggregated.

**8. Why did Condorcet apply mathematics to voting?**  
He wanted to understand how fallible judgments can be combined, how decision rules affect outcomes and when a majority might provide reliable guidance. His inquiry connected probability with the public institutions that make a decision binding.

**9. Why did he regard public instruction as politically essential?**  
Because formal rights are weakened when citizens depend on others to read laws, interpret evidence or understand economic transactions. Public instruction could distribute capacities for independent judgment and the effective exercise of rights.

**10. Did Condorcet support education for women?**  
Yes. His educational project defended women's access to instruction, and his 1790 essay explicitly defended women's political citizenship. Educational and political equality are related in his work, but they are not the same claim.

**11. What did he mean by freedom of inquiry?**  
Publicly supported institutions of learning should be protected, as far as possible, from political commands to teach contested beliefs as truths. Knowledge must remain open to evidence, revision and criticism, including criticism of government.

**12. Was Condorcet an abolitionist?**  
Yes. He condemned slavery as a violation of rights and campaigned against the slave trade. His proposals also included gradualist measures that allowed paternalistic delay in emancipation; this is a serious tension in his position.

**13. What did Condorcet mean by progress?**  
The possibility that human capacities, knowledge, freedom and institutions could improve through learning and transmission across generations. His *Sketch* expresses strong confidence in perfectibility without providing a proof that history must advance automatically.

**14. Was Condorcet a naïve optimist?**  
That is too simple. His confidence in the possibility of improvement was shaped by scientific and political commitments, and he wrote on progress while being persecuted. Nevertheless, his account can be criticized for Eurocentric assumptions and for overstating the coherence of historical development.

**15. What was Condorcet's role in the French Revolution?**  
He participated in the Legislative Assembly and the National Convention, worked on education and constitutional projects, advocated rights and opposed capital punishment. Disagreement with the political direction of 1793 contributed to his persecution.

**16. Why was Condorcet persecuted?**  
After revolutionary factional struggles and his opposition to the new constitutional settlement in 1793, his position became dangerous. He went into hiding and was later arrested. His case shows the vulnerability of dissent within a political movement claiming to speak for the people.

**17. How did Condorcet die?**  
He died in custody at Bourg-la-Reine in March 1794, shortly after arrest. Historians disagree over the precise cause. The familiar claim of suicide by poison should be treated as unproven.

**18. Did Condorcet invent modern social choice theory?**  
He made foundational contributions to problems later central to social choice, especially preference cycles and the analysis of majority decisions. Modern social choice theory has developed many other methods and results; attributing all of it directly to Condorcet would be inaccurate.

**19. What is the link between Condorcet, Cicero and Tocqueville?**  
In the Cerchi triad, Cicero explores the commonwealth's legal and moral claims; Tocqueville examines the learned practices of civic liberty and the power of opinion; Condorcet studies the conditions under which fallible citizens can form and revise a collective decision. The three address different historical and institutional worlds.

**20. Why read Condorcet today?**  
He helps us ask what lies behind an apparently decisive number: the definition of the question, the knowledge available, the independence of judgments, the rights of excluded people and the means of correcting an error. Those questions matter for elections, public consultation and institutions that aggregate data or preferences.

### An editorial closing

Condorcet asked what a community can know when no citizen can know everything. The mathematics matters because its results have conditions. Citizens may improve their collective judgment if they contribute sufficiently competent and independent assessments; they may also assemble a cycle that defeats the picture of a perfectly coherent public will. The force of the analysis lies in exposing how much of a decision has already been shaped before the result appears.

He wanted education to give substance to rights, and he defended rights that his own political world withheld from women and enslaved persons. His failures are part of that story: the freedom recognized in principle could still be postponed by those who appointed themselves the judges of another person's readiness. His hopes for historical improvement were written under persecution, in a republic whose authorities could no longer bear his disagreement.

The citizen at the end of this guide has a ballot, but needs more than a ballot. She needs to understand the question, recognize the reasons offered for each answer, know that a misleading procedure can be challenged and trust that a public mistake may be corrected without ending her standing as a member of the political community. The vote matters. So do the conditions that make the act of voting worthy of free people.
