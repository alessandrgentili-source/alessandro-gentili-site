# Claude Shannon

**Measuring information and the boundary between communicating, understanding, and knowing**

**Information, noise, and the limits of transmission**

## Introduction

Some intellectual revolutions begin by adding something to the world. Claude Shannon's takes shape, above all, through an act of subtraction.

A message may be a declaration of love, a stock quotation, a military order, a page of poetry, a mistake, or a lie. For the person receiving it, the differences are immense. For the problem Shannon chooses to address, they can temporarily be set aside. The question becomes: how can a message be represented, encoded, and transmitted through a channel, using finite resources and in the presence of noise, while establishing quantitatively what is achievable?

This delimitation gave rise to a decisive part of twentieth-century communications engineering. Different sources can be described through probabilities; different signals can be compared in terms of information quantity; every channel has limits; the statistical structure of a source can be exploited to represent it more efficiently; redundancy can be introduced to protect transmission from errors.

Shannon's strength lies in the rigour with which he defines what his theory measures and what it leaves out.

Today we call almost everything 'information': data, news, knowledge, documents, images, search results, social content, answers generated by machines. The technical meaning Shannon made measurable is much narrower: it does not guarantee the truth of content, does not coincide with knowledge, and does not measure the importance of a message to a human life.

A civilisation may therefore become extraordinarily capable of transmitting information while remaining exposed to a different problem: determining which of the things in circulation deserve to be believed, understood, and remembered.

The theory's precision and the boundary that makes it possible are inseparable.

## Claude Shannon at a Glance

**Claude Elwood Shannon** was born in 1916 in Petoskey, Michigan, and died in 2001. A mathematician, engineer, and researcher, his career took him through some of the defining institutions of twentieth-century American science: the University of Michigan, MIT, the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, and Bell Laboratories.

His first enduring result came from bringing Boolean algebra together with relay circuits. He demonstrated how logical operations could be systematically used to analyse and design switching networks—a fundamental connection for the theory of digital circuits.

At the centre of his work stands the 1948 paper *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*. Shannon constructed a general theory of information transmission through channels, giving rigorous form to concepts that would become central to the vocabulary of the digital world: measurable information, bits, entropy, channel capacity, coding, redundancy, and noise.

The decisive step was to separate the technical problem of communication from the semantic meaning of the message. The theory asks how much can be transmitted, how efficiently, and how reliably. Truth, importance, intelligibility, and value belong to other levels of the problem.

This delimitation is what makes formalisation possible.

From it arises the contemporary paradox that makes Shannon necessary still. We have built a civilisation capable of producing, storing, and transmitting enormous quantities of information; information quantity, meaning, knowledge, and truth nevertheless remain distinct quantities and problems.

## Why Shannon Matters

Before Shannon, telecommunications already possessed an extraordinary wealth of devices, techniques, and mathematical results. Telegraphy, telephony, and radio had created concrete problems involving speed, bandwidth, distortion, noise, and coding. Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley had developed essential results concerning transmission and the logarithmic measurement of information. In the nineteenth century, George Boole had developed an algebra of logic. Norbert Wiener was treating communication, filtering, and prediction as statistical problems.

Shannon brought these strands together in a theory of wider scope.

His originality lies in defining an object precisely enough to be measured. Narrowing the problem makes it possible to formalise it; that very restriction yields results of remarkable generality.

Two results reveal its reach.

The first concerns representation: a source with a given statistical structure has a theoretical limit to lossless compression. If certain symbols or sequences are more predictable than others, their representation can exploit that regularity.

The second concerns transmission: a noisy channel has a capacity. Below that limit there are codes that can, asymptotically, make the probability of error arbitrarily small; above it the same guarantee cannot be obtained.

A decisive part of digital infrastructure emerges from these two questions: **how to represent efficiently** and **how to transmit reliably**. Shannon made it possible to formulate limits that every concrete technology must respect or seek to approach.

## The Human Problem: What We Choose to Leave Out

Every measurement begins with a choice: deciding which differences matter for the question we are asking.

The human problem arises when the success of a measurement conceals that choice. What is counted then acquires a greater appearance of reality than what remains outside the measure; a metric designed to describe one part of a phenomenon may end up standing for the whole.

Views, probabilities, scores, performance measures, and flows are not false merely because they are partial. They become misleading when the measured part replaces the object it was only supposed to describe.

Shannon makes this tension particularly clear because the boundary of his inquiry is explicitly stated. The technical problem of communication is isolated so that it can be treated mathematically. The model's validity also depends on clarity about the limits within which it operates.

*Cerchi d'inchiostro* draws a methodological lesson from this, not a universal philosophy: responsible formalisation must remember what it has chosen not to measure.

## Essential Biography

Claude Elwood Shannon was born on 30 April 1916 in Petoskey, Michigan, and grew up in Gaylord. From an early age he was familiar with electrical and mechanical devices; he also worked as a messenger for Western Union.

In 1936 he completed a double course of study in mathematics and electrical engineering at the University of Michigan. That combination would remain a recognisable feature of his scientific intelligence: Shannon continually crossed the boundary between theory and construction.

At MIT he worked as an assistant on Vannevar Bush's **Differential Analyzer**, a large analogue computer. The machine's control circuits used electromechanical relays. Shannon recognised that the open and closed states of switches could be described using George Boole's logical algebra. He developed his master's thesis in 1937, publishing it in 1938 as *A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits*, turning that insight into a method for analysing and synthesising circuits.

In 1940 he earned a PhD in mathematics at MIT with *An Algebra for Theoretical Genetics*, an application of algebraic methods to theoretical genetics.

Between 1940 and 1941 he was a National Research Fellow at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study. In 1941 he joined Bell Laboratories, where he worked during the war on control systems, communications, and cryptography. Many of the elements that would converge in information theory matured in this environment.

In 1948 he published *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* in two parts in the *Bell System Technical Journal*. The following year brought *Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems*, a public development of a classified cryptographic report written during the war.

He returned to MIT in 1956, initially as a visiting professor; in 1958 he became Donner Professor of Science. He retired in 1978.

He died on 24 February 2001, aged eighty-four.

## The Life That Illuminates the Work

Shannon's biography becomes interesting when it makes his method visible.

Faced with the Differential Analyzer, he did more than understand how a machine worked. He looked for a common structure between things belonging to different languages: electrical switches and logical propositions. In telecommunications he made a related move: behind different media—telegraph, telephone, radio, discrete or continuous signals—he sought a structure general enough to make their problems comparable.

His more playful devices share this character. The electromechanical mouse **Theseus**, which could find its way through a maze and remember the route, juggling machines, and experiments with chess show the same inclination to turn an idea into a device and test an abstraction against a machine. Play thus becomes a laboratory for isolating structures.

His 1952 talk on creative thinking is consistent with this approach. Shannon emphasises simplification, analogy, reformulation, generalisation, structural decomposition, and reversing a problem. These strategies are recognisable in his major work: removing what is unnecessary, changing representations, seeking a more general level, and reversing the point of view.

The talk must be treated for what it is: a Bell Labs internal typescript dated 20 March 1952, not a published scientific article. The bibliography of the *Collected Papers* records it as a *typescript* and specifies that it was not included in the collection; transcripts circulated later have made the contents accessible.

A constant emerges: Shannon often finds the representation that makes a problem tractable before attempting to solve it.

## Context: When Communication Becomes a Mathematical Problem

The world in which Shannon's theory emerged was already saturated with technical communications.

Telegraphy had brought the problem of discrete coding. Telephony had converted speech into a continuous electrical signal. Radio had multiplied distance and interference. Telephone networks needed to use expensive infrastructure efficiently. Every system had its own techniques, constraints, and vocabulary.

At Bell Labs, Harry Nyquist had studied transmission speed and the relationship between signals, bandwidth, and intersymbol interference. In 1928 Ralph Hartley had proposed a logarithmic measure of information quantity associated with the number of possible selections.

There was another genealogy as well: probability, statistics, statistical mechanics, and Wiener's work on stochastic processes, filtering, and prediction.

Shannon unified these lines of inquiry without erasing their differences. A communication system could be described in terms of a source, message, transmitter, signal, channel, noise, receiver, and destination. The physical nature of the equipment matters to practical engineering; the theory seeks a common mathematical structure.

Choice among possibilities, coding, channel constraints, and error probability thus become parts of a single problem capable of formal treatment.

## Disciplinary Circles

### Mathematics

Probability, logarithms, stochastic processes, and entropy make it possible to treat sources and channels quantitatively.

### Electrical Engineering and Telecommunications

The theory's original terrain: bandwidth, signals, noise, power, coding, and transmission.

### Logic and Circuits

Boolean algebra applied to relays shows how abstract operations can become physical architectures.

### Computer Science

Shannon's work predates computer science as an established discipline, but his research into circuits and binary representation became part of digital computing's conceptual foundations.

### Cryptography

An adversary's uncertainty, keys, and secrecy can be addressed mathematically. Shannon helped turn modern cryptography into a theoretical field.

### Coding Theory

The possibility of representing sources efficiently and protecting messages against errors opens a field later developed by Hamming and many subsequent generations.

### Cybernetics and Systems Theory

Shannon shares some tools and problems with Wiener; feedback and control, however, play a role in cybernetics that does not define the core of information theory.

### Artificial Intelligence, Automata, and Problem Solving

Chess, Theseus, and automata theory reveal a genuine interest in memory, choice, search, and machines. Shannon was also among the four signatories to the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence and co-edited *Automata Studies* with John McCarthy in 1956. His relationship with AI's historical beginnings is therefore documented, without making Shannon the father of its contemporary forms.

### Philosophy of Information

This is chiefly a later history. The theory's success made it inevitable that people would ask what 'information' meant outside the engineering domain. It is precisely here that Shannon's boundaries become necessary.

## Major Works and Contributions

### *A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits* — 1938

Applies Boolean algebra to the analysis and design of relay circuits. Its historical value lies in making systematic the passage between symbolic logic and switching networks.

### *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* — 1948

The centre of his work. Published in two parts in the *Bell System Technical Journal*, it formalises sources, information, entropy, coding, channels, noise, capacity, and the limits of transmission.

### *Communication in the Presence of Noise* — 1949

Develops the geometric representation of signals, sampling, and the capacity of continuous channels in the presence of noise. It should be read within the history leading to the theorem now associated with Nyquist and Shannon.

### *Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems* — 1949

Derived from a confidential wartime report. Applies probabilistic methods to cryptography and formalises, among other things, the notion of perfect secrecy.

### *Programming a Computer for Playing Chess* — 1950

Approaches chess as a search problem within an immense space of possibilities and discusses strategies for making it tractable. The text belongs to the early history of problems that would enter artificial intelligence, without containing later developments.

### *Prediction and Entropy of Printed English* — 1951

Studies the statistical predictability of written English and the problem of linguistic redundancy. Shows how closely information theory can approach language without becoming a theory of meaning.

### *Creative Thinking* — 1952

A typescript of an internal Bell Labs talk on 20 March 1952. It is valuable chiefly for understanding Shannon's method: simplify, change perspective, decompose, and reverse. Its documentary status requires greater bibliographic caution than published scientific papers.

### Dartmouth Proposal — 1955

Shannon signed the proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence with John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and Nathaniel Rochester. The document places him within the field's historical formation, without making information theory a theory of AI.

### *The Bandwagon* — 1956

In this short editorial, Shannon warned against indiscriminately extending information theory beyond its own domain. Applications in other fields, he argued, require assumptions and verification: simply transferring the vocabulary of information, entropy, and redundancy is not enough.

### *Automata Studies* — 1956

A volume edited by Shannon and John McCarthy, bringing together work on automata, neural networks, Turing machines, and computing problems. It documents another genuine point of contact with the emerging field of intelligent machines.

### *Coding Theorems for a Discrete Source with a Fidelity Criterion* — 1959

Develops a more systematic treatment of the relationship between coding rate and tolerable distortion. It is one of the foundational texts of rate–distortion theory and completes the picture of source coding when perfect reconstruction is unnecessary.

## The First Revolution: When Logic Enters Circuits

Before information theory, Shannon had already made a decisive advance.

A relay can be open or closed; a logical proposition can take either of two values. Shannon showed that the rules of Boolean algebra could be systematically applied to describe relay networks, verify their equivalence, simplify them, and design them from desired logical conditions.

The historical contribution lies in formalising a method of analysis and synthesis: a logical structure can become a physical switching network.

The birth of digital computing belongs to a much wider history. Shannon's work nevertheless provided a fundamental theoretical language for the design of logic circuits.

A feature of his thinking is already visible: relating different domains, identifying a shared structure, and choosing a representation that makes the problem manipulable.

The circuit can thus be read as the physical realisation of a logical structure. Methodologically, it is the same kind of move that in 1948 would allow different communication technologies to be reduced to a common mathematical structure.

## *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*

The 1948 paper begins with an apparently simple problem: a message is selected at one point and must be reconstructed, exactly or approximately, at another.

The simplicity is deceptive.

To design a system, considering only the message actually sent is insufficient. We must consider the entire set of possible messages from which the source could have chosen and the probabilities with which it produces them.

The general model distinguishes:

- **information source**, which generates or selects the message;
- **transmitter**, which converts the message into a signal suitable for the channel;
- **channel**, the technical means through which the signal passes;
- **noise source**, which introduces disturbances;
- **receiver**, which reconstructs the message from the received signal;
- **destination**, to which the message is addressed.

The theory deals with discrete, continuous, and mixed systems. Their common problem concerns representation and transmission under constraints. For continuous sources, exact reconstruction would generally require an unlimited number of bits; it therefore becomes necessary to specify a **fidelity criterion**, establishing what distortion of the reconstruction is acceptable. Shannon would develop this idea further in rate–distortion theory.

Here the concepts that would make Shannon famous enter the picture.

Information is connected to the probabilities of possible selections. Entropy measures a source's average uncertainty. Capacity establishes the maximum information rate compatible with a channel. Coding theorems demonstrate the fundamental limits of compression and reliable transmission.

What remains striking today is not merely the number of results. It is the level at which Shannon chooses to frame his question.

Telegraphy, speech, images, and data become different instances of a common problem.

## The Price of Abstraction

Shannon's technical universality is born of a deliberate renunciation.

To build a theory common to telegraphy, speech, images, and data, we must disregard differences that remain decisive in other contexts. The formalism considers statistical structure, codes, channels, and reception conditions; the message's particular value, truth, and meaning do not enter the calculation.

This exclusion is the contract underlying Shannon's abstraction. The model acquires generality precisely because it declares the level at which it operates.

The price appears when that contract is forgotten. If 'information' becomes an undifferentiated name for data, meanings, knowledge, decisions, or forms of life, the concept's technical precision does not automatically transfer into those new domains.

Here lies the original reading developed by *Cerchi d'inchiostro*: **an abstraction remains fruitful as long as it remembers what it has excluded**.

## What 'Information' Means

In everyday language, 'information' suggests useful content: news, knowledge, something that makes us more aware.

For Shannon, the term acquires a different, technical meaning.

A source produces symbols or messages according to certain probabilities. The greater the uncertainty surrounding the next selection, the greater the average quantity of information associated with the source.

In modern notation, the self-information associated with an event \(x\) having probability \(p(x)\) is expressed as:

\[
I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)
\]

A highly probable event carries little self-information because it was already expected; an improbable event carries more because observing it reduces uncertainty to a greater extent. This 'surprise' is probabilistic, not psychological.

If a source always generates the same symbol, there is no uncertainty about its selection. If it generates two equally probable symbols, each selection resolves an alternative.

An important consequence follows: truth and information quantity belong to different levels. A false but improbable statement may have high self-information relative to the distribution under consideration; a truth repeated continually may add very little.

The distinction between **information**, **meaning**, **knowledge**, and **truth** is more than terminology: it prevents us from asking Shannon's measure to assess something it was never designed to evaluate.

## The Bit: A Measure, Not a Metaphysics

The bit has become one of the most familiar units of our time.

The word is a contraction of *binary digit*, suggested by John Tukey and adopted by Shannon in 1948. When the logarithm has base 2, information quantity is measured in bits.

In the elementary case, one bit corresponds to the quantity of information resolved by a choice between two equally probable alternatives.

Two frequently conflated usages should be distinguished. A **binary digit** is a 0 or 1 used in a binary representation; a **bit as a unit of information** measures a quantity. An equiprobable binary digit carries one bit of information, while a heavily biased binary source produces, on average, less than one bit per symbol.

Base 2 is natural for many digital systems and makes the relationship with states such as 0 and 1 transparent. The theory does not, however, maintain that the world is ontologically made of bits.

Circuits, memory, coding, and digital communications can be designed with robust binary representations. Turning this engineering effectiveness into a claim about reality's ultimate substance means leaving the domain demonstrated by the theory.

For the same reason, a bit is not the modern equivalent of a Lucretian atom. The atom belongs to a theory of matter's constitution; the bit measures information and can participate in a representation.

The resemblance between 'minimal elements' does not erase the ontological difference.

## Entropy: Measuring Uncertainty

For a discrete source with possible outcomes \(x_i\) and probabilities \(p_i\), Shannon entropy, using base 2, is expressed as:

\[
H(X) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
\]

Entropy measures a source's average uncertainty and, within the model, the expected value of the information associated with its selections.

If an outcome is certain, entropy is minimal. For a fixed number of alternatives, it reaches its maximum when the outcomes are equally probable. For sources with temporal dependencies, many coding problems require the **entropy rate**, which also accounts for the structure between successive symbols.

A regular source contains exploitable predictability; a less predictable source requires, on average, more information to describe its selections.

The word 'entropy' creates one of the most persistent misunderstandings in Shannon's reception. The formula has a mathematical kinship with entropy in statistical mechanics, but these quantities belong to different theoretical frameworks and should not automatically be identified.

The celebrated story that John von Neumann suggested the term to Shannon must also be treated as retrospective testimony, not a contemporary document. The most frequently quoted version derives from an account published by Myron Tribus and Edward McIrvine in 1971 of a conversation with Shannon that took place years afterwards.

For Shannon, entropy is a measure defined over a distribution of possibilities. Using it as a synonym for everyday disorder, political confusion, or the quality of a text requires an additional metaphorical step, which should be acknowledged.

## Channel, Noise, and Capacity

In Shannon, the channel is a technical structure for transmission.

It may be a wire, a frequency band, a radio link, or any system able to carry a signal from transmitter to receiver. The channel has constraints: bandwidth, power, noise characteristics, and transition probabilities between symbols.

Noise introduces uncertainty between what is transmitted and what is received. In the theory it is not synonymous with falsehood, propaganda, or the irrelevance of content: those are metaphorical extensions belonging to other levels.

**Channel capacity** establishes the maximum rate compatible with reliable transmission under the model being considered.

For a discrete memoryless channel, a modern formulation is:

\[
C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)
\]

where \(I(X;Y)\) is the mutual information between input and output, and the maximum is taken over possible input distributions. In 1948 Shannon expressed the same structure in terms of entropy and **equivocation**; the notation \(I(X;Y)\) is now more common.

For a band-limited channel with additive white Gaussian noise, bandwidth \(B\), average signal power \(S\), and noise power \(N\) **within the bandwidth considered**, the relation commonly called the Shannon–Hartley formula is:

\[
C = B \log_2\left(1+\frac{S}{N}\right)
\]

With \(B\) in hertz and the logarithm in base 2, \(C\) is expressed in bits per second. The formula applies to this specific channel model and does not replace the general definition of capacity.

Nyquist had studied limits imposed by bandwidth upon signal rates; Hartley had connected the amount transmitted to the number of distinguishable selections. Shannon incorporated noise and showed that capacity and reliability could be treated within a general theory.

The decisive result is a quantitative boundary: below capacity, error can be made arbitrarily small in the asymptotic sense through suitable codes; above capacity the same arbitrarily high reliability cannot be achieved.

## The Redundancy That Saves the Message

'Redundancy' sounds negative. In everyday language it suggests waste, repetition, inefficiency.

In information theory, the word has two functions that must remain distinct.

**Source redundancy** arises from statistical predictability: some sequences are more probable than others. This structure can be exploited to represent a message using fewer resources, on average. This is the principle making lossless compression possible.

**Code redundancy**, by contrast, may be deliberately added when a message must cross a noisy channel. Additional constraints and symbols help the receiver detect or correct errors; they do not increase the information produced by the source, but they strengthen the robustness of the transmitted representation.

One of the most fruitful paradoxes of Shannon's legacy follows:

**Efficient representation tends to remove redundancy; reliable transmission may require adding it.**

A source can be compressed to avoid waste, and the resulting stream can then be protected with a code that reintroduces structure to withstand errors. The word is the same; its function within the system is different.

## Compression, Distortion, and Error Correction

Shannon's coding theorems establish different boundaries depending on the problem we want to solve.

### Source Coding

For a discrete source and lossless reconstruction, statistical structure determines how much average compression is possible. Entropy provides the fundamental limit on average length per symbol, attainable asymptotically with sufficiently long blocks under the model's assumptions.

Not every communication, however, requires an exact copy.

A telephone voice signal, image, or continuous signal can tolerate a certain difference between original and reconstruction. Shannon had already formulated this problem in 1948 through fidelity criteria; in 1959 he developed the relationship between **coding rate** and **permitted distortion** more systematically. Lossy compression therefore asks how much accuracy we can sacrifice to reduce the necessary resources.

### Channel Coding

A noisy channel presents a different problem: protecting transmission against errors.

In 1948 Shannon showed, through an existence argument based on ensembles of codes, that below channel capacity it is possible to find codes whose error frequency or probability becomes arbitrarily small as block length increases. The result is asymptotic and supplies no universal recipe for constructing efficient codes; above capacity, moreover, the same guarantee of arbitrarily high reliability cannot be maintained.

A vast part of later coding theory emerged within this territory: designing implementable schemes that approach the theoretical limits at acceptable costs in complexity, delay, and energy.

Lossless compression, compression with controlled distortion, and error protection thus answer three different questions. The theory establishes the limits within which concrete technologies must operate.

## Meaning Placed in Brackets

In his 1948 paper Shannon makes one of the most famous delimitations in twentieth-century science: the semantic aspects of a message are irrelevant to the engineering problem he is formalising.

The statement is precise. Shannon recognises that messages have meaning; he then establishes that the system must be designed with reference to the set of possible selections, independently of the particular meaning of the message actually chosen.

Suspending semantics makes the model technically general. A poem and a weather table can pass through the same channel; a true sentence and a false one can require the same quantity of resources.

As soon as we ask whether the recipient has understood, whether a statement corresponds to facts, whether the source is reliable, or whether a message has value, we have entered problems different from the one Shannon formalised.

Meaning thus remains outside the model by methodological choice, not because of a claim that it is irrelevant to human communication.

## Shannon and Weaver: A Necessary Distinction

In 1949 Shannon's paper was republished as a book with an introductory essay by **Warren Weaver**. The title changed from *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* to *The Mathematical Theory of Communication*: a volume consisting of two distinct texts that subsequent reception has often merged under the label 'Shannon–Weaver model'.

Weaver played a decisive role in spreading the theory across disciplines. He organised the general communication problem into three levels:

- **technical** problem: how accurately symbols are transmitted;
- **semantic** problem: how precisely the transmitted symbols convey the intended meaning;
- **effectiveness** problem: how successfully the received meaning produces the intended effect.

This threefold division is Weaver's and should not be attributed indiscriminately to Shannon.

Shannon was fully aware that meaning exists but built his own theory around the technical problem. Weaver considered the possibility that the mathematical framework might have wider implications and helped make it attractive to linguistics, psychology, and the social sciences.

The theory's interdisciplinary success arose in part from this extension. Distinguishing the authors allows us to see where Shannon's formalism ends and where a broader programme of communication begins.

## Shannon and Wiener: Information Is Not Cybernetics

In 1948 Shannon's *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* and Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics* appeared almost simultaneously.

Their historical proximity has encouraged later conflations. The fields share probability, statistics, and problems of communication; Shannon acknowledged the importance of Wiener's work on filtering and prediction.

Their central objects nevertheless remain different. Shannon concentrates on the representation and transmission of information, the limits of sources and channels, coding, and noise. Wiener builds cybernetics around **communication and control**, gives feedback a decisive role, and extends the framework to machines, organisms, and systems.

Information theory becomes a resource for cybernetics without coinciding with it. 'Information', 'feedback', 'control', 'decision', and 'learning' belong to theoretical families that intersect but are not synonymous.

## Shannon and Turing: Two Paths That Meet

In 1943 Alan Turing spent time in the United States and visited Bell Labs in connection with wartime activities involving cryptography and secure communication. There he met Shannon.

Both worked on classified topics, limiting what they could share about their respective projects. Later accounts recall conversations about computers, machines, and the possibilities of mechanical intelligence.

The convergence is real without requiring a founding legend. Turing formalises computation and will pursue the question of intelligent machines along his own path; Shannon works on circuits, communication, information, cryptography, automata, and search problems.

Their paths reveal two different ways of making problems previously left to intuition tractable: Turing through the formalisation of computation, Shannon through logical, probabilistic, and communicative structures.

## Cryptography and Secrecy

The war brought Shannon into problems of secure communication.

In 1949 he published *Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems*, based on an earlier confidential report. The paper applies to cryptography the same kind of probabilistic abstraction characteristic of communication theory.

An encryption system can be studied in terms of possible messages, keys, cryptograms, and probabilities.

**Perfect secrecy** specifies a very strong condition: observing the ciphertext must not change the probabilities assigned to possible original messages.

The one-time pad and the Vernam system predate Shannon as cryptographic constructions. Shannon's contribution lies in giving perfect secrecy a theoretical formulation. In the standard one-time pad construction, the key must be genuinely random, kept secret, used only once, and at least as long as the message; the theory clarifies why perfect secrecy requires key uncertainty adequate to that of the message.

Perfect secrecy does not solve every security problem. Authentication, integrity, key management, and operational security require additional properties.

Once again, the method's strength lies in defining exactly what guarantee is to be demonstrated.

## Chess, Automata, and Tractable Problems

In 1950 Shannon published *Programming a Computer for Playing Chess*.

Chess interests him because it reveals a general problem: the space of possibilities grows too rapidly for a machine with finite resources to explore it in full. One must select, evaluate positions, limit the search, and construct heuristics.

During the same period he built **Theseus**, an electromechanical mouse whose control apparatus explored a maze and stored the route it found. In the language of the time, the device could be presented as a machine capable of 'learning'; more precisely, it implements memory, search, and the reuse of a simple solution.

Shannon's relationship with artificial intelligence became more explicit a few years later. In 1955 he signed the proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence with John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and Nathaniel Rochester; in 1956 he edited *Automata Studies* with McCarthy. These are direct historical links with the formation of the field.

This does not make information theory a theory of contemporary AI. It shows something more precise: Shannon participated in the environment in which problems of computation, memory, automata, search, and machine behaviour were being organised into a new research domain.

Chess and the maze make the continuity between theory and construction visible: when confronted with a task apparently requiring intelligent skill, Shannon looks for the structure that will make it manageable by a machine.

## The Form of Shannon's Thought

Shannon's method becomes clearest when followed through concrete problems.

Faced with the relays of the Differential Analyzer, he changes representations: an electrical network becomes a logical structure that can be manipulated algebraically. In information theory he makes a related move on a greater scale: he separates transmission, meaning, and value, describes sources and channels probabilistically, and establishes limits before looking for a particular machine that can achieve them.

The same approach reappears in the objects he builds. Chess, mazes, and mechanical devices serve to isolate memory, search, constraints, and temporal regularities. Abstraction does not remain suspended above the physical world: it returns to objects and tests its own robustness there.

The *Creative Thinking* typescript makes explicit certain strategies already visible in the work—simplifying, changing perspective, using analogies, reversing the problem—but it should not be transformed into a systematic manifesto. It is testimony to the working methods of a scientist searching for the representation that would reveal the problem's essential structure.

## Key Ideas

### Information Can Be Measured Without Measuring Meaning

This is the theory's methodological core. Information quantity concerns probabilities and possibilities, not semantic value.

### Uncertainty Is Part of Information's Structure

A completely predictable source produces no new statistical information. A choice acquires informational content because alternatives exist.

### Communication Has Fundamental Limits

Every channel has a capacity. Technology and engineering can approach that limit; they cannot abolish it.

### Noise Does Not Make Reliability Impossible

Below capacity, and in the asymptotic sense, suitable codes can make the probability of error arbitrarily small.

### Redundancy Can Serve Opposite Functions

Source redundancy can be exploited for compression; redundancy added to a code can protect against errors.

### A Theoretical Limit Is Not a Design Recipe

Shannon's theorems establish what is possible or impossible under the model's conditions. Building efficient systems approaching those limits remains a further engineering problem.

## Key Concepts

### Information

A quantity connected to the probabilities of the possible selections produced by a source. It is not identical with meaning, truth, or knowledge.

### Source

A process that produces symbols or messages according to certain statistical regularities.

### Message

The sequence or configuration selected by the source for transmission.

### Signal

The physical form in which the message is represented in order to pass through a channel.

### Bit

The unit of information associated with logarithmic base 2. It must be distinguished from the mere presence of a binary digit: a 0/1 symbol carries one bit only when the outcomes are equiprobable.

### Entropy

A measure of the average uncertainty of a source and its average information.

### Channel

The physical or abstract system through which a signal is transmitted.

### Noise

A disturbance introducing uncertainty between the channel's input and output.

### Channel Capacity

The maximum rate at which information can be transmitted with arbitrarily high reliability, given the characteristics of the channel model.

### Coding

The transformation of a message into a representation suitable for efficiency, transmission, or error protection.

### Distortion

A measure of the permitted difference between source and reconstruction when an exact copy is unnecessary. Central to lossy compression and fidelity criteria.

### Source Redundancy

Statistical predictability present in data produced by a source that can be exploited for a more efficient representation.

### Code Redundancy

Structure deliberately added to a representation to detect or correct errors during transmission or storage.

### Mutual Information

A quantity measuring how much uncertainty about one variable is reduced by knowing another; central to the modern formulation of capacity for a discrete memoryless channel.

### Perfect Secrecy

A condition in which observing the cryptogram does not change the information available about the original message.

## Constellation

### Before Shannon — What Prepared the Problem

**George Boole** provided the logical algebra that Shannon connected to switching circuits. The relationship is direct at the formal level, though Boole was not constructing a theory of computers.

**Harry Nyquist** studied transmission speed, bandwidth, and telegraphic signals. His work prepared an essential part of mathematical communications engineering.

**Ralph Hartley** proposed a logarithmic measure of information related to the number of possible selections. Shannon extended this approach by introducing probabilities, entropy, the statistical structure of sources, and noise.

**Vannevar Bush** provided Shannon with the technical setting of the Differential Analyzer and a model of research capable of crossing mathematics and engineering.

**Bell Laboratories** supplied the historical laboratory of the problem: networks, speech, telegraphy, bandwidth, noise, cost, and reliability.

### Contemporaries and Interlocutors

**Warren Weaver** broadened the reception of the theory towards problems of semantics and effectiveness. He was a decisive intermediary and should be distinguished from Shannon.

**Norbert Wiener** shared the statistical treatment of communication and influenced Shannon, but built cybernetics around communication, feedback, and control.

**Alan Turing** worked on computation, cryptography, and machines. Their 1943 encounter at Bell Labs brought two trajectories together without merging them.

**John von Neumann** belonged to the same mathematical and computational world; their historical relationship extends beyond the celebrated but uncertain anecdote about entropy.

**John Tukey** suggested the term 'bit', which Shannon used in 1948.

**Richard Hamming** was working at roughly the same time on detecting and correcting errors. The codes named after him gave concrete form to one of the great problems opened by the theory: how to build useful redundancy against errors.

**John McCarthy** encountered Shannon through automata and emerging artificial intelligence: together they edited *Automata Studies*, and Shannon was among the sponsors of the Dartmouth proposal.

### After Shannon — What Changes Scale

**Coding theory:** seeks implementable codes approaching the limits established by the theory.

**Data compression:** uses the statistical structure of sources to reduce the necessary representation, with or without loss according to the problem.

**Digital telecommunications:** make capacity, the signal-to-noise ratio, and coding central design problems.

**Digital storage:** addresses memory and reliability as problems of representation and error correction.

**Modern cryptography:** inherits from Shannon an information-theoretic way of defining security properties, while subsequently developing also through computational complexity.

**Algorithmic information:** Kolmogorov, Solomonoff, and Chaitin develop a different notion connected with description length and the complexity of sequences. Its object is not the average uncertainty of a probabilistic source but the describability of individual sequences or objects.

**Philosophy of information:** examines the status, meaning, and reach of the concept once it leaves its original domain.

### Contemporary Phenomena Readable Through Shannon

Streaming, mobile networks, satellite links, data storage, audio and image compression, error correction, fibre-optic transmission, and wireless systems belong to a technical history profoundly shaped by information theory.

Big data, platforms, and artificial intelligence raise a second level of inquiry: what happens when the term 'information' is extended to embrace data, language, knowledge, and decision?

Shannon does not offer a ready-made theory of the present. He provides distinctions that help us frame the problem more accurately.

## Legacy

Shannon's legacy crosses several genealogies without exhausting any of them.

Information theory establishes a language for measuring sources, representations, channels, noise, and the limits of transmission. Work on circuits connects logical algebra with switching networks. Cryptography gains a probabilistic formulation of secrecy. Coding theory receives a horizon of performance within which to seek concrete constructions.

Compression, digital telecommunications, reliable storage, and many communication technologies directly inherit this framework. Computers, the Internet, and artificial intelligence belong to broader histories in which Shannon remains a fundamental node.

The decisive transformation can be stated precisely: **information becomes an engineering quantity**, with units, limits, and theorems.

## Lucretius, McLuhan, Shannon

The triad **Lucretius — McLuhan — Shannon** does not recount a genealogy of communication. It constructs three autonomous questions about what reaches a subject from the world, passes through a medium, and can be transmitted.

### Lucretius

In *De rerum natura*, the problem concerns matter, bodies, void, and the thin images that detach from surfaces and reach the senses. The vehicle of perception belongs to the same material ontology as the world it describes.

### McLuhan

With McLuhan, attention shifts towards media as environments and extensions of the senses. The medium's form alters scale, pace, perception, and relationships; it is not simply a neutral conduit with respect to the experience it makes possible.

### Shannon

Shannon addresses a different question: how much information can be represented and transmitted through a channel subject to constraints and noise? The channel is characterised by technical properties—transitions, bandwidth, power, capacity—without requiring a theory of meaning or the medium's cultural effects.

Here the distance between the authors becomes productive.

**McLuhan's medium is not Shannon's channel.**

**McLuhan's message is not Shannon's information.**

**Lucretius's simulacrum is not a signal.**

**The atom does not lead to the bit.**

The greatest tension runs between McLuhan and Shannon. McLuhan questions the medium's non-neutrality with respect to the human environment; Shannon abstracts from meaning and cultural effects in order to measure the constraints of transmission. The perspectives do not refute one another: they select different properties and answer different questions.

Lucretius keeps a different level open: he asks what bodies are and how reality can reach the senses.

**Matter, medium, information** are not three phases of the same history. They are three irreducible levels that the present easily conflates: the constitution of reality, the environment of perception, and the formalisation of transmission.

## The Contemporary Wound: Transmission Without Understanding

The world built after Shannon has attained a degree of technical reliability that would have seemed almost inconceivable for much of human history.

Texts, images, speech, and video cross immense distances in minimal time. Codes correct errors; weak signals are reconstructed; archives are duplicated; networks carry ever-increasing quantities of data.

This success makes more visible what the technical problem did not include.

**A communication can succeed technically and fail as understanding.**

A file may arrive intact: that means transmission preserved the data, not that we know the source is reliable. A sentence may be reproduced faithfully and remain misunderstood. A proposition may circulate without being true. An abundance of data may fail to become knowledge.

The contemporary wound is not simply a generic 'excess of information'. It is an **error of transfer**: **we tend to attribute to meaning the achievements we have made in transmission**.

Speed, availability, and technical accuracy are genuine qualities, but they belong to the level at which they were measured. When they become evidence of understanding, truth, or epistemic value without further proof, infrastructure's success is mistaken for knowledge's success.

Weaver distinguished the technical problem from the semantic problem and that of effectiveness. The contemporary reading by *Cerchi d'inchiostro* arises in the gap between these levels: the first may improve enormously without automatically resolving the others.

Shannon did not formulate a critique of digital society. His theory nevertheless provides the boundary necessary to recognise the error: more transmissible information, by itself, does not imply more knowledge.

## Limits, Tensions, and Misuses

### The Semantic Limit Is Deliberate

Shannon's theory was built for the technical problem of communication. Meaning remains outside the formalism because it is unnecessary to the calculation, not because it is unimportant.

### Information and Knowledge Are Different Problems

A statistical quantity does not establish that anyone has learned something true, relevant, or useful.

### Information Entropy and Thermodynamic Entropy Must Be Distinguished

The mathematical and historical kinship is real, but the quantities belong to different theoretical frameworks. Metaphors transferring them between domains need to state the transition.

### Coding Theorems Are Not Implementation Recipes

Knowing that a level of performance is theoretically possible is not the same as immediately possessing a practical algorithm or code. Finite lengths, complexity, delays, energy, and real channel characteristics remain engineering problems.

### Interdisciplinary Extensions Require New Evidence

In 1956, in *The Bandwagon*, Shannon explicitly warned against assuming that information theory's success authorised automatic transfers to psychology, economics, or other sciences. He considered certain applications possible and promising, but insisted they must rest on assumptions and empirical verification, not the mere transplantation of a vocabulary.

This caution reinforces the reading of the 'discipline of boundaries': a concept's power does not remove the need to redefine it when the domain changes.

## Details That Illuminate the Method

### Theseus Is More Than a Toy

The electromechanical mouse embodies an abstract problem: exploring a maze, memorising a solution, and reusing it when the configuration recurs.

### Juggling Becomes an Engineering Problem

Shannon studied and built machines capable of performing juggling movements. His interest lay less in the hobby's oddity than in the possibility of isolating temporal and mechanical regularities within an apparently human gesture.

### Chess Is a Laboratory of Complexity

The problem is making a space of possibilities too vast for exhaustive exploration tractable: selecting, evaluating, and limiting search.

### Creativity Can Be Studied as Transforming a Problem

In the 1952 typescript Shannon returns to simplification, analogy, reversal, and changes of representation. The same strategies emerge in different forms in his technical work.

Biographical curiosities thus become clues to method: Shannon builds in order to understand and changes representations in order to make problems tractable.

## Common Errors

### 'Shannon Invented the Bit'

John Tukey suggested the word *bit*. Shannon used it in 1948 and made it central as a unit of information quantity.

### 'A Bit Is Simply a Zero or a One'

A binary digit is a symbol in a representation; the bit as a unit measures an information quantity. A nonequiprobable binary source produces less than one bit per symbol on average.

### 'Shannon Invented the Digital Computer'

His work on relay circuits is fundamental to the theory of logic circuits, but the computer emerged from a history involving many contributors.

### 'Shannon Invented the Internet'

Information theory supplies fundamental principles for digital communication. The Internet emerged through later architectures, protocols, institutions, and innovations.

### 'Information Means Knowledge'

In Shannon, it is a probabilistic quantity. A sequence may be highly informative without being true or useful.

### 'A More Surprising Message Is More Important'

Surprise is statistical with respect to a probability distribution. It is not a measure of human value.

### 'Entropy Means Chaos'

That is an unreliable simplification. In Shannon, entropy measures the average uncertainty of a probabilistic source.

### 'Noise Means Disinformation'

Shannon's noise is a disturbance in the channel. Disinformation involves content, intention, interpretation, and social context.

### 'Redundancy Is Always Inefficient'

Source redundancy can be exploited for compression; redundancy added to a code can be used to detect and correct errors.

### 'Shannon Explains Artificial Intelligence'

Shannon genuinely participated in the field's early history—from chess and automata to the Dartmouth proposal and *Automata Studies*. Information theory, however, is not a general theory of AI and does not by itself explain contemporary systems.

### 'A Language Model Token Is a Bit'

A token is a unit of segmentation in a language system; a bit is a unit of information quantity. Probabilistic analyses can measure quantities such as bits per token, but tokens and bits remain different objects.

## Glossary

**Boolean algebra** — An algebraic system of logic based on operations such as AND, OR, and NOT. Shannon demonstrated its systematic applicability to switching circuits.

**Bit** — The unit of information quantity when logarithmic base 2 is used; it is not automatically equivalent to a single binary digit.

**Channel** — A medium or system through which a signal is transmitted.

**Channel capacity** — The maximum information rate compatible with reliable transmission under the channel model.

**Code** — A rule associating messages or symbols with representations used for transmission or storage.

**Channel coding** — The addition of structure or redundancy to make data resistant to errors.

**Source coding** — Efficient representation of a source, typically by exploiting its statistical predictability.

**Lossless compression** — Reduction of the necessary representation while preserving the ability to reconstruct the original data exactly.

**Distortion** — A measure of the difference between original and reconstruction accepted by a system that does not require perfect fidelity.

**Entropy** — A measure of the average uncertainty of a probabilistic source.

**Error** — A difference between a transmitted symbol or message and what is reconstructed at reception.

**Information** — In Shannon's sense, a quantity defined with reference to the probabilities of possible selections.

**Mutual information** — A measure of informational dependence between two variables; it indicates how far knowledge of one reduces uncertainty about the other.

**Message** — An element or sequence selected by the source.

**Probability** — The mathematical structure used to describe uncertainty surrounding selections.

**Source redundancy** — Statistical predictability that can be exploited to compress a representation.

**Code redundancy** — Structure deliberately added to detect or correct errors.

**Noise** — A channel disturbance changing the relationship between transmitted and received signals.

**Signal** — The physical or mathematical form used to represent a message in the channel.

**Perfect secrecy** — A condition in which observing the cryptogram does not change the probabilities assigned to the original message.

**Source** — A process generating messages according to certain probabilities.

**Transmitter** — A component translating the message into a signal suitable for the channel.

## Points to Remember

- Shannon turns information into a mathematical quantity connected to probability and choice.
- His theory is concerned primarily with the technical problem of communication.
- Meaning is methodologically excluded, not denied.
- The bit is a unit of information measurement, not a metaphysical atom.
- Entropy measures a source's average uncertainty.
- Capacity establishes a fundamental limit of the channel.
- Below capacity, suitable codes can make error arbitrarily small.
- Lossless compression, compression with controlled distortion, and error protection address different problems.
- Compression tends to exploit and remove redundancy; error correction may add it.
- Shannon and Weaver must be distinguished.
- Shannon and Wiener share certain problems, but information theory and cybernetics are not identical.
- Shannon's contribution to the digital world is fundamental without amounting to the invention of the Internet, computers, or AI.
- His strongest contemporary lesson concerns the boundary between transmissible information and understanding.

## FAQ

### Who Was Claude Shannon?

Claude Shannon was an American mathematician and engineer, born in 1916 and deceased in 2001. He founded mathematical information theory and made fundamental contributions to logic circuit theory, digital communication, and cryptography.

### What Is Shannon's Information Theory?

It is a mathematical theory studying how information can be quantified, represented, and transmitted through channels subject to constraints and noise. It introduces concepts including entropy, channel capacity, coding, and redundancy.

### What Does 'Information' Mean for Shannon?

It denotes a quantity related to the probabilities of the possible selections produced by a source. It is not the same as a message's meaning, its truth, or the knowledge it may produce.

### Did Shannon Invent the Bit?

Shannon made the bit central as a unit of information quantity by using base-2 logarithms. The word *bit*, short for *binary digit*, was suggested by John Tukey.

### What Is Shannon Entropy?

It is a measure of the average uncertainty of a probabilistic source. For a fixed number of alternatives, it reaches a maximum when outcomes are equally probable and decreases as the source becomes more predictable.

### Is Shannon Entropy the Same as Thermodynamic Entropy?

The two formulations have a profound mathematical kinship and intertwined conceptual histories, but they belong to different theories and should not be identified without qualification.

### What Is Channel Capacity?

It is the maximum information rate at which, under the channel model, transmission with an arbitrarily small probability of error is possible using suitable codes.

### Why Does Shannon Exclude Meaning?

Because his aim is to formalise the technical problem of transmission. Meaning is placed outside the model because it is unnecessary for calculating quantities such as entropy, capacity, and error; that does not mean denying its importance in human communication.

### What Is the Difference Between Shannon and Weaver?

Shannon formulates a mathematical theory of communication's technical problem. In his introduction to the 1949 volume, Weaver also distinguishes semantic and effectiveness levels and helps broaden the work's interdisciplinary reception. The common label 'Shannon–Weaver model' can therefore conceal two different contributions.

### Can a False Message Carry a Great Deal of Information According to Shannon?

Yes. Information quantity depends on the probability of the selection, not its semantic truth. A false and improbable message may therefore have high self-information in the technical sense.

### What Is the Relationship Between Shannon and the Internet?

Information theory supplies fundamental principles for communication, coding, compression, and noise management, all of them decisive for digital networks. Shannon did not invent the Internet, however, and his theory alone does not describe its architecture.

### What Is the Relationship Between Shannon and Artificial Intelligence?

Shannon worked on chess, automata, and machines and was among the signatories of the 1955 Dartmouth proposal; in 1956 he edited *Automata Studies* with John McCarthy. He therefore participated in the field's historical formation, but contemporary AI arose through later developments and cannot be reduced to information theory.

### Is Claude AI Named After Claude Shannon?

According to a 2026 reconstruction in *The New Yorker* based on Anthropic's internal company culture, the name **Claude** is partly a tribute to Claude Shannon and partly the choice of a name perceived as friendly. There appears to be no institutional statement by Anthropic presenting Shannon as the name's sole origin.

### What Is the Difference Between Shannon and McLuhan?

Shannon studies the quantitative conditions of transmission through a channel. McLuhan studies how media transform perception, environments, and relationships. Shannon's *channel* and McLuhan's *medium* belong to different problems.

### Why Is Shannon Still Important Today?

Because the digital world continues to depend on coding, capacity, compression, and error correction; and because distinguishing information, meaning, knowledge, and truth has become even more urgent in a society saturated with data and messages.

## Closing

Claude Shannon gave the word 'information' a precision that the term's very success continually threatens to stretch.

His theory shows that a formalisation can be extraordinarily general without claiming to exhaust the phenomenon. In 1956 Shannon himself warned his field against assuming that the vocabulary of information could be transferred elsewhere without new assumptions and new evidence.

Today we possess higher-capacity channels, larger archives, and machines capable of producing and transforming language on a scale Shannon never knew. The distinction between what a system can transmit and what a person can understand has become, if anything, more visible.

A question remains, belonging to us rather than to the theory of 1948:

**When everything can be transmitted, what still enables us to distinguish what has arrived from what we have truly understood?**
